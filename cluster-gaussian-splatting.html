The principles underneath this cluster

Gaussian splatting stores a 3D scene as many soft blobs that can be projected and blended quickly. The deeper principle is a trade: replace a slow continuous volume with a finite set of differentiable scene elements, then train those elements by how well they render the observed photos.

A blob is a local scene hypothesis

Everyday evidence. Each Gaussian says: there is some colored matter around this 3D location, with this size, direction, opacity, and color.

Fundamental math principle. The principle is local basis representation. A complex scene is approximated by adding many simple smooth functions. Each blob covers a small part of space, and many blobs together form surfaces.

How it shows up in the papers. Static splatting, compact splatting, avatar splatting, object splatting, and scene editing papers all change what each blob stores and how blobs are placed.

Fast rendering comes from ordered projection

Everyday evidence. To draw a view, blobs are projected onto the image and blended by depth and opacity. The result must approximate how light from surfaces reaches the camera.

Fundamental math principle. The principle is differentiable alpha compositing. The rendered pixel is a weighted sum of visible blob colors, where opacity and ordering decide how much each blob contributes.

How it shows up in the papers. Real-time rendering, antialiasing, transparency, depth sorting, and quality-improvement papers all tune this projection-and-blend step.

Geometry is learned through image error

Everyday evidence. The training data is often only photos. If a blob is in the wrong place, the rendered image will be wrong from one or more views.

Fundamental math principle. The principle is inverse rendering by gradient descent. Rendering error supplies gradients that move blob position, shape, opacity, and color toward a scene that explains all views.

How it shows up in the papers. Optimization, feed-forward splatting, iterative refinement, sparse-view splatting, and pose-free splatting papers all differ in how they initialize and correct the blobs.

The problem — from first principles

Photography is a lossy process. Each photograph throws away depth—the camera collapses a 3D scene onto a 2D image sensor, and that distance information is lost. If you want to store a scene so you can later recreate it from any angle (not just the few angles your camera captured), you need a 3D representation: something compact enough to fit in memory, flexible enough to learn from partial views, and quick enough to render in real time.

The classical approaches stumble. Meshes are precise—you can export them to game engines and CAD tools—but they're slow to fit from images; finding the right triangle topology is a discrete, non-differentiable problem. Neural fields (continuous implicit functions like NeRF) can be optimized end-to-end from images, but rendering them is expensive: each pixel shoots a ray through the scene and samples the function dozens of times. For real-time rendering—think AR, robotics, autonomous driving—you need something faster.

Gaussian Splatting's core idea is deceptively simple: represent the scene not as a mesh or a volumetric grid, but as a cloud of thousands of small, soft, colored 3D blobs. Each blob is a Gaussian (a smooth bell curve in 3D space); it has a position, a size (covariance), a color, and an opacity. To render the scene from a new viewpoint, project each Gaussian onto the image plane, and blend them in order from back to front. Because projection and blending are both differentiable and highly parallel, you can train the Gaussians by comparing rendered images to ground-truth photos, and the optimization converges in minutes instead of hours. The rendering itself runs in real time on modern GPUs.

The fundamental hardness remains: building a good 3D model from limited views, handling occlusions, capturing fine detail, dealing with changing light and motion. But Gaussian Splatting shifts the problem into a form the GPU can solve fast. That shift unlocked a whole research direction.

Where it stands

What basically works now: You can reconstruct high-fidelity 3D scenes from 10–50 casual photographs in minutes, render them in real time, and edit them interactively. You can capture human heads and bodies as animatable avatars. You can reconstruct dynamic scenes from video. You can fit Gaussians from diverse sensors (event cameras, thermal, medical imaging). You can embed semantic labels, language, and interactive reasoning. For many applications—AR, VR, robotics, autonomous driving—Gaussian Splatting is already the default choice.

What is still open: Truly few-shot and single-image reconstruction remains challenging; generalization to new scenes from a trained model is improving but not solved. Mesh extraction is fast but not always water-tight or simulation-ready. Fitting Gaussians to very large scenes (city-scale) still requires careful engineering and hierarchical approaches. Handling extremely complex materials (caustics, sub-surface scattering) remains difficult. Long-horizon video (hours of capture) and continuous online reconstruction hit memory limits. Finally, the theory is still incomplete—we don't fully understand why Gaussians are so effective at this task, or how to predict a priori what density and configuration of primitives a scene needs.

Dynamic scenes and video

The everyday problem: Ordinary splats describe a scene as if it freezes in place, but videos contain motion, blur, deformation, and long sequences. This family asks how each blob should move, appear, disappear, or persist through time.

The hidden mathematical idea: The hidden principle is temporal correspondence. A Gaussian at one moment should be traceable to related evidence in nearby moments, with motion fields, lifespans, pruning, optical flow, or anchor relays controlling which parts stay fixed and which parts deform.

Why it matters: Without motion structure, a 3D video flickers or melts. These papers make splatting useful for streamable scenes, moving people, dynamic driving worlds, and long clips where the viewer expects the same object to remain itself from frame to frame.

What the field still can't do — and why

The honest frontier of computer vision in 2026: the hard problems that cut across every theme, the root cause of each, and how researchers are chipping at them.

← All themesThe enginesOpen problemsThe through-line

Progress is easiest to see; limits are more useful to understand. These are the problems the field still cannot solve reliably — not because no one is trying, but because each has a root cause that resists the usual fixes. For each: why it is fundamentally hard, and how 2026 is attacking it anyway.

01  Trusting what a model tells you about what it saw

Why it's hard. A vision model's output is generated by the same machinery whether it is looking or bluffing, and the machinery was rewarded only for being right on average, never for being honest about a single case. Language priors are usually a better bet for the next word than the actual pixels, so a model that describes a scene it never checked and one that carefully read it produce identically fluent, identically confident text. There is no internal signal that separates grounded evidence from a plausible guess, because the training objective never had to create one.

How they're chipping at it. The perceive and vlm themes attack this from three angles at once: inference-time surgery that finds and re-weights attention heads leaning on language over pixels, contrastive decoding that subtracts the model's image-blind guess, and rewards that force answers to change when the image changes so visual sensitivity becomes explicit. The learning and perceive themes add calibration, out-of-distribution scoring, and abstention. None closes the gap because all of these estimate confidence from the same representation that is already fooled; a model steered to attend to pixels can still be steered to attend to the wrong pixels, and calibration tuned on one distribution decays the moment the input shifts.

02  Recovering 3D and metric scale from too few views

Why it's hard. A flat image discards depth by construction: a small near object and a large far one land on identical pixels, and true distance in meters is simply not present in one photograph without knowing the exact lens. Every scene consistent with the observed views is a valid answer, so the problem is not under-solved but under-determined. Filling the missing dimension means inventing geometry for surfaces no camera ever saw, and there is no way to verify the invention against the data because the data is what was missing.

How they're chipping at it. The threed theme leans hard on generative priors: feed-forward point-map transformers that predict a 3D coordinate per pixel across all views at once, Gaussian-splatting pipelines that hallucinate unseen surfaces from depth guidance, and monocular depth models repurposed from diffusion backbones, with sparse LiDAR or time-of-flight hints bolted on to pin down metric scale. It is not closed because the prior is doing the work the data cannot: it produces confident geometry that is plausible rather than correct, degrades on textureless or repeated structure where matching is ambiguous, and inherits whatever bias its training scenes carried, so out-of-distribution objects still come out warped.

03  Generalizing past the exact conditions you trained on

Why it's hard. A model learns the statistics of its training set, and nothing forces it to distinguish the cause of a label from an accidental correlate that happens to predict it. Fog, a new scanner, night, a different demographic, or the next generation of fake all break that hidden correlation while a human sees the same scene. No finite training set can enumerate the future, so a fixed model is permanently somewhere outside its comfort zone, and it has no way to know when it has crossed the line because the spurious cue it relied on still fires.

How they're chipping at it. This shows up in nearly every theme: domain generalization that tries to separate style from content and inject causal invariances, test-time adaptation that updates a live model from the unlabeled stream it is seeing, and deepfake detectors chasing fingerprints that survive to unseen generators. The efforts collide with a wall: adapting to today's distribution tends to erase yesterday's (continual-shift methods add whole subsystems just to prevent collapse), self-supervised test-time updates can drift into degenerate solutions, and detectors trained on the last generator's traces predictably fold on the next one because it is trained specifically not to leave them.

04  Measuring whether a system actually works

Why it's hard. An averaged accuracy score is a single number summarizing wildly uneven behavior, and it can be earned by exploiting the very shortcuts the benchmark meant to test, so a model can look excellent while being blind to a specific ability or a rare, high-stakes case. Worse, once a benchmark is public it leaks into training data, so high scores may reflect memorization rather than competence, and for generation there is often no ground truth at all, since 'good' lives in human judgment that itself resists definition.

How they're chipping at it. The video, vlm, and generation themes are pouring effort into disentangled benchmarks that isolate one atomic skill at a time (counting, temporal grounding, physical plausibility, hallucination), contamination-resistant and parametrically-generated testbeds, and vision-language models recruited as automatic judges and reward models with chain-of-thought explanations. It stays open because the judge is itself an unreliable vision-language model with the same grounding failures it is meant to catch, parametric benchmarks test only what their generator can express, and each new isolating benchmark becomes the next thing to overfit once it is published.

05  The compute wall: getting the same result for far less work

Why it's hard. The dominant tools are intrinsically expensive by design. Attention cost grows with the square of sequence length, and an image is thousands of tokens, a video dozens of frames per second on top of that; a diffusion image is many full network passes turning noise into structure. Quality has historically come precisely from doing more, so cutting compute means betting that some of the work is redundant, but importance is not fixed, it depends on the layer, the query, and the moment during decoding, so the cheap-to-identify redundancy and the load-bearing computation are tangled together.

How they're chipping at it. Every theme has an efficiency wing: distilling many-step diffusion into one or few steps, pruning and merging visual tokens, replacing quadratic attention with linear state-space (Mamba) blocks, key-value cache compression, quantization to low-bit arithmetic, and block-sparse attention for many-view 3D. Progress is real but bounded: aggressive token pruning can underperform random deletion because saliency is misjudged, one-step distillation loses diversity and fidelity, low-bit quantization breaks on activation outliers, and linear-attention backbones trade away some of the exact long-range modeling that made the expensive version good in the first place.

06  Staying consistent over long horizons in time and space

Why it's hard. Coherence is a global constraint but generation and tracking are local operations, so nothing structurally forbids an object from flickering, morphing, or teleporting between frames, or a reasoning chain from drifting off the image step by step. Small per-step errors have no reason to cancel; they accumulate and compound, and the longer the sequence the more certainly they diverge. Enforcing that frame ten agrees with frame one, or that a scene reconstructed from views taken minutes apart is one consistent world, requires holding the whole horizon in view while the model only ever sees a window of it.

How they're chipping at it. Responses span video generation (position-embedding tricks and reference images to preserve identity, autoregressive and streaming schemes for minute-long clips), 4D reconstruction that untangles static background from moving foreground with a time axis, point tracking that forecasts through occlusion, embodied agents whose single early mistake derails a long task, and vlm reasoning that ties each step to a region to stop it drifting. None fully closes it because error accumulation is a property of sequential prediction itself: memory mechanisms bound drift but do not eliminate it, and physical or temporal plausibility is rewarded after the fact rather than guaranteed, so failures still surface at longer horizons than the training window.

07  Turning perception into reliable physical action

Why it's hard. Vision and language are discrete and abstract; the physical world is continuous, contact-rich, and unforgiving of millimeter errors, and a model trained on internet images has no built-in sense of force, friction, or its own body. The trials needed to learn control can only be afforded in simulation, but a policy that grasps or drives flawlessly in a game engine collapses on real hardware because pixels, timing, and contact forces do not match. Compounding this, the situations that matter most, a child darting into the road, an occluded object, are exactly the ones almost absent from any dataset.

How they're chipping at it. The embodied and vlm themes stack partial fixes: aggressive domain randomization and privileged-teacher-to-pixel-student training to survive the sim-to-real gap, tactile and force feedback to correct what vision misses, vision-language-action models with 3D affordance fields and visual-foresight planning, world models to let an agent practice in imagination, and long-tail curation plus uncertainty scores for driving. The gap persists because randomization buys robustness at the cost of peak performance, world models must be both accurate enough to trust and cheap enough to plan with (a photorealistic predictor is too slow, a crude one misleads), and no amount of curation manufactures the genuinely rare events, so tail failures remain the dominant safety risk.

08  Controlling and correcting what a model has already learned

Why it's hard. Knowledge in a network is distributed and entangled across shared weights with no index, so there is no clean handle for 'this person,' 'this concept,' or 'this style' to grab and remove, and gradient descent had no reason to keep them separable. Erasing one thing damages its neighbors and tends to leave leakable traces, while the same entanglement lets an attacker plant a backdoor or a spurious cue that fires only on a chosen trigger while everything else looks normal. Law and safety now demand surgical edits, but the substrate was never built to be edited surgically.

How they're chipping at it. The emerging and learning themes pursue machine unlearning that aims to be verifiable and even federated, concept erasure that scrubs thousands of styles or objects from generators while preserving the rest, backdoor detection and weight editing, immunization against harmful fine-tuning, and closed-form debiasing with utility guarantees. It remains open because verifying that something is truly gone rather than merely hidden is nearly as hard as the removal itself, edits that look local still shift behavior on untested neighbors, and every defense that maps the entanglement also hands an attacker the same map, keeping it an arms race rather than a solved problem.

3D from an image — why it works, mathematically

Look at one photo of a street: how far away is that parked car? A single picture genuinely cannot tell you — a toy car up close and a real car far off can make the very same image. This is what one photo can and cannot recover, and how a second viewpoint brings the missing distances back.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

Depth asks how far things are from the camera. A flat image has thrown that distance away, so the model has to recover the missing world from clues such as size, perspective, shadows, focus, and motion.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology asks what is connected, what surrounds what, and where holes or boundaries are. Depth helps answer those questions in the physical world. A bridge, blood vessel, room, road, or folded surface cannot be understood from color alone; distance tells which parts meet and which only overlap in the picture.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

Depth matters in robotics, driving, augmented reality, mapping, medicine, inspection, and film. Any system that must move through the world or measure it needs more than a flat label.

Why 3D from an image is worth learning

It matters because action needs space. A model that knows what an object is but not where it sits cannot safely grasp it, avoid it, or rebuild it.

The problem, as a quantity

A photograph is flat. Recovering the 3D it came from — how far away everything is — is the goal, but part of that goal is provably impossible from a single image, and knowing exactly which part is the whole game.

When a camera makes a picture it projects the 3D world onto a flat plane, dividing everything by how far away it is — which is why distant things look smaller. That division quietly destroys information: a small object nearby and a large object far away can land in exactly the same place on the image. So the problem splits in two. From one photo you can hope to recover the shape of a scene — what is nearer than what — but not its true size, because size was thrown away. To pin down real distances in meters you need something more: a second viewpoint, or a moving camera that supplies one for free.

The core ideas — and why they work

One photo cannot tell you how far — a real, unavoidable ambiguity

x = K·X/Z = K·(λX)/(λZ) for all λ > 0 ⇒ scale is unobservable
The camera turns 3D into a flat picture by dividing each point's position by how far away it is — the reason distant things shrink. But that division hides a genuine ambiguity: scale everything in the scene up and push it proportionally farther away, and every point lands in exactly the same spot on the image. The picture is pixel-for-pixel identical.

So from one photo alone, the overall size of the scene — and therefore the true distance to anything — is fundamentally unrecoverable. A doll's house photographed up close can be identical to a real house across the street. This is not a shortcoming of any particular method that a cleverer one could fix; it is baked into what a single projection discards. Everything else on this page is about what you add to break this ambiguity.

A second eye restores the true distances

Z = f·B / d, d = u_L − u_R, d > 0 iff point is in front
The remedy is a second viewpoint. Look at the same scene from two eyes a known distance apart, and each point appears in a slightly different place in the two views — near things shift a lot between the eyes, far things barely shift at all.

That shift is directly measurable, and it pins the distance exactly: the distance to a point equals the camera's focal length times the spacing between the eyes, divided by the shift. Big shift, close; tiny shift, far. Because the spacing between the eyes is a known, fixed number, the one-photo ambiguity from the previous idea is gone — the second view supplies the missing scale, turning 'some unknown multiple of the truth' into true distances you can state in meters. This is why we have two eyes, and why depth cameras use two lenses.

One photo plus learned experience gives shape, but not true scale

D̂ = f_θ(I), D̂ ≈ s·D_true for unknown scalar s > 0 (scale-invariant, not metric)
You can still get a great deal from a single photo if you have seen millions of them. A network trained on huge numbers of images learns the regularities of the world — skies are far, floors recede toward the horizon, faces come in usual sizes — and leans on them to guess a sensible distance for every pixel.

But it can only recover the shape of the scene, never its true size. Because of the one-photo ambiguity from the first idea, its guess is correct only up to a single unknown overall multiplier: it can say confidently 'this is twice as far as that,' but not whether the whole scene is a dollhouse or a mansion. The learned experience fills in relative depth beautifully; the absolute scale simply is not present in a single picture for any amount of experience to find.

A moving camera is its own second eye, for free

L = Σ_{p} ||I_t(p) − I_{t+1}(π(D̂_t, T̂, p))||₁, T̂ from PoseNet, D̂ from DepthNet
Here is how to train a depth-guesser when nobody ever measured a real distance. A single moving camera hands you two views for free: this frame and the next.

The training signal is pure geometry. Suppose the network guesses the depth of every pixel and also guesses how the camera moved between the two frames. Then you can take this frame, push each pixel to where those guesses say it should appear in the next frame, and check: does it land on the real next frame? If the depths and the motion are right, it lands exactly; if they are wrong, it misses. So you make the mismatch between the predicted next frame and the actual next frame the only thing to minimise. Geometry itself supplies the answer key, and raw video — with no human ever labelling a distance — teaches the network depth.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Work here turns a few dials on the same facts.

How many views. One photo (learned shape, no true scale), two views a known distance apart (exact metric distance from the shift), or many views combined into one consistent reconstruction. Where the second view comes from. A second lens, or the same camera a moment later as it moves — motion turns a video into a stream of free view-pairs. What supplies the missing scale. A known camera spacing, a known object size, or a depth sensor, since a single image can never supply it itself. How it is trained. On measured distances where you have them, or with no labels at all by reprojecting one frame into another and penalising the mismatch. The thread never changes: one projection loses scale, and only a second view — or a known size — can bring it back.

A worked numeric example

For stereo cameras with focal length f=800, baseline B=0.1m, and disparity d=40 pixels, depth is Z=fB/d=2m. Halve disparity to 20, and depth doubles to 4m. Small disparity errors therefore explode for distant objects.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

1One image, instant depth
d = f(I; θ) where θ encodes shape prior from training data. Loss compares relative scale, not absolute Z.
A single camera perspective projection squashes 3-D onto 2-D, losing depth — ten meters and ten centimeters look identical. Neural networks sidestep this by learning which shapes are common in the world (flat floors, tall people) from massive training sets of photos and depth measurements. The network outputs relative depth (which is closer), and diffusion models or foundation models anchor it with learned distributions of plausible scenes rather than equations.

2Two eyes are better than one
Z = fB/d where d = x_L - x_R is disparity (pixel offset), f is focal length, B is baseline (distance between cameras).
Stereo triangulation is simple geometry: two viewpoints create a triangle with the 3-D point at the apex. The disparity — the horizontal offset (in pixels) of an object between left and right images — is inversely proportional to depth. Matching pixels left-to-right (scoring how similar each pixel-pair looks) finds that disparity, and the formula inverts it to recover absolute distance in one step, solving the monocular ambiguity.

3Images in, geometry out
P = Decoder(Encoder(I₁, I₂, ..., Iₙ)) where P is a pointmap or voxel grid and Encoder fuses multi-view features via homography or learned projection.
Instead of solving for camera positions and then reconstructing (structure-from-motion, then dense matching), feed-forward models (neural networks that process input in one direction, without loops) learn to bypass geometry entirely. They take raw images and output 3-D predictions directly. The implicit learned projection is faster than hand-crafted rules, and specialized sub-networks focus on different scene types (indoor vs. outdoor, close vs. distant).

Predicts sparse per-pixel 3-D points in a feed-forward pass, then feeds them into a compact voxel grid backend that re-scores and refines coordinates.
Reliev3R: Relieving Feed-forward 3D Reconstruction from Multi-View Geometric Supervisionread it →
Trains feed-forward reconstruction using only weak 2-D reprojection loss (comparing pixel positions after re-projecting 3-D points back onto the images) instead of expensive multi-view photometric or geometric ground-truth labels (pixel-value matching or point coordinate ground-truth).
4The cameras, and the whole solid
(R, t, X, o) = JointSolver(I₁, ..., Iₙ) where optimization alternates: refine poses (R,t), refine structure (X), refine occupancy map (o).
Single-image reconstruction or multi-view fusion alone is brittle. Hybrid pipelines use structure-from-motion to anchor camera poses globally, then feed-forward models to fill in detail, then bundle adjustment (joint optimization that adjusts camera positions and 3-D point coordinates to minimize reprojection error, enforcing that each camera has one position, not two) to lock everything together. The camera network must be rigid, and that rigidity can be expressed as mathematical constraints on pose updates.

5Putting the ruler back
Z_metric = Z_relative · s where s is computed from sparse anchors: s = Σᵢ(Ẑ_i / Ẑ_rel,i) / N, averaging over i true reference points.
Monocular depth networks output relative depth (which is closer), not metric depth (actual meters). To convert to real units, the network needs one or more ground-truth anchor points from a depth-sensor, radar, edges that imply known object sizes, or language describing scale. A single anchor determines global scale; multiple anchors allow per-region scale correction and detect errors in the relative depth output.

6Metric, real-time, onboard
ε_pose = Cov(Δp) estimated in real-time; Δp = Z_pred ± σ_depth. Gaussian belief propagates uncertainty through pose updates and path planning.
Robots need both depth and confidence bounds to navigate safely. Modern methods predict not just depth but per-pixel variance (a measure of how uncertain each depth is). This builds a Gaussian map where each voxel (a 3-D pixel, a unit of volumetric space) stores both the average depth and its spread. Kinematics (the math describing how a robot's joint angles determine where its parts are located) act as a second ruler — robot geometry anchors scale independent of vision. Uncertainty flows directly into safety filters (control barrier functions — math that checks 'is the next motion safe before we move?') that modulate acceleration to avoid collision even when depth is noisy.

The through-line. The core fact is that a single photo genuinely cannot recover true distance: projection throws scale away, so a dollhouse and a mansion can produce identical pictures. A second viewpoint restores exact distances through the shift between the two views; a single photo plus learned experience recovers shape but never absolute scale; and a moving camera acts as its own second viewpoint, letting raw video teach depth with no labels at all. Two plain ideas carry it: one projection loses scale and only a second view can bring it back, and geometry itself — reproject one view into another and compare — hands you a free answer key when nobody measures a distance for you.

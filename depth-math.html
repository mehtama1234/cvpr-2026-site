3D from an image — why it works, mathematically

A camera smashes an infinite ray of points onto one pixel. One eye cannot undo that smash — but two eyes can, and a network learns the shape rules the world already obeys.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

Depth asks how far things are from the camera. A flat image has thrown that distance away, so the model has to recover the missing world from clues such as size, perspective, shadows, focus, and motion.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology asks what is connected, what surrounds what, and where holes or boundaries are. Depth helps answer those questions in the physical world. A bridge, blood vessel, room, road, or folded surface cannot be understood from color alone; distance tells which parts meet and which only overlap in the picture.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

Depth matters in robotics, driving, augmented reality, mapping, medicine, inspection, and film. Any system that must move through the world or measure it needs more than a flat label.

Why 3D from an image is worth learning

It matters because action needs space. A model that knows what an object is but not where it sits cannot safely grasp it, avoid it, or rebuild it.

The problem, as a quantity

A camera takes a 3-D point in the world and squashes it down onto a 2-D flat image using a simple rule: pixel-position = (lens-sharpness × world-coordinate) ÷ depth. That division by depth throws away the actual distance. If you scale the whole scene — move everything twice as far back AND make everything twice as large — the pixel position does not change: both the numerator and denominator scale by the same amount. This is the core trap: one image alone cannot tell you the real distance. A single equation gives you two pieces of information (the x and y pixel position), but the 3-D point has three unknowns (x, y, and z). The system is one clue short. No amount of cleverness can fix this with just one photo. The entire theme is about finding one extra clue to break this tie.

The core ideas — and why they work

One image, infinite solutions (the ray ambiguity)

x = K·X/Z = K·(λX)/(λZ) for all λ &gt; 0 ⇒ scale is unobservable
When you point a camera at a 3-D point, the light travels along a ray from that point through the lens onto the sensor. Perspective projection divides by depth: pixel-position = (lens-sharpness × position) ÷ distance. If you move everything twice as far away and make it twice as large, both the numerator and denominator scale by 2, so the pixel position stays the same. Scale is a "null direction" — a change that produces zero image signal. Depth recovery is not 'solve for depth given the pixel' — it is 'solve for depth given the pixel, knowing you have one fewer equation than unknown.' The solution space is infinite: every point along the ray from the camera to the pixel looks identical. You must inject information from somewhere else — another camera, time, or learned rules about what shapes exist in the world.

Two eyes pin the depth (stereo triangulation)

Z = f·B / d, d = u_L − u_R, d &gt; 0 iff point is in front
A second camera, offset from the first by a known distance (the baseline), gives a second ray through the same 3-D point. Two rays from two known starting points intersect at exactly one location — this is triangulation, and it pins the depth. For two cameras aligned and facing the same direction (rectified stereo), the math simplifies: a point at distance Z shifts by d pixels horizontally between the left and right images, where d = (lens-sharpness × baseline) ÷ distance. Rearranging: depth = (lens-sharpness × baseline) ÷ (pixel-shift). The pixel-shift is called disparity — it is inversely proportional to depth. Close objects jump far across the images; distant objects barely move — exactly what you experience when you close one eye and then the other. Disparity is the only signal that breaks the scale-ambiguity, because the two cameras see different images.

One image, learned shape prior (monocular networks)

D̂ = f_θ(I), D̂ ≈ s·D_true for unknown scalar s &gt; 0 (scale-invariant, not metric)
Real scenes follow rules: walls are flat, people stand on floors, faces curve outward. A neural network (a mathematical function with millions of adjustable knobs) trained on millions of (photo, depth-map) pairs learns to recognize these patterns. It does not learn the physics of projection — those are unchanged — but rather which 3-D shapes are common in the training data. The network infers relative depth: which regions are closer than others, the shape of the depth landscape. It does not recover absolute distance in meters; to get that, you need one anchor point — a known object size, a depth-sensor measurement, or a prior about height above ground. This is not a failure — it is the scale-ambiguity showing up again. The network learns the best shape estimate that matches the image, and that estimate is scale-free by law.

Self-supervised depth from motion (ego-motion as a free second view)

L = Σ_{p} ||I_t(p) − I_{t+1}(π(D̂_t, T̂, p))||₁, T̂ from PoseNet, D̂ from DepthNet
Video gives you a free stereo rig: the camera moves between frames, creating a baseline you did not need to build. Self-supervised depth trains two networks together: one predicts depth for each frame, the other predicts how the camera moved (its transformation — how it rotated and shifted). Given both, you can warp (reproject) frame-t into frame-(t+1) and compare the warped pixels to the real pixels. The pixel-difference is the only supervision signal — no depth labels, no depth-sensor. The system works when both networks agree: depths and motions that jointly explain the pixel motion. But a trap waits: a network could cheat by predicting infinite depth (all motion comes from the object moving, none from the camera) or zero motion (nothing moved at all), and either way the loss becomes zero. Real systems block this by masking pixels that stay still between frames and by picking the best-matching frame in a window. This is the same trap that haunts other self-supervised methods: you must add a constraint that makes the cheat path costly.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Every method is a different way to inject the missing clue that breaks the scale-ambiguity. Stereo triangulation injects it geometrically: point the two cameras at the same 3-D point, measure the pixel offset, and math gives you absolute distance. Structure-from-motion generalizes this: use a single moving camera across many frames, solve jointly for where the camera was and where the 3-D points are, and the camera motion anchors the scale. Supervised monocular networks inject it statistically: train on thousands of (photo, depth-sensor-reading) pairs so the network learns shape priors, but it still outputs relative depth without an anchor. Self-supervised monocular networks inject it through pixel consistency: train two networks to predict depth and camera motion that jointly explain frame-to-frame pixel changes, with no depth labels, but the system is fragile when objects move. Diffusion models and foundation models treat depth as a learned distribution over plausible depth maps — like asking "what are all the valid 3-D scenes that could produce this image?" and sampling one. The choice of method is the choice of which constraint to trust: hard geometry (reliable but needs careful setup), learned statistics (flexible but needs training data), or both mixed together.

A worked numeric example

For stereo cameras with focal length f=800, baseline B=0.1m, and disparity d=40 pixels, depth is Z=fB/d=2m. Halve disparity to 20, and depth doubles to 4m. Small disparity errors therefore explode for distant objects.

1One image, instant depth
d = f(I; θ) where θ encodes shape prior from training data. Loss compares relative scale, not absolute Z.
A single camera projection squashes 3-D onto 2-D, losing depth — ten meters and ten centimeters look identical. Neural networks sidestep this by learning which shapes are common in the world (flat floors, tall people) from massive training sets of photos and depth measurements. The network outputs relative depth (which is closer), and diffusion models or foundation models anchor it with learned distributions of plausible scenes rather than equations.

2Two eyes are better than one
Z = fB/d where d = x_L - x_R is disparity (pixel offset), f is focal length, B is baseline (distance between cameras).
Stereo triangulation is simple geometry: two viewpoints create a triangle with the 3-D point at the apex. The disparity — how many pixels the point shifts between left and right images — is inversely proportional to depth. Matching pixels left-to-right (scoring how similar each pixel-pair looks) finds that disparity, and the formula inverts it to recover absolute distance in one step, solving the monocular ambiguity.

3Images in, geometry out
P = Decoder(Encoder(I₁, I₂, ..., Iₙ)) where P is a pointmap or voxel grid and Encoder fuses multi-view features via homography or learned projection.
Instead of solving for camera positions and then reconstructing (structure-from-motion, then dense matching), feed-forward models learn to bypass geometry entirely. They take raw images and output 3-D predictions directly. The implicit learned projection is faster than hand-crafted rules, and specialized sub-networks focus on different scene types (indoor vs. outdoor, close vs. distant).

4The cameras, and the whole solid
(R, t, X, o) = JointSolver(I₁, ..., Iₙ) where optimization alternates: refine poses (R,t), refine structure (X), refine occupancy map (o).
Single-image reconstruction or multi-view fusion alone is brittle. Hybrid pipelines use structure-from-motion to anchor camera poses globally, then feed-forward models to fill in detail, then bundle adjustment (rigidity constraints — enforcing that each camera has one position, not two) to lock everything together. The camera network must be rigid (no two cameras in two places at once), and that rigidity can be expressed as mathematical constraints on pose updates.

5Putting the ruler back
Z_metric = Z_relative · s where s is computed from sparse anchors: s = Σᵢ(Ẑ_i / Ẑ_rel,i) / N, averaging over i true reference points.
Monocular depth networks output relative depth (which is closer), not metric depth (actual meters). To convert to real units, the network needs one or more ground-truth anchor points from a depth-sensor, radar, edges that imply known object sizes, or language describing scale. A single anchor determines global scale; multiple anchors allow per-region scale correction and detect errors in the relative depth output.

6Metric, real-time, onboard
ε_pose = Cov(Δp) estimated in real-time; Δp = Z_pred ± σ_depth. Gaussian belief propagates uncertainty through pose updates and path planning.
Robots need both depth and confidence bounds to navigate safely. Modern methods predict not just depth but per-pixel variance (a measure of how uncertain each depth is). This builds a Gaussian map where each voxel (a 3-D pixel) stores both the average depth and its spread. Kinematics (the robot's joint angles and motors) act as a second ruler — robot geometry anchors scale independent of vision. Uncertainty flows directly into safety filters (control barrier functions — math that checks 'is the next motion safe before we move?') that modulate acceleration to avoid collision even when depth is noisy.

The through-line. The whole theme rests on one algebraic fact: perspective projection divides by depth, so scale vanishes into a null direction that no image signal can excite. Every method is a different route around that null direction — a second eye (stereo), a second moment in time (ego-motion video), a learned shape prior (monocular network), or a direct range sensor (depth-sensor). The two moves that run underneath all of them are break the symmetry with a new constraint and match observations across views or frames. Depth is the purest instance of the Underdetermination primitive: one flat image cannot pin the answer, so the whole field is an elaborate negotiation over which extra constraint is cheapest to obtain and most reliable to use.

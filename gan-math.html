Generative adversarial networks — why it works, mathematically

Two networks fight each other: one tries to forge perfect fakes, the other tries to spot them. Their duel trains both to get better, until fakes become indistinguishable from real data.

The problem, as a quantity

A network can be trained to recognize patterns in real data, but creating new, realistic data from scratch is much harder. You could ask the network to guess what real data looks like and produce it — but the network has no way to know if its guess is good or bad. There's no scoreboard, no error message, no clear path to improvement.



One way out is to set up a competition: train one network to forge convincing fakes, and train a second network to tell real data from fakes. The forger learns by watching the detector catch mistakes. The detector learns by checking both real and fake samples. As they duel, both get sharper — and eventually the fakes become so good that the detector can't tell the difference.

The core ideas — and why they work

Two networks in a duel: a forger and a detective

min_G max_D E_x[log D(x)] + E_z[log(1 − D(G(z)))]

This is the core setup: two networks playing against each other. The detector (often called D) takes any piece of data and outputs a number between 0 and 1 — how confident it is that the data is real. The forger (called G) takes random noise as input and outputs fake data that it hopes will fool the detector. The equation says: the detector wants to be right as often as possible (make real data give numbers close to 1, fake data close to 0), while the forger wants the detector to be wrong (make it output numbers close to 1 for the fakes).



The equation is a minimax game: one player (detector) tries to maximize its score, the other (forger) tries to minimize it. The detector looks at real examples x and measures how expected they are under its own view — how close to 1 its outputs are. Then it looks at fake examples made by the forger from random noise z, and measures how expected *those* are to be fakes — how close to 0 its outputs are. The forger wants to raise those fake-data scores, to fool the detector. This setup forces both to improve together.

The detective's feedback tells the forger how to improve

G is nudged to raise D(G(z)) — to fool the current detective

The forger doesn't get explicit feedback like "your fake is wrong because the nose is too wide." Instead, it gets a single number from the detector: a score between 0 and 1. The forger's job is to adjust its internal numbers (its weights) to make that score higher. If the detector outputs 0.2 for a fake, the forger wants the next fake to push it toward 1.



This is where the duel creates a training signal. The detector is not static — it's being retrained constantly to get better at catching fakes. So the forger is always chasing a moving target. When the forger improves, the detector retrains itself to spot the new generation of fakes, which forces the forger to improve again. The two networks push each other upward in a feedback loop.

At the ideal balance, fakes are indistinguishable from real

optimum: p_G = p_data, D ≡ 1/2 everywhere

If both networks train perfectly, something remarkable happens: the forger becomes so good that its fake data comes from the exact same spread as the real data. The detector can no longer tell them apart, so its best strategy is to guess randomly — output 0.5 for everything. At this ideal point, the forger's output spread equals the real data's spread, and the detector outputs 0.5 everywhere.



This equilibrium is the goal. In practice, networks don't reach it perfectly — training stops early, architectures matter, and hyperparameters affect the path — but the math shows us what we're aiming for: a forger that has learned to mimic the true data, indistinguishable in the detector's eyes.

The duel can go unstable: mode collapse and endless oscillation

G collapses to a few outputs; D and G chase each other, never settling

The duel doesn't always reach balance. Sometimes the forger gets stuck making only a few kinds of fake outputs — say, always generating faces that look similar, even though real faces are diverse. This is called mode collapse: the forger found a shortcut that fools the current detector, and never learned the full range. Meanwhile, the detector keeps improving at spotting the one trick the forger knows.



Other times, neither network settles. The detector improves, forcing the forger to change; the forger changes, forcing the detector to retrain; they chase each other without reaching stability. The equations themselves don't prevent this — they just describe what each network wants at each step. Making the duel actually converge to the ideal balance is a separate problem that researchers solve with tricks: different loss functions, architectural choices, or training schedules.

The approaches, as variations on the math

The core duel stays the same, but researchers tune the game to make it stable. The loss function can be swapped: a smoother distance measure penalizes the gap between the fakes and the real data differently than the plain scoring, sometimes with better convergence. The network architectures affect what patterns the forger and detector can learn — deeper, wider, or convolutional networks learn different kinds of features. Training schedules control how many steps each network trains before the other retains — training the detector too many times can overfit to the current fakes, while training the forger too many times can cause it to memorize. Spectral normalization and other regularizers keep the detector's slopes under control so it doesn't collapse or blow up during training. The thread never changes: two networks in competition, each improving by responding to the other's feedback.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

1Loss function design
L_critic = E[D(real)] - E[D(fake)] (or log-odds, or squared error)
The critic's loss function decides what it's actually measuring—how far apart real and fake images appear, how confident it is making mistakes, or how squared-off the gap is. Switching from logarithms to squared error, for instance, creates gentler slopes near the decision boundary, so the generator gets steadier feedback on how to improve instead of sudden jumps. Wasserstein distance measures the minimum cost to transform one batch into another, which gives the forger information even when fakes are obviously wrong, preventing silence.
2Training stabilization
L_critic + λ · ||∇_x D(x)||^2 (gradient penalty with weight λ)
Without limits, the critic can learn ultra-steep cliffs between real and fake, which sends wild blame signals to the forger and causes it to overshoot or oscillate forever. Adding a penalty for steep slopes keeps the critic's surface smooth. Spectral normalization, an alternative, rescales the critic's weights so no single weight can amplify signals too much. Both methods act like friction: they damp the oscillations that cause mode collapse, where the forger forgets how to make half the image types.
3Architectural conditioning
G(z, c) and D(x, c) where c is class label or auxiliary info
A plain generator takes only random noise as input and has to guess what class to make. Adding the class label directly to both the generator and critic is like giving the forger a blueprint: 'paint a dog, not a cat.' Progressive growing starts training with tiny 4-by-4 images, then gradually adds layers to build larger ones—this lets the generator nail overall shape before worrying about fine details. Self-attention lets distant pixels communicate, so a dog's left ear can influence the right ear.
4Game-theoretic objective
max_G D(G(z)) instead of min_G log(1 - D(G(z)))
The original minmax game has the forger minimizing log(1 - D(G(z))), which creates tiny blame signals when fakes are obviously fake—the detective is so certain the forger gets almost no feedback. Flipping the generator's goal to maximizing D(G(z)) instead makes the signals stay large and informative even early in training. Relativistic GANs flip the detective's job: instead of rating fakes as 0 and real as 1, it predicts which is more realistic—this naturally forces balance because improving fakes automatically makes the job harder.

The through-line.

The core problem is: how do you train a network to generate realistic data when you have no way to measure if the output is good? Traditional training needs an error signal — a comparison to a ground truth. GANs sidestep this by building the error signal into a second network. The detector learns to spot fakes, and its feedback becomes the forger's training signal.



Two plain ideas carry it: an adversarial game replaces a direct error measure — instead of comparing fake-to-real pixel by pixel, let one network learn what real data looks like and judge the fakes against its learned model, and this game only converges to a good solution if both networks improve together — the detector must stay sharp enough to guide the forger, but not so sharp that the forger gives up. Balancing that tension is where the art of training GANs lives.

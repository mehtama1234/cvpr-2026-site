Energy-based models — describing a distribution by its landscape

The most flexible way to describe a probability distribution is to say, for every point, how plausible it is — a single scalar, its energy — and declare low energy to mean high probability. The catch is that turning those energies into real probabilities needs an impossible sum over all of space. The beautiful discovery is that you never have to: you can both train the energy and draw samples from it using only its gradient. This page is the plain story of that idea — the landscape, the downhill walk that samples it, and the mixing tax — on a real run. It is also the parent of diffusion.

1 · The problem — the normalizer you can't compute

Suppose you describe a distribution with any function you like: give each point x a score, and say more score = more probable. It is maximally expressive. But to make it a real probability you must divide by the total score summed over every possible x — an integral in a huge space that you cannot compute. Most of probabilistic modelling is a fight with that one intractable number.

The problem, stated once: you can score how plausible each point is with a totally free function, but converting those scores into a normalized probability requires an intractable sum over all of space.

2 · The reframe — you only ever need the gradient

The idea: work with the energy's slope, never its total. Define p(x) ∝ exp(−E(x)) — low energy, high probability. Two things you actually want turn out to need only the gradient of E, never the normalizer. To SAMPLE: walk downhill in energy with a dash of noise (Langevin dynamics) and you drift to the likely regions. To TRAIN: push the energy down on real data and up on your own samples until they match (contrastive divergence) — or directly learn the score, the gradient of log-probability, which by definition has no normalizer in it. The impossible sum simply never appears.
p(x) ∝ exp(−E(x)) sample: x ← x − (ε/2)·∇E(x) + √ε·noise score: s(x) = ∇log p = −∇E     # everything you need is a gradient of E; the normalizer Z is never computed

It is the most flexible model of a distribution there is — bought with a sampling process that is a random walk, with all the promise and pain that implies.

3 · Seen on a real run — landscape, walk, and mixing

1 · The model is a landscape

We trained an energy network on three blobs of 2D data. It never outputs a normalized probability — it outputs an energy, and we read probability as low-energy = likely. Here is the learned landscape (bright = high probability, i.e. low energy): three wells have formed exactly where the data lives.

Crucially, the network was trained without ever computing the normalizer — it only ever needed the energy's gradient. Any scalar function is a valid energy, so this is a maximally flexible way to describe a distribution.

2 · Sampling by walking downhill — Langevin dynamics

How do you draw samples with no normalizer? Start anywhere and walk downhill in energy, with a little random noise each step, and you drift toward the wells while the noise keeps you exploring — that is Langevin dynamics. Each brown dot starts scattered; the trails show it flowing to a low-energy mode (white rings = the true data modes). It needs only the gradient ∇E — the intractable normalizer never appears.

3 · The honest catch — mixing

Sampling is a random walk, and random walks are slow to mix. Our Langevin samples land near the modes but stay a bit looser than the real data (mean distance to the nearest mode 0.692) — the chains do not always settle tightly, and can get stuck. This slow, sometimes-unreliable mixing is the standing pain of energy-based models, and exactly the problem the modern score-based reincarnation was built to tame.

4 · Why it matters — and why it became diffusion

Energy models are the most general generative idea: no architecture constraint, no invertibility, just a freely-shaped landscape — which makes them natural for composition (add two energies to satisfy two constraints at once) and for anomaly detection (a high-energy point is an outlier). Their weakness was always sampling: MCMC mixes slowly and unreliably. The fix reshaped modern generative AI — learn the score at many noise levels and sample by gradually denoising, and the random walk becomes stable and fast. That is diffusion: a score-based energy model in its most successful form.

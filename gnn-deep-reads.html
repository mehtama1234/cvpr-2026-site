1 · Building the graph: scene graph generation

Graph neural networks excel at reasoning over structured relationships by passing messages along edges—each node receives information from its neighbors in a permutation-invariant way. Scene graph generation exemplifies this principle: computer vision extracts entities (nodes) and their spatial or functional relationships (edges), then a GNN propagates information across the scene to refine predictions. This theme explores four modern approaches to building and reasoning over scene graphs, from factor graphs for probabilistic functional inference to flow-matching for continuous-discrete denoising to efficient panoptic methods to robustness under visual corruption. Together they show how neural graphs enable machines to move beyond object classification toward genuine scene understanding.
CVPR'26FunFact: Building Probabilistic Functional 3D Scene Graphs via Factor-Graph Reasoning
Problem
Functional 3D scene understanding—identifying which objects control or operate other objects, such as which switch turns which lamp—suffers from inherent visual ambiguity. Existing methods reason over isolated object pairs, ignoring scene-wide interdependence that would help resolve ambiguous functional assignments.
Approach
FunFact uses a dual factor graph where candidate functional edges become binary variables constrained by two types of factors: proximity factors (biasing shorter edges) and cardinality factors (penalizing invalid connection counts). A graph-based belief propagation algorithm jointly infers all functional relations, yielding calibrated confidence scores. Nodes are 3D objects and parts; edges represent candidate functional relationships such as knob-burner pairings.
Why it matters
Modeling functional relations as an interconnected graph captures the constraint that one knob activating one burner restricts which burners other knobs can control. The factor graph formulation lets the model reason globally over all candidate edges simultaneously rather than independently, yielding far better confidence calibration (ECE 0.07 vs 0.51 for pairwise methods) and stronger recall on ambiguous spatial configurations.

CVPR'26Can We Build Scene Graphs, Not Classify Them? FlowSG: Progressive Scene Graph Generation via Flow Matching
Problem
Treating scene graph generation as multi-label classification ignores the geometric and semantic continuity of visual relationships, leading to hard label boundaries and difficulty representing relationship uncertainty. Long-tail bias further degrades performance on rare predicates.
Approach
FlowSG casts scene graph generation as joint flow matching over a hybrid discrete-continuous state space. A graph transformer backbone with relation-modulated self-attention processes object and predicate features quantized via VQ-VAE. Continuous bounding boxes are denoised via Conditional Flow Matching; discrete semantic labels are denoised via Discrete Flow Matching using a Continuous-Time Markov Chain. Both flows train jointly with shared transformer blocks.
Why it matters
Modeling the scene graph as a continuous denoising problem lets the network represent relationship uncertainty and compositional structure rather than forcing hard class boundaries. The graph structure—nodes and relation edges—flows through the network as the model progressively refines both geometry and semantics together, enabling more expressive scene understanding than separate classification heads.

Problem

Approach

CVPR'26Robo-SGG: Exploiting Layout-Oriented Normalization and Restitution Can Improve Robust Scene Graph Generation
Problem
Scene graph models assume clean images but fail on corrupted inputs (noise, blur, weather distortions). Visual features undergo domain shift under corruption, eroding performance on structural relationships. Existing robust methods focus on object-centric features while neglecting robustness of spatial and relational reasoning.
Approach
Robo-SGG is a plug-and-play module inserting Layout-Oriented Normalization and Restitution (NRM) plus a Layout-Embedded Encoder (LEE). NRM applies instance normalization to reduce domain-specific variance, extracts residuals, and uses layout-aware attention to preserve structural features. LEE replaces concatenation-based fusion with gated fusion that adaptively weights visual features versus spatial cues, allowing the message-passing graph to prioritize layout information under corruption.
Why it matters
Layout—the global spatial arrangement of objects—is inherently more robust to visual corruption than color or texture cues. By routing messages through a layout-aware attention mechanism and using gated fusion to adaptively balance visual and spatial information, the scene graph can maintain robust understanding of object relationships even as individual feature channels degrade under noise or blur.

2 · Reasoning over relations: relational graphs for recognition and pose

Graph neural networks reason over visual scenes and human structure by modeling entities and their relationships as graphs, passing messages through edges to propagate constraints and context. In computer vision, this relational reasoning appears across diverse tasks: understanding hidden relationships in videos, synthesizing geometrically valid 3D scenes with part-level constraints, learning robust classifiers despite label noise, and estimating anatomically plausible poses even under occlusion and clutter. The core insight is that relations—spatial, geometric, skeletal, or noisy—are edges in a graph that a GNN can traverse to refine predictions beyond what isolated features alone permit.

3 · Native graphs: GNNs for structured domains

Graph neural networks are the natural choice when data already lives on a graph: molecules, medical images with multiple views, CAD geometry, point clouds, and compression landscapes all possess inherent connectivity. Rather than forcing structure into fixed grids or sequences, GNNs message-pass directly over the graph, respecting topology. This makes them especially powerful for incomplete data (missing modalities), adaptive matching (2D-3D correspondence), content-dependent compression, and domains where topology itself must be learned. CVPR 2026 shows GNNs solving problems that struggle in flat domains: semantic-aware matching without hand-crafted features, resilience to missing medical modalities, adaptive compression that respects image content, and dynamic topology in evolving graph structures.
CVPR'26SAG-GNN: Semantic-Aware Guided GNN for Descriptor-Free 2D-3D Matching
Problem
2D image features must match to 3D scene points for localization and reconstruction, but hand-crafted descriptors are rigid and dataset-specific. Descriptor-free approaches need to learn matching without fixed feature representations.
Approach
SAG-GNN uses semantic information to guide a graph neural network, where 2D and 3D points form nodes and GNN message-passing learns correspondence weights. Semantic cues from the scene directly influence edge weights and aggregation, enabling learned matching that adapts to data variation without descriptors.
Why it matters
2D-3D matching is fundamentally a graph problem: points are nodes, possible correspondences are edges, and semantic relationships determine which connections matter. A GNN respects this structure and learns descriptors end-to-end rather than relying on brittle hand-crafted ones.

4 · Relational grounding: graphs across video, HOI, and open-vocabulary

Graphs encode relations between entities—objects, actions, people—providing structure for reasoning across visual scenes. This theme explores how relational representations ground vision models across three critical domains: open-vocabulary scene understanding, where graphs bridge objects to semantic knowledge; surgical video triplet recognition, where temporal edges connect actions and instruments over time; and human-object interaction detection, where spatial queries ground interaction reasoning without instance-level supervision. Relational similarity itself emerges as a distinct visual dimension: images share underlying transformational logic even when surface attributes differ. These papers demonstrate that explicitly modeling relations—via graph mixing, co-attention, or grounding queries—sharpens discrimination and enables transfer from weak to strong supervision.
CVPR'26Mixture-of-Experts based Feature Decoupling for Open Vocabulary Scene Graph Generation
Problem
Open vocabulary scene graph generation fails to extract discriminative attributes for novel objects and misses semantic interactions between objects and relationships, leading to confusion among visually similar categories.
Approach
MoE-FD uses multiple expert layers that decouple object and relationship features adaptively, with a routing network weighted by semantic priors from ConceptNet. Iterative cross-attention models semantic interactions as explicit relations between objects and relationships in the graph.
Why it matters
Relations are the graph structure: by separating what objects *are* from how they *relate*, and letting a semantic knowledge graph guide expert routing, the model reasons over structured object-relationship graphs rather than flat features, improving both discriminability and alignment with open-vocabulary semantics.

5 · The robot scene graph: relational maps a robot builds and plans over

A robot navigating a human space needs a lightweight way to hold what it knows: where objects sit, how they relate to each other, and what changes when it touches something. Scene graphs—compact node-and-edge maps where objects are nodes and spatial or logical relations are edges—let a robot build, update, and reason over a structured view of its world. A language model can use this graph to plan a multi-step task. When the map is wrong, the robot can actively move things out of the way and re-observe to keep the graph current. Four papers from ICRA 2025 show the payoff: faster perception on embedded hardware, real-time map maintenance during manipulation, physics-aware forward planning from point clouds, and coordinated perception between robot and human when their views of the room diverge.

A room classifier (Random Forest) is added to a 3D map-building pipeline that segments the scene. The whole system updates the scene graph at over 10 frames per second on a small embedded computer (Nvidia Jetson), keeping the graph current as the robot moves.
Why it matters
By reusing features already computed for mapping, the room classification stays lightweight enough to run in real time on robot hardware. The scene graph becomes richer and faster without expensive computation.

6 · Graphs as the robot's structure: morphology, multi-agent, and prediction

A robot is fundamentally a graph: joints and links form a tree-like skeleton; multi-agent teams are graphs of agents interacting through proximity and communication; traffic scenes are graphs of vehicles and lane structure. Graph neural networks respect this structure natively. When a model's neurons mirror the robot's body, it inherits the robot's symmetries and constraints as inductive bias, slashing data requirements. When a GNN treats a multi-robot team as a connected structure, coordination emerges without explicit commands. When a traffic prediction system models vehicles and lanes as a heterogeneous graph, it learns which relationships matter for safety. This theme shows how respecting the graph structure of robotics problems leads to models that are more data-efficient, more interpretable, and more robust to distribution shift.

The GNN encodes the robot's body structure directly: each joint becomes a node, each link becomes an edge. The message-passing updates propagate contact information through the skeletal graph, and the model bakes in body symmetry as built-in inductive bias so knowledge transfers across legs.
Why it matters
A legged robot IS a graph of joints and rigid links. By aligning the GNN's computation graph with the robot's morphological graph, the model starts with structural knowledge already embedded, needing far fewer parameters and training samples to learn ground contact perception.

ROBOTICS'25SE-STDGNN: A Self-Evolving Spatial-Temporal Directed Graph Neural Network for Multi-Vehicle Trajectory Prediction
Problem
Learned trajectory predictors fail when deployed in traffic conditions different from training: a model trained on highways struggles in dense urban traffic, and vice versa. The static model has no way to adapt as distribution shifts.
Approach
The system builds a spatial-temporal graph where vehicles are nodes and proximity edges connect them across time steps. It adds self-evolution: the GNN's own weights update during deployment via an evolving GCN mechanism, so the model continuously adapts to local traffic patterns without retraining.
Why it matters
Traffic is a dynamic graph of vehicle interactions. By making the graph neural network itself adaptive during inference, the model tracks shifts in the underlying traffic graph structure — density, lane usage, behavior — that it never saw in training data.

ROBOTICS'25HGAT-CP: Heterogeneous Graph Attention Network for Collision Prediction in Autonomous Driving
Problem
Predicting imminent collisions requires reasoning about how vehicles and road infrastructure interact over time. Hand-coded interaction rules are incomplete and brittle, missing the subtle patterns that lead to danger.
Approach
The model treats vehicles and lanes as two node types in a heterogeneous graph, with edges representing spatial proximity and lane membership. A graph attention mechanism learns which vehicle-lane relationships matter most for collision risk; an LSTM reads the temporal sequence of these attention patterns to track evolving danger.
Why it matters
Autonomous driving scenes are heterogeneous graphs of vehicles, lanes, and signals. By letting the attention mechanism discover which graph edges matter for safety from data, rather than from hand-written rules, the model finds collision predictors that humans might overlook.

ROBOTICS'25Coordinated Multi-Robot Navigation with Formation Adaptation
Problem
Robot teams moving in a fixed formation like a wedge cannot squeeze through narrow passages and get stuck. Rigid shapes break multi-robot coordination when the environment demands flexibility.
Approach
A hierarchical system uses a graph neural network at the group level to decide when and how to reshape the formation, treating robots as nodes in a dynamic graph. Each robot independently handles local movement and collision avoidance; a spring-damper model smooths shape transitions. The GNN reads the spatial graph of robot positions and outputs formation changes in real time.
Why it matters
A robot team is a graph of spatial neighbors and communication links. By using a GNN to coordinate formation changes at the group level while letting individual robots plan locally, the team can dynamically rewire its effective topology, squeezing through gaps and reforming without shaking or collisions.

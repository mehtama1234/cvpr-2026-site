1 · Craft a better attack

If you sprinkle random static onto an image, a neural network shrugs it off. The whole art of an adversarial attack is aiming the change instead of scattering it. The attacker asks the model one question over and over: which direction makes you most wrong? That direction is the gradient, and by nudging every pixel a hair along it, the attacker pushes the image across the model's decision boundary while it still looks unchanged to a human. The classic recipes, FGSM and PGD, are just different ways of taking that gradient step, once or many times. The three open frontiers all start from that same aimed step: making the change imperceptible so no person or filter notices it, making it transferable so a perturbation built against one model still fools models the attacker never saw, and making it physically realizable so it survives being printed, photographed, or run through a defense that tries to clean the image.

CVPR'26DASH: A Meta-Attack Framework for Synthesizing Effective and Stealthy Adversarial Examples
Problem

No single attack recipe wins against every defended model. Modern robust classifiers on the RobustBench leaderboard have learned to shrug off individual attacks, so an attacker who commits to just one method keeps losing to whichever defense that method happens to be weak against.

Approach

DASH stops picking one attack and instead blends ten of them at once, including the whole FGSM and PGD family: FGSM, PGD, CW, FAB, and momentum and input-transformation variants like MI-FGSM, DI-FGSM, and TI-FGSM. At each stage it learns a softmax weight for every base attack, leaning on the ones that are working against a given model and input while quieting the ones that just add ugly distortion. The whole thing is trained end-to-end with Adam under a single meta-loss that rewards fooling the model while a structural-similarity (SSIM) penalty keeps the image looking untouched. This directly trades strength against imperceptibility: the combined perturbation is stronger than any one attack yet no more visible. Across seven RobustBench models on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet it beats every individual attack and prior meta-attacks at equal or lower perceptual distortion.

Why it matters

Because the boundary is paper-thin, ten aimed steps averaged together find a worst direction that no single gradient step could, slipping past defenses tuned to expect only one kind of attack.

CVPR'26AdvFM: Lookahead Flow-Matching Velocity-Field Attacks for Imperceptible and Transferable Adversarial Examples
Problem

Attacks that build fake images from generative models tend not to transfer well and get scrubbed away by defenses. The trouble is that diffusion pipelines inject random noise at every step, and their perturbations point off the manifold of natural images, exactly the directions a purifier is built to erase.

Approach

AdvFM moves the attack out of pixel space and into the velocity field of a flow-matching generator, the smooth, deterministic transport that carries noise to an image. Because that transport has no random noise, small nudges propagate with low variance and stay predictable. The method reconstructs the perturbation at the endpoint of the flow, converts it into a change in the velocity field, and amplifies it with PGD-style steps; a lookahead variant optimizes over both the current point and a rolled-out future point so the step aims further ahead. Crucially the perturbations land in tangent, on-manifold directions that look natural and align with robust gradients, so they survive purification and adversarial training. On ImageNet it transfers to black-box classifiers better than diffusion-based unrestricted attacks and holds up against those defenses.

Why it matters

When the perturbation rides along the manifold instead of jumping off it, the tiny step across the paper-thin boundary looks like a real image the whole way, giving a defender nothing to clean.

CVPR'26The Power of Decaying Steps: Enhancing Attack Stability and Transferability for Sign-based Optimizers
Problem

The workhorse attacks I-FGSM and MI-FGSM take a fixed-size step in the sign of the gradient every iteration, and it turns out this never really settles. Run them longer and the success rate can actually drop; in theory sign-based steps fail to converge even on simple problems, because a constant step-size violates the classic rule that steps must shrink over time.

Approach

The fix, MDCS, re-reads a sign-based attack as coordinate-wise gradient descent and forces each pixel-coordinate's step-size to only ever decrease. For each coordinate it caps the new step at the smaller of its current best and one over the momentum magnitude, so once a step shrinks it can never grow back, killing the oscillation. Momentum still accumulates past gradients to keep aiming consistently, and the whole scheme drops into I-FGSM, PGD, and their momentum variants. This buys a proven convergence rate for the attack, so more iterations reliably help instead of hurting. On NIPS2017 it lifts MI-FGSM's black-box transfer from 59.4% to 67.2% against VGG16, and the OPS variant is strongest in ensemble attacks across classification, VQA, and cross-modal retrieval.

Why it matters

A stable, converging optimizer lands the perturbation precisely on the far side of the paper-thin boundary rather than bouncing back and forth across it, which is exactly what makes the same attack carry over to unseen models.

CVPR'26Improving Adversarial Transferability with Local Perturbation Augmentation
Problem

When you sharpen an attack against one surrogate model, it tends to overfit to that model's exact quirks and then fails to transfer to the black-box target you actually care about. Existing tricks that jitter the image with random neighborhood sampling are wasteful and never fully use the structure of the perturbation itself.

Approach

LPAA fights this over-specialization by carving each iteration's search into many small local subspaces rather than optimizing one global perturbation. It randomly masks part of the perturbation, restricting the step to a subspace, does this N independent ways, and aggregates the gradients, while a coefficient dynamically widens the search range. It also starts from a smarter place: a global momentum initialization turns an accumulated gradient direction into the opening perturbation, giving a highly transferable starting aim before the first real step. The whole thing plugs into standard momentum-based FGSM optimization. On ImageNet it reaches 80.0% average black-box success across eleven CNN and ViT models, versus 72.2% for the prior MuMoDIG method.

Why it matters

By aiming across many overlapping subspaces instead of one, the attack finds a direction that crosses the paper-thin boundary of the surrogate and of models it has never seen, since it stops memorizing the quirks of any single one.

2 · Break the new models

The story of adversarial robustness used to be about image classifiers, but by 2026 the interesting work has moved somewhere else: the newest, most capable models are now the target. The fragile decision boundary never healed when the field switched architectures; it simply re-appeared, wearing a new disguise, every time a new kind of model arrived. What changes from model to model is the attack surface — the specific place an attacker reaches in to nudge the boundary. In a vision-language model, that surface is the stream of image tokens flowing into a language model, so attacks now aim at the internal value features inside attention blocks rather than raw pixels. In a self-driving perception stack, the surface is a physical, 3D-consistent object that stays adversarial across every camera view and every frame. In a Gaussian-splatting reconstruction system, the surface is not the answer at all but the cost of computing it — a poisoned image that makes the model spawn runaway Gaussians until the GPU chokes. And in a commercial black-box multimodal LLM, the surface is transferability: crafting a perturbation on models you own that survives the jump to a model you can only call through an API. Same fragile boundary, four new doors into it.

CVPR'26V-Attack: Targeting Disentangled Value Features for Controllable Adversarial Attacks on LVLMs
Problem

Large vision-language models turn an image into a set of tokens and hand them to a language model, and that hand-off is the new attack surface. Earlier attacks poked at the raw patch features of the image, but those features tangle many concepts together, so an attacker could rarely change one specific thing in the scene — success rates on fine-grained, multi-concept manipulation stayed low.

Approach

V-Attack skips the tangled patch features and instead targets the value features computed inside the transformer attention blocks. The insight is that these value features are more disentangled: they suppress the noisy global-context channels and hold onto clean, local, per-concept meaning, which makes them a precise handle to grab. A Self-Value Enhancement module first sharpens the semantic richness already sitting in those value features. Then a Text-Guided Value Manipulation module uses a text prompt to locate the source concept and push it toward a chosen target concept, optimizing across an ensemble so the shift is reliable. The result is control: an attacker can steer what the model reports seeing, one concept at a time, with a reported 36% average jump in attack success over prior methods and strong black-box transfer across LLaVA, InternVL, DeepseekVL, and GPT-4o.

Why it matters

The boundary a vision-language model draws between concepts is just as fragile as an old classifier's — V-Attack shows the crack simply moved inward, to the value features where the image is quietly translated into language.

CVPR'26SABER: Spatially Consistent 3D Universal Adversarial Objects for BEV Detectors
Problem

Self-driving cars fuse several cameras into a bird's-eye-view map and run 3D object detection on it, and that perception stack is the surface under attack here. Older attacks were invasive and impractical — they required physically painting or modifying the target vehicle itself — and non-invasive alternatives fell apart because a patch that fools one camera view or one frame stops fooling the next.

Approach

SABER builds the first universal, non-invasive, 3D-consistent adversarial object — something you place near a vehicle without ever touching it. Because the same physical object has to stay adversarial from every angle and across time, the method optimizes it through a differentiable renderer that models the real scene geometry and how the object occludes and is occluded. A Realistic Occlusion Processing Module keeps the object physically plausible as viewpoints and frames change. Crucially, the optimization attacks the detector's internal BEV feature representation with a scene-confusion loss, not just its final boxes, so the confusion holds up across views and distances. Doing so exposes something deeper: the detectors lean heavily on learned environmental priors and contextual cues, and a single well-placed object can hijack that reliance.

Why it matters

Lifting perception into 3D did not close the boundary — SABER shows the same fragility now lives in the BEV feature space, reachable by an ordinary object sitting on the roadside.

CVPR'26RemedyGS: Defend 3D Gaussian Splatting Against Computation Cost Attacks
Problem

3D Gaussian splatting reconstructs a scene by spawning many small Gaussians to fit the input images, and that adaptive cost is itself the attack surface. An adversary can poison the input images so the system triggers runaway Gaussian proliferation — blowing up GPU memory, training time, and rendering latency — a denial-of-service by resource exhaustion rather than a wrong answer. Naive defenses like blurring the input or hard-capping the Gaussian count fail, because they cannot undo the non-linear poisoning and they wreck reconstruction quality for everyone.

Approach

RemedyGS defends without punishing legitimate users. It pairs a detector, which separates attacked images (carrying poisoned textures) from safe ones, with a learnable purifier that recovers a benign image from an attacked one. The purifier is trained with a discriminator that forces the recovered images to match the distribution of real natural images, so purification restores a usable input instead of a smeared one. Only inputs flagged as compromised are ever purified, which preserves service quality for honest requests. In practice it drops GPU memory from 47GB to 11GB and training time from 48 to 24 minutes while lifting rendering from 31 to 185 FPS on poisoned inputs, and it holds up against white-box, black-box, and adaptive attacks.

Why it matters

Even a purely geometric reconstruction pipeline inherits the fragile boundary — here the attacker does not flip a label but weaponizes the model's own adaptive compute, and defending it still comes down to detecting and reversing an adversarial input.

CVPR'26Omni-Attack: Adversarial Attacks on Open-Ended VQA in Black-Box Multimodal LLMs
Problem

Commercial multimodal LLMs answer open-ended visual questions from behind an API, so the only attack surface is transferability — a perturbation crafted on models you control has to survive the jump to a model you can only call. This is hard for two reasons: open-ended answers have no fixed target to aim at, so a valid adversarial answer must be invented, and pushing a perturbation through a single surrogate model rarely transfers to a strong commercial target.

Approach

Omni-Attack works in two stages. First it uses a language model to construct a plausible-but-wrong target answer for each question, with a cycle-verification step confirming the fake answer is clearly different from the true one yet believable enough to fool an evaluator. Second, it runs a multi-target ensemble attack: one norm-bounded perturbation (epsilon = 8/255) is optimized to transfer across several surrogate models at once, which sharply raises the odds it also lands on the hidden black-box target. A location-aware OCR pipeline finds text regions in text-heavy images and perturbs them specifically. The authors package this as AdvRobustBench, a standardized 1000-example robustness benchmark, and report 71.8% attack success on GPT-4.1 — while noting the Claude models were the most robust in their tests (roughly 15.5% and 22.4%).

Why it matters

Hiding a model behind an API does not repair the boundary — Omni-Attack shows it can still be pried open from the outside, through perturbations that transfer from open surrogates into a black-box system.

3 · Defend the boundary

Once you accept that a tiny, invisible nudge to an image can flip a network's answer, the question becomes: how do you fight back? The corpus organizes into three defense families. The first is adversarial training — you bake the attack into the training loop, generating adversarial examples on the fly and teaching the model to classify them correctly; it works, but it is expensive (some methods burn nine times the compute of ordinary training) and it tends to dent clean accuracy, because a model braced for attacks is a model that trusts its input less. The second is purification — instead of retraining the model, you scrub the incoming image, pushing it back toward the clean data it was meant to see, using filters, noise, or a short optimization at test time. The third is certification — rather than just measuring robustness against known attacks, you try to prove that no perturbation within some radius can change the answer, a mathematical guarantee rather than an empirical score. In this corpus the first two families are thriving and the third is nearly absent — and that absence is itself a finding: the field is far better at building defenses than at proving they hold.

CVPR'26A Combination of Noise and Bilateral Filters Achieve Supralinear and Scalable Adversarial Robustness in CNNs
Problem

The standard way to make a vision model robust is adversarial training, but it is brutally expensive — roughly nine times the compute of normal training — and it scales badly as models grow. Cheaper tricks like adding noise or smoothing the image help a little, but they had only ever been studied one at a time, in isolation.

Approach

This is a purification approach: rather than retrain the network, it cleans the input before the network ever sees it. The authors prove that two simple operations fight adversarial noise through different mechanisms — Gaussian noise and a bilateral filter (a smoother that blurs flat regions while preserving edges) — so stacking them yields more robustness than the sum of their parts, what they call a supralinear gain. The result is a tiny, cheap preprocessor that bolts onto any model, and it can also be layered on top of adversarial training. On the RobustBench leaderboard it lands second on AutoAttack and third overall while using only about 35 percent of the training compute, half the parameters, and 15 percent of the training data of the state of the art.

Why it matters

It shows that robustness need not mean nine-times-the-compute retraining — a principled combination of cheap input-cleaning steps can compete with the heavyweights, which is what makes defense practical for real deployment.

CVPR'26Mitigating Error Amplification in Fast Adversarial Training
Problem

Adversarial training is strongest when it uses multi-step attacks to build hard training examples, but that is slow. The fast version uses a single-step attack instead, and it is far cheaper — but small mistakes in that one step compound into bad adversarial examples, so the resulting models end up much less robust.

Approach

This sits squarely in the adversarial training family — baking the attack into the loop — and tries to make the cheap single-step version behave like the expensive multi-step one. The authors decompose the single-step error into two culprits: a noisy gradient estimate and a step size that does not match the local loss landscape. They then correct each: the gradient is smoothed by averaging over random perturbations or with momentum, the step size is learned adaptively per sample or per layer, and training is biased toward the hard examples where the single-step attack tends to fail. The payoff is a better robustness-versus-speed trade-off — 5 to 8 percent more robustness than plain fast training while staying 3 to 5 times faster than multi-step methods.

Why it matters

It attacks the central tension of the whole family — that the strongest defense is also the most expensive — by diagnosing exactly why the cheap shortcut fails and patching those specific errors.

CVPR'26Verifying Neural Network Robustness with Dual Perturbations
Problem

Certification tools try to prove a network is robust, but existing ones consider only one kind of perturbation at a time — either independent pixel noise or a restricted set of convolutional distortions. Real images suffer several at once: a motion blur (spatially correlated) layered on top of brightness jitter (independent noise). No prior verifier could handle both simultaneously.

Approach

This is the rare certification paper — it does not build a defense, it tries to prove properties of a model. The VeriDou framework defines a flexible space of convolutional perturbations, where each variable controls one entry of the blur kernel, so it covers arbitrary continuous distortions rather than a fixed few. It then combines that with a masked pixel-level noise space, folds both into a single unified input specification, and hands that to standard off-the-shelf verifiers (αβ-CROWN, NeuralSAT, Venus) to check whether the classification stays consistent across the entire combined region. The striking result: on networks that existing methods had certified as up to 100 percent robust, testing under these dual perturbations surfaces adversarial examples up to 99 percent of the time.

Why it matters

This is the certified-versus-empirical gap made concrete — a network can carry a clean certificate under a narrow threat model and still shatter under a realistic combination of distortions, meaning a proof is only as trustworthy as the perturbation space it was proven over. That so few papers in this corpus even attempt such proofs is itself the finding: certification remains the least-traveled of the three defense roads.

CVPR'26A Provable Energy-Guided Test-Time Defense: Boosting Adversarial Robustness of Large Vision-Language Models
Problem

Large vision-language models are powerful but remain easy to fool — a small perturbation to the image alone can corrupt their answers on captioning or visual question answering, which is alarming for high-stakes uses. Retraining these giant models to be robust is impractical.

Approach

This is a purification defense that runs entirely at inference, with no retraining. The method, ET3, treats a clean-looking image as one with low energy under the model, and an adversarial one as high energy, so at test time it runs a short optimization through the vision encoder to nudge the input toward lower energy — effectively scrubbing it back toward the clean manifold before the model reads it. It carries a partial proof: the authors show mathematically that minimizing input energy leads to correct classification for the binary-classifier case. It is training-free, plug-and-play, adds only minor overhead, and stacks on top of already-robust models for extra gains across zero-shot classification, captioning, and VQA.

Why it matters

It brings a rare sliver of provability into purification, but the guarantee holds only for two-class problems — the real multi-class results rest on empirical evidence, a reminder of how narrow honest certification still is even when a paper reaches for it.

4 · Poison the training, and pay for robustness

A backdoor is a rule that gets baked into a model during training: the model behaves normally on ordinary inputs, but whenever a secret trigger is present it flips to an attacker-chosen answer. Because the corruption lives inside the learned weights and stays silent on clean data, ordinary accuracy tests look perfectly healthy and the damage is invisible until the trigger appears. The two papers here show the trigger can be planted upstream and then spread on its own — through a generative model that infects every classifier trained on its output, or through a lightweight LoRA adapter that hides the backdoor inside what looks like a routine fine-tune. The second half of the theme is a different but related lesson: robustness is not free. The features that survive worst-case, adversarial inputs are often not the same features that best separate the classes, so hardening a model against attack tends to cost clean accuracy, and pushing on both fights a third goal — handling ordinary out-of-distribution shift. Underneath all of it is one culprit: models lean on shortcut features like local texture instead of stable structure like shape, which is exactly why a tiny trigger or a mild corruption can knock them over.
CVPR'26Unleashing Stealthy Backdoor Pandemic by Infecting a Single Diffusion Model
Problem
Practitioners routinely grab an off-the-shelf diffusion model to generate synthetic training images, and never verify what the generator secretly encodes. If that one generator is compromised, every classifier later trained on its fake images can inherit a backdoor — a supply-chain hole where a single poisoned source infects unlimited downstream models with no further attacker involvement.
Approach
The attack, called Eidolon, plants a hidden trigger-to-target rule inside the diffusion model itself rather than in any one dataset. The infected generator still produces high-quality, correctly-labeled images on clean prompts, but a fraction of its output carries a subtle trigger paired with an attacker-chosen label, so a classifier trained on that output silently learns the backdoor as if it were a normal pattern. To stay hidden it is engineered to pass four strict tests: clean images look good, triggered images map consistently to the target, labels show no obvious noise, and the whole thing propagates passively — the downstream victim trains normally with no attacker in the loop. The trigger therefore spreads like a contagion: infect one generator, and it seeds the backdoor into every model that drinks from it.
Why it matters
It turns a single trusted generative model into a silent distribution channel for backdoors, showing that data poisoning no longer needs the attacker to touch each victim — the poison rides along inside seemingly clean synthetic data.
CVPR'26When LoRA Betrays: Backdooring Text-to-Image Models by Masquerading as Benign
Problem
LoRA adapters are the standard cheap way to customize a text-to-image model, and people download and stack them freely. That trust is the weakness: a shared adapter can carry a backdoor that looks indistinguishable from an ordinary style or subject fine-tune.
Approach
The attacker trains a LoRA adapter so that on normal prompts the model generates exactly what you would expect, preserving its benign behavior, but on a secret trigger prompt it switches to the attacker's target output. Because a LoRA is just a small low-rank weight update, the malicious behavior hides among the ordinary parameter changes any fine-tune produces, so the backdoor reads as a routine adaptation artifact and slips past inspection. The trigger is embedded during the adapter's own training and then propagates wherever that adapter is applied — the base model stays clean, yet anyone who loads the adapter inherits the hidden rule. The paper also proposes detection and defense mechanisms once the vulnerability is understood.
Why it matters
It shows the most convenient unit of model sharing — a tiny LoRA file — is also a convenient carrier for a hidden trigger, so borrowing an adapter is a real trust decision, not a cosmetic tweak.
CVPR'26The Geometry of Robustness: Optimizing Loss Landscape Curvature and Feature Manifold Alignment for Robust Finetuning of Vision-Language Models
Problem
When you fine-tune a vision-language model like CLIP, three goals pull against each other: accuracy on clean in-distribution data, generalization to shifted out-of-distribution data, and resistance to adversarial inputs. Existing methods can satisfy at most two of the three, leaving the model fragile on whichever axis they ignored — the robustness-accuracy tradeoff, made three-way.
Approach
GRACE reframes the tradeoff as a matter of geometry in two spaces at once. In parameter space, fragility shows up as sharp, lopsided minima — tiny weight changes cause big loss swings — so it uses a low-rank adversarial weight perturbation (LAR-AWP) to push training toward flatter, more forgiving minima. In feature space, the fragility is that clean, adversarial, and shifted versions of the same image drift onto different parts of the representation manifold; a Gram-Volume Alignment Loss keeps the class structure consistent across all three so the model does not rely on brittle shortcut features that only hold on clean inputs. By flattening the landscape and aligning the manifold together, it attacks the root cause of the conflict rather than trading one axis for another. The framework is grounded in robust PAC-Bayesian theory.
Why it matters
It gives a concrete, geometric account of why robustness usually costs accuracy — sharp minima and misaligned features — and shows the tension can be eased rather than merely paid, improving in-distribution and adversarial accuracy at once while holding out-of-distribution accuracy.
CVPR'26Revisiting Visual Corruptions in LVLMs: A Shape–Texture Perspective on Model Failures
Problem
Large vision-language models break down under everyday visual corruptions — blur, noise, color shifts — and prior work blamed weak grounding or language bias without asking how each corruption actually hurts perception. Different corruptions damage different things, so lumping them together hides the real failure mechanism.
Approach
The paper reorganizes corruptions along two perceptual axes: shape and texture. Shape-degrading corruptions like blur and geometric distortion wreck global structure, while texture-degrading corruptions like noise and color shift disrupt surface appearance — and the fragility comes from the model leaning on the wrong shortcut, trusting local texture cues that a mild corruption easily scrambles instead of stable shape. Its ST-CD framework builds two complementary contrastive views at inference time: an edge-extracted view that emphasizes contours and shape, and a jigsaw-permuted view that keeps texture statistics but destroys structure. By contrasting the model's normal reading against these views, it isolates whether the error is shape- or texture-driven and corrects accordingly, fusing the two fixes with adaptive entropy-based weighting. The whole correction is training-free, applied purely at decoding time.
Why it matters
It pins model fragility on an over-reliance on shortcut texture features, and shows that separating shape from texture failures lets you repair the specific weakness — a concrete handle on why robust and discriminative cues are not the same.

5 · Attack the sensor, hijack the action

In computer vision, an adversarial example ends at a wrong label: the classifier says gibbon instead of panda and the story stops there. In robotics the fooled perception does not stop; it feeds straight into a committed maneuver. A spoofed LiDAR return is not a misread pixel, it is a real vehicle that drifts more than a lane width out of its lane because its sense of where it is has been quietly poisoned. An adversarial prompt is not a clever wrong sentence, it is a robot arm that swings or a ground robot that carries out a harmful action it was supposed to refuse. The attack surface here is not a digital image buffer, it is the physical sensor and the natural-language instruction, both of which an attacker can touch from outside the system. A laser aimed at a LiDAR, a traffic cone placed on the road, a reworded command, or a slightly altered camera view all become live inputs to a control loop. That is why these papers matter: they measure the attack not in accuracy points but in meters of position error, in success rates of harmful actions, and in dropped task completions, because in robotics the loss is a trajectory, not a number.
ROBOTICS'25Slamspoof: Practical Lidar Spoofing Attacks on Localization Systems Guided by Scan Matching Vulnerability Analysis
Problem
Self-driving cars figure out where they are by matching the live LiDAR scan against a map, but that matching step can be fooled by an attacker who injects fake laser returns. The open question was practical: which physical spots along a route are actually vulnerable, and how much does a spoofing attack really move the car once it flows through the whole localization stack?
Approach
SLAMSpoof builds a Scan Matching Vulnerability Score, a point-by-point measure of how much each part of a LiDAR scan can be disturbed before the location estimate slips. That score tells the attacker exactly where a malicious laser injection will do the most damage, rather than spoofing blindly. They then fire real laser attacks guided by the score against three widely used LiDAR localization algorithms running on actual ground vehicles. The result is not a corrupted image but a corrupted sense of self-position: the car believes it is somewhere it is not.
Why it matters
The measured position error exceeds 4.2 meters, wider than a traffic lane, so the wrong percept becomes a wrong lane: a vehicle that trusts its poisoned localization steers itself out of its lane in the physical world.
ROBOTICS'25Jailbreaking LLM-Controlled Robots
Problem
When a large language model drives a robot, the words you feed it are effectively commands that turn into motion, and those words can be crafted to slip past the model safety rules. Before this work there was no systematic way to test whether such a robot could be talked into doing something physically harmful.
Approach
ROBOPAIR is an optimization loop that keeps rewording an adversarial prompt, checking how close it gets to the harmful behavior, and refining it until the robot safety guardrails give way. It is tested in white-box, gray-box, and black-box settings, meaning with full, partial, or no knowledge of the target, across three robot platforms including a deployed commercial system. The attack does not corrupt a sensor here; it corrupts the instruction channel, the natural-language input that the robot treats as ground truth. The output is a real physical action the robot was designed to refuse.
Why it matters
With attack success rates reaching 100 percent, a fooled interpretation of a sentence becomes a committed maneuver: the robot does not merely say the wrong thing, it does the wrong thing in the world.
ROBOTICS'25On the Vulnerability of LLM/VLM-Controlled Robotics
Problem
Robots steered by language and vision models react to the raw instruction text and the raw camera view, and both can wobble in ways no attacker even has to engineer. Small, natural variations in how a task is phrased or how a scene looks can flip a successful run into a failure, which for a physical robot means a dropped, mishandled, or unsafe execution.
Approach
The authors systematically perturb the two input channels a robot actually depends on, the instruction and the visual input, using controlled variations across several manipulation tasks. They pair these empirical probes with a formal mathematical framework that explains the failures as a misalignment between what the model intended and what the perturbed input pushed it toward. This exposes the attack surface as the sensor and the command themselves, not some internal digital layer. The failures show up as tasks the robot can no longer complete.
Why it matters
A simple perturbation drops the success rate by up to 22.2 percent, so a slightly altered percept or instruction becomes a slightly wrong action, and in manipulation a slightly wrong action is a missed grasp or a knocked-over object.
ROBOTICS'25Gradient-Based Adversarial Attacks on Deep LiDAR Odometry
Problem
Deep LiDAR odometry networks estimate how a vehicle is moving by comparing one 3D scan to the next, and that estimate can be knocked off by carefully crafted points added to the scans. This physical attack surface, spread across consecutive frames rather than a single image, had not been explored.
Approach
The attack shapes a small cluster of adversarial points constrained to look like a plausible real object, such as a traffic cone sitting on the road. Using gradient-based optimization, those points are tuned to maximally degrade the estimated motion across consecutive LiDAR frames, and they are rendered to mimic how a real LiDAR would actually measure them. So the poison is not an abstract perturbation but something that could physically stand in the sensor field of view. Tested on KITTI and Waymo, it drives the estimated pose steadily off course.
Why it matters
Because odometry is what tells the vehicle how far and which way it has moved, a fooled scan becomes a wrong belief about its own trajectory, and every downstream steering and speed decision inherits that drift.

6 · Know when perception is unreliable

A robot has no way to check a ground-truth label at runtime — when its camera or classifier hands back an answer, nothing tells it whether that answer is right. The dangerous failures are the silent ones: the perception model confidently reports a clear road or an empty cell for an input it has never really seen, and the robot acts on it. So the first job of robustness here is self-awareness — the system must estimate its own competence, calibrate how much to trust each percept, and flag when the input has drifted out of the distribution it was trained on. When that competence score drops, the robot falls back to a safe action instead of trusting a possibly-hallucinated reading — a detect-and-fall-back reflex that plain computer vision, which just returns its best guess, almost never needs. The second job is a certified safety envelope: a control-level filter that provably keeps the action inside safe bounds even while the sensors are noisy, corrupted, or actively attacked. Together these two layers — estimate when you cannot be trusted, and wrap a provable guarantee around what you do anyway — turn robustness from an accuracy metric into a runtime safety layer.
ROBOTICS'25Competency-Aware Planning for Probabilistically Safe Navigation Under Perception Uncertainty
Problem
A ground vehicle that steers using a learned perception model can fail silently: on an unfamiliar, out-of-distribution scene the model still returns a confident answer, so the robot drives straight into an obstacle it never flagged. At runtime there is no label telling the robot whether the current image is one it actually understands.
Approach
The method, called PaRCE, gives the perception model a way to score how familiar an input is by asking how well it can reconstruct that input from what it has learned — a probabilistic, reconstruction-based competence estimate. Crucially it does this not just for the whole image but region by region, so it can separate the familiar parts of a scene from the unfamiliar patch that contains a strange obstacle. This competence score is then fed directly into the planner and controller, which adjust the robot's behavior to keep the probability of a misclassification-driven mistake low. In effect the robot slows or steers away exactly where its own perception says I have not seen this before. The result is measurably fewer collisions with unfamiliar obstacles than planners that ignore uncertainty.
Why it matters
This is robustness-as-safety-layer in its purest form: the robot cannot verify its perception at runtime, so it estimates its own competence and lets that estimate, not blind trust in the classifier, drive the safe action.
ROBOTICS'25OCCUQ: Exploring Efficient Uncertainty Quantification for 3D Occupancy Prediction
Problem
Self-driving cars build a 3D occupancy map — which cells of space are filled — from camera input, but these models do not say how much to trust that map. When a camera is fogged, a sensor drops out, or an input is adversarially corrupted, the model still fills in the grid confidently, and nothing warns the planner that the percept is unreliable.
Approach
OCCUQ adds an efficient epistemic uncertainty estimate — the kind of uncertainty that comes from seeing an input unlike anything in training — directly to the 3D occupancy predictor. Instead of the expensive route of running many model copies (Deep Ensembles) or many random dropouts (MC-Dropout), it uses a single lightweight estimator that dynamically calibrates the model's confidence per scene and per region. It is stress-tested against realistic camera corruptions, including the case where just one of several cameras is degraded, so the uncertainty must localize to the affected part of the scene. This lets the system both detect out-of-distribution inputs and report calibrated confidence, outperforming the heavier ensemble and dropout baselines.
Why it matters
A calibrated I am not sure here signal is what lets the safety layer act — the planner can treat low-confidence occupancy cells as unknown-and-dangerous rather than trusting a percept that may have been corrupted.
ROBOTICS'25Secure Safety Filter: Towards Safe Flight Control under Sensor Attacks
Problem
A drone's autopilot trusts its sensors, so an attacker who tampers with those sensor readings can steer the flight controller into an unsafe state. Standard safety filters that are supposed to catch this assume the system is linear and the measurements are clean — neither of which holds under a real attack.
Approach
This work builds a secure safety filter as two stacked pieces. First, a secure state reconstructor takes the (possibly attacked, possibly noisy) sensor stream and produces the set of states that are actually plausible given bounded measurement error, so a spoofed reading cannot masquerade as the true state. Second, a control barrier function (CBF) safety filter — extended to the drone's real nonlinear dynamics via a reduced-order model — sits between the pilot command and the motors and minimally edits any command that would leave the certified safe set. Because the CBF reasons over the plausible-state set rather than a single trusted reading, the guarantee holds even while the sensors are compromised. The whole filter is modular and was validated on real flight hardware.
Why it matters
This is the certified-envelope half of robustness-as-safety: perception may be actively attacked, yet the filter provably keeps every commanded action inside the safe set no matter what the corrupted sensors report.
ROBOTICS'25Safe Decentralized Multi-Agent Control using Black-Box Predictors, Conformal Decision Policies, and Control Barrier Functions
Problem
Robots sharing a space must predict where the others will go, but those predictors are uncertain black boxes, and their errors are not free — a wrong prediction can push a safety controller into a collision. A CBF-based controller that trusts the raw prediction has no runtime label telling it how far off that prediction actually is.
Approach
The method uses conformal decision theory to make the safety margin adapt to how well the predictor has been doing. As each robot observes its actual prediction errors over time, it tightens or loosens the restrictiveness of its CBF safety constraint accordingly — trusting a good predictor to move efficiently, and pulling back hard when predictions have been unreliable. This is fully decentralized, so each agent adjusts its own margin from its own observed errors without a central coordinator. The scheme comes with a theoretical guarantee: the average amount by which the safety constraint is ever softened is provably bounded. It was validated on real human-motion data from the Stanford Drone Dataset.
Why it matters
Rather than trusting a percept that has no runtime ground truth, the robot measures how wrong its predictions keep being and lets that error rate size a provably-bounded safety margin — uncertainty converted directly into a certified safe action.

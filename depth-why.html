3D from a flat image — recovering the depth a photo throws away

A camera flattens the world: it records where light lands, not how far it travelled. Getting the third dimension back — how far is every pixel — is one of the oldest and largest problems in vision, and by our count 3D is the single biggest cluster at CVPR 2026. This page is the plain story, built on a real run: why one image can never tell you the true size of the world, why it can still tell you its shape, how a second viewpoint puts the size back, and why a robot cares about the difference far more than a photographer does.

1 · The problem — a photo throws a dimension away

Point a camera at the world and every ray of light that reaches it is stamped with where it landed on the sensor — but not how far it came. All the points along a single ray, near and far, pile onto the same pixel. So a photograph is a shadow of a 3D scene with one dimension deleted, and undoing that is guessing which of infinitely many scenes cast this particular shadow.

The problem, stated once: from flat images, recover the depth of every point — ideally in real, absolute units — knowing that infinitely many different 3D worlds produce the exact same picture.

The rest of this page is three facts about that guess, each one measured on a tiny synthetic world so you can see it rather than take it on faith.

2 · One image cannot fix the scale

Scenes A and B are genuinely different in 3D — B is every point scaled 2.4× away from the camera. Yet the two photographs on the right are pixel-for-pixel identical: we measured the largest difference between them at 0.0000. Move a thing twice as far and make it twice as big and it lands on exactly the same pixels. No amount of cleverness applied to a single image can tell A from B — the absolute size of the world is simply not in the picture.

Fact 1 — one image cannot fix scale. This is why every single-image depth method gives relative depth (this is nearer than that) and never true metres, unless it is handed a scale from somewhere else. It is a fact about geometry, not a shortcoming of any particular network.

3 · But the shape survives

Now the good news. Scale is gone, but the relief of the scene — its bumps and hollows, what is in front of what — is richly encoded in shading, texture gradients, occlusion and familiar sizes. A network can learn to read it:

Warm = near, cool = far. The net was trained on 500 random little scenes with a scale-invariant loss — it is told not to care about absolute size (because, by Fact 1, it can't know it). It learns the shape: which parts stick out, which recede.

On held-out scenes the predicted depth tracks the truth closely in ordering — rank correlation 0.96 (1.0 would be perfect) — and once you allow it the single global scale it cannot know, its error is only 4% (100% of pixels within 25% of the true depth). The shape is there; the one missing number is the scale.

Fact 2 — shape survives. A single image is enough to recover the relief of a scene remarkably well. This is exactly what the big monocular-depth foundation models do (Depth Anything and kin): gorgeous relative depth from one photo — and a separate, honest step to pin the scale.

4 · A second viewpoint puts the scale back

If one image can't give absolute depth, the fix is not a cleverer network — it is a second point of view:

Now give the method two photos from slightly apart. A near point jumps sideways between them; a far point barely moves. That shift — the disparity — is a direct, absolute ruler on depth: matching each patch across the pair (no ground truth used) and applying one line of geometry recovers metric depth to 16%, with the right absolute scale that no single image could supply.

depth = focal × baseline / disparity     # the wider the camera gap (baseline), the more parallax, the better the depth
Fact 3 — a second viewpoint restores scale. Parallax is where the missing dimension comes back. Two eyes, two cameras, or one camera that moves a known amount all do it — the baseline is the ruler. This is the backbone of stereo rigs, multi-view stereo, and a robot's structure-from-motion.

5 · Why a robot cares more than a photographer

For a photographer or a graphics artist, relative depth is often plenty — a pretty depth map to blur a background or insert an object. A robot has no such luxury. To grasp a cup or avoid a wall it needs depth in real metres, computed right now, on its own hardware — and a mistake is not a worse photo, it is a collision or a missed grasp.

Same problem, higher stakes. This is why robotics leans on the scale-restoring tricks — stereo rigs, depth sensors, and visual-inertial odometry where the IMU supplies the metric scale a lone camera lacks — and runs them in a tight real-time loop. Vision often stops at relative depth; a robot has to close the gap to metric. The in-the-wild page walks both sides.

Start here: the plain version

The course is not a pile of separate tricks. Each topic asks a simple question about information: what should be kept, what should be ignored, what should be matched, what should stay connected, what should move, and what should happen next. The equations are useful because they make those everyday questions precise enough for a machine to learn from examples.

Topology is the recurring shape question underneath the whole course. It asks what stays connected, what surrounds what, where the holes and boundaries are, and which paths are possible. That same question appears in 3D scenes, robot motion, graphs, segmentation masks, video tracks, and the hidden surface where real data lives. The topic pages now open with a plain-English essay that explains each idea from this bigger picture before the math begins.

The reason this matters is practical. A model that ignores shape may draw a road that does not connect, segment a medical scan with the wrong boundary, plan a robot path through a blocked space, or trust an answer outside the data it knows. First principles are not decoration; they are the guardrails that say what kind of mistake the method is trying to avoid.

The recurring primitives

These 22 mathematical ideas surface again and again. Once you can see them, a new paper is often a known primitive pointed at a new problem.

Softmax — turning pick one into something you can differentiate

softmax(z)_i = exp(z_i) / Σ_j exp(z_j)
Choice is discrete, but learning needs gradients. Softmax turns a hard pick into positive weights that sum to one, so attention, routing, matching, and distillation can all train through selection.

Reshape a Gaussian into your data — transport between distributions

noise → map / flow / reverse walk → data
Generators often start with an easy density and move its mass onto the data. Flows, ODEs, diffusion, energy models, adversarial games, and code-space autoencoders differ in how they write the transport, not in the underlying ambition.

Aggregate many tries — spend samples to beat one guess

answer = vote / best-of-N over K samples
If the truth is findable in a model distribution and the aggregator is honest, repeated samples concentrate signal. If the verifier is wrong, more compute amplifies the wrong thing.

Low rank and compression — the useful signal lives in few dimensions

ΔW = B·A with rank r ≪ d
The measured object can be huge while the useful degrees of freedom are small: an adapter update, a latent state, a codebook, the leading stretch-directions of a table, or the representation that survives a bottleneck.

Matching — for sets and pairs, the loss must respect correspondence

loss = min_σ Σ_i cost(pred_i, target_σ(i))
When outputs are unordered or paired, learning starts by deciding what corresponds to what. DETR, CLIP, slots, flow matching, and optimal transport all make assignment part of the loss.

The gradient of an expectation of a black box

∇_θ E[R] = E[∇_θ log π_θ(a)·(R−b)]
A sampled reward or external score can still produce a gradient by weighting log-probabilities by advantage. This is the bridge from black-box outcomes to optimization.

The degeneracy trap — every strong objective has a trivial minimizer

shortcut scores perfectly while learning nothing
Push a loss hard enough and it finds the cheap solution: collapse, dead codes, one expert, reward hacking, over-adaptation. Most method design is the leash that forbids that answer.

Symmetry as a constraint on the function class

f(g·x) = ρ(g)·f(x)
If the world has a known symmetry, bake it into the function instead of relearning every transformed copy from data.

Underdetermination — one observation cannot pin the answer

one equation, many unknowns
Many vision problems have more possible worlds than measurements. The cure is a prior, a second view, or multiple hypotheses instead of one overconfident answer.

Differentiable rendering — put the physics in the loss

C(ray) = Σ_i T_i·α_i·c_i
When image formation is differentiable, photos themselves supervise 3D. NeRF, splatting, and depth all exploit the same projective physics.

Factorize the joint by the chain rule

p(x) = ∏_i p(x_i | x_<i)
A huge joint distribution becomes a sequence of conditionals. Generation turns into repeated prediction, with drift as the price.

Information bottleneck — keep predictive bits, discard accidental bits

min I(Z;X) − β·I(Z;Y)
A representation generalizes when it is narrow enough to drop sample-specific accidents and wide enough to keep the evidence that predicts the target.

The manifold hypothesis — data lives on a thin surface

x ∈ ℝ^D, x=f(u), u∈ℝ^d, d≪D
Images are high-dimensional measurements of a low-dimensional physical world. Learning works because real data occupies a constrained surface, not the full pixel box.

Scaling laws and emergence — smooth curves cross hard thresholds

L(N)=L∞+A·N^{-α}; capability=1[L<τ]
Scale lowers reducible error predictably. Capabilities can look sudden when a smooth curve crosses the pass-fail line of a benchmark.

A model can memorize first and discover the rule later. Regularization, symmetry, and data coverage decide which zero-loss explanation survives.

Assign blame by the chain rule — the machinery all learning runs on

∂L/∂w = product of local sensitivities, back to front
Every trainable model rests on one procedure: compute each number's share of the blame by multiplying local reactions backward. The plumbing around it — optimizers, normalization, skip connections — exists to keep that backward chain healthy across depth.

Maximum likelihood — make the data you saw most expected

θ* = argmax_θ Σ_i log p(x_i; θ)
A single principle sits under most training objectives: choose the settings that make the observed data most expected, which is the same as minimizing surprise and the same as shrinking the gap to the true odds.

Judge by similarity — a straight rule in similarity-space is a curve outside it

f(x) = Σ_i α_i · k(x, x_i)
Comparing new inputs to seen ones by a similarity gives curved decisions, rich implicit feature spaces, and — with Gaussian processes — honest uncertainty. Meaning-as-geometry embeddings are the learned version of the same move.

Bayes — predict from belief, then update on evidence

p(z|x) ∝ p(x|z)·p(z)
A prior belief times the fit to new evidence gives an updated belief. The exact update is usually intractable, so it is approximated — by an easy family, by sampling, by alternating guesses, or by a running predict-then-correct loop.
Variational inference Expectation–Maximization Sampling by random walk (MCMC) Kalman filtering & state estimation Variational autoencoders Uncertainty and calibration

Everything is waves — the frequency view

f = Σ_ω (amount of wave ω)
Signals, images, and even graphs decompose into smooth-to-sharp frequency components. Smooth is low frequency, edges and noise are high, and sliding-and-summing becomes plain multiplication — the basis of filtering, positional encodings, and graph smoothing.

The cost of description — bits, surprise, and the wasted-bit gap

H = −Σ p log p; waste = KL(true ‖ model)
Entropy is the true cost of describing data; the gap between your model and reality is measured in wasted bits. This underlies compression, the training loss itself, and the bottleneck that keeps only predictive bits.

The deepest through-line. Nearly all of it is one meta-move: take something you cannot do directly — choose discretely, sample an intractable distribution, compress a world, obey a symmetry, extrapolate from finite data — and replace it with a structure gradient descent can work with. The other half is refusing the cheap shortcut that structure invites.

Normalizing flows — reshaping a Gaussian into your data, reversibly

Most generative models can make you a sample but cannot tell you the exact probability of a given point — and the ones that give exact probabilities usually cannot sample well. A normalizing flow does both, through one disciplined idea: transform a simple Gaussian into the data distribution with a map you can run backwards. Because the map is reversible, a clean piece of calculus hands you the exact likelihood for free. This page is the plain story of that idea — the change-of-variables trick at its heart, the exact invertibility, and the price it pays — on a real run.

1 · The problem — sampling and exact probability at once

Two things you might want from a model of a distribution: draw new samples from it, and evaluate the exact probability of any given point (its likelihood). Most methods give you only one. A GAN samples but has no likelihood; an autoregressive model has a likelihood but samples slowly; diffusion samples beautifully but its exact likelihood is awkward. Can one model do both, exactly?

The problem, stated once: build a model of a distribution that can both generate samples AND report the exact probability of any point — with a likelihood you can actually compute and compare.

2 · The reframe — a reversible change of variables

The idea: start from a Gaussian and push it through an invertible map f into the data. If f is a bijection, the change-of-variables formula gives the exact density: the probability at x is the base (Gaussian) probability at f(x), corrected by how much f locally stretches or squeezes space (the Jacobian determinant). Build f from layers whose Jacobian is triangular (coupling layers: transform half the coordinates as a function of the other half) and that determinant is just a cheap product of scales. To sample, run f backwards on Gaussian noise. Exact likelihood and sampling, from the same reversible map.
log p(x) = log p_base( f(x) ) + log |det ∂f/∂x|     # base density at f(x), corrected by how much f stretches space; sample = f⁻¹(Gaussian)

It is a mathematically exact idea, and the run shows both its power and the constraint it imposes.

The held-out log-likelihood is -0.533 per point — a real, comparable number, which most generative models cannot give you.

2 · Invertibility is exact — that is the whole point

Encode every test point to noise and decode it straight back: the reconstruction error is 1.1e-06 — machine precision. The map is a genuine bijection, which is exactly what makes the change-of-variables formula (and therefore the exact likelihood) valid. Nothing is lost; the flow is a perfectly reversible re-arrangement of probability mass.

3 · The cost of invertibility — expressiveness vs depth

Invertibility is not free. Each layer can only bend the Gaussian a limited, reversible amount, so a sharp target needs many layers: log-likelihood climbs from -1.764 at 1 layer to -0.554 at 8, then saturates. This is the standing tension of flows — the invertibility that buys exact likelihood also caps how much any single layer can reshape the distribution.

4 · Why it matters — exact likelihood, and an ancestor

An exact, computable likelihood is worth a lot: it makes flows the natural tool for density estimation — which is exactly how you detect anomalies (a point with very low probability is an outlier), quantify uncertainty, and compress. And historically flows are an ancestor of the generative mainstream: relax the discrete invertible layers into a continuous-time flow and you get exactly the transport ODE behind flow matching. Diffusion and flow matching traded the exact-likelihood constraint for easier, more expressive training — but they are the same reshape-a-Gaussian idea, freed from strict invertibility.

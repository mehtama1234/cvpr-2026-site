World models — learning to dream, so you can plan

How does an agent decide what to do without trying everything for real? People imagine: we run a quick mental simulation of what would happen if we acted, and pick the move whose imagined outcome looks best. A world model gives a machine that same ability — a network that learns, from experience, to simulate its world: predict what it will see next if it takes an action. Once it has one, it can dream out futures and plan inside them. This page is the plain story of that idea — the simulator at its heart, the drift that limits it, and the planning it unlocks, all on a real run.

1 · The problem — you cannot try everything for real

To act well, an agent needs to know the consequences of its actions: if I turn the wheel now, where will the car be in two seconds? The obvious way to find out — just try it — is hopeless when the real world is slow, expensive, or dangerous, and when there are countless action sequences to consider. A chess engine can look ahead because it has the rules of chess. A robot in the world does not come with the rules of the world.

The problem, stated once: to plan, an agent must predict what will happen if it acts — but it cannot afford to find out by acting in the real world. It needs its own model of the world to try things in.

2 · The reframe — learn the simulator from experience

The idea: don't hand-code the rules of the world — learn them. Let the agent gather experience (situation, action, next situation), and train a network to predict the next situation from the current one and an action. Do it in a compact latent space: encode the observation to a small code, learn a transition that steps that code forward under an action, decode when you want to look. Now you have a simulator in the agent's head — roll it forward without touching reality (that is dreaming), and search over action sequences by imagining their outcomes (that is planning).
z = encode(obs) z' = transition(z, action) obs' = decode(z')     # dream: roll z forward under imagined actions · plan: pick the action sequence whose dream ends best

It is a powerful idea — an agent that learns to simulate its own world and then thinks by imagining. It also has one honest weakness that the run makes you feel.

2 · The honest catch — imagination drifts

One step ahead the dream is nearly perfect (0.0142 error). But each imagined step feeds the model its own slightly-wrong output as the next input, so the error compounds — by 12 steps it has grown to 0.4124, and the dreamed blob has wandered off the true path. This compounding drift is the central, unavoidable limitation of every world model, and a whole research thread exists to slow it down.

3 · Yet you can plan inside it — and it nearly matches reality

Here is the payoff. Give the agent a goal and let it plan by imagining: at each moment it dreams out hundreds of short action sequences in the model, keeps the first move of whichever dream ends closest to the goal, then replans. Compared against acting randomly, and against planning in the true simulator (an upper bound):

planning in the learned model — 98% reach goal (7.5 steps)
random actions — 43% reach goal (31.7 steps)
planning in the TRUE simulator — 100% reach goal (4.3 steps)
Planning in the imperfect dream reaches the goal 98% of the time — almost as often as planning in the perfect simulator (100%), and far above random (43%). The drift costs a little (a few extra steps), but a model good enough to imagine a few moves ahead is good enough to act well. That is the whole promise of world models: learn to dream, then plan in the dream instead of in the costly real world.

4 · Why it matters — imagination is cheap, reality is expensive

A world model turns experience into a simulator, and a simulator is free to try things in. That buys two huge wins. First, sample efficiency: an agent can learn a skill mostly in imagination, touching the real world only to keep its dream honest — priceless when real trials are slow or risky. Second, unification with generation: a modern video generator that predicts the next frame is a world model, so the same systems that dream up video are being turned into driving simulators and game engines you can steer. The standing tax is the drift you saw — which is why so much recent work is about keeping long dreams coherent.

5 · The surprise — the robot that learns in its sleep

For a robot the world model is not a curiosity — it is how you learn at all without breaking the hardware. The robot gathers a little real experience, builds a world model, and then trains its policy almost entirely inside the model, dreaming millions of practice episodes it could never afford to run for real. This is the Dreamer lineage, and it is everywhere in 2025 robotics.

Same dream, higher stakes. A vision model dreams to make a prettier video; a robot dreams to practice — and can even flag danger by noticing when reality diverges from what it imagined. Drift that is a cosmetic flaw for video is a safety question for a robot, which is why robot world models lean on privileged teachers and tight horizons. The in-the-wild page lays out both sides.

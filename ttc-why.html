Test-time compute — thinking harder without retraining

A trained model has a fixed accuracy — on the first try. But you can often do better without touching a single weight, simply by spending more compute at inference: try the question many times and aggregate, or reason through it step by step. This is the idea behind the reasoning-model era — and it has a precise shape, with real limits. This page is the plain story of it: how sampling-and-voting and best-of-N turn extra compute into extra accuracy, and exactly when they stop working, on a real run.

1 · The problem — one shot is not always enough

A model answers a hard question and gets it wrong. Retraining is expensive and may not help. Yet the model often could have gotten it right — a different sample, a bit of reasoning, would have landed the answer. The information is in there; a single greedy pass just does not always extract it. Can you spend a little more compute at inference to reliably do better, with the weights frozen?

The problem, stated once: get more accuracy out of a fixed model at inference time, without retraining — by spending compute instead of changing weights.

2 · The reframe — sample many, then aggregate

The idea: don't trust one answer — generate many and combine them. Draw a batch of candidate answers (the model is stochastic, so they differ) and aggregate. Two ways. Self-consistency: take the majority vote — if the correct answer is the most likely single outcome, more samples concentrate the vote on it. Best-of-N: score each candidate with a verifier and keep the top one — you only need one correct sample in the batch if the verifier can recognize it. Either way, extra inference compute becomes extra accuracy. Reasoning step-by-step is the same trade in another form: spend intermediate tokens to reach a better final answer.
self-consistency: answer = mode{ sample_1 … sample_K } best-of-N: answer = argmax_i verifier(sample_i)     # weights frozen; more samples / more steps = more accuracy — if the aggregation is sound

It is a genuine free lunch — until it is not. The run shows both the gain and the ceiling.

3 · Seen on a real run — the scaling curves

1 · Sample many, vote — self-consistency

We built a weak solver: on each question it picks the right answer only 42% of the time (there is even an attractive wrong answer to trip it up). One shot gets 41%. But ask it the same question many times and take the majority answer:

Accuracy climbs from 41% to 92% at 64 samples. Why? Because the correct answer is the single most likely one, so across many tries it accumulates votes while the wrong answers scatter and cancel — the law of large numbers, turned into accuracy. Pure inference compute, no retraining.

2 · Or pick the best — best-of-N with a verifier

Instead of voting, draw N answers and keep the one a verifier scores highest. With a perfect verifier you only need ONE correct sample among N, so accuracy rockets to 100% by N=16. But swap in a noisy verifier and it lags the whole way — it increasingly picks confident-but-wrong answers.

The honest lesson: compute buys accuracy only if the aggregation is sound. Voting works when the right answer is the mode; best-of-N works when the verifier is good. A noisy verifier caps the gain (and, pushed harder, degrades it) — the very same failure as reward hacking in reinforcement learning. There is no free lunch in just thinking longer.

4 · Why it matters — the reasoning-model era

This single idea — spend inference compute to think harder — is the engine of the current wave of reasoning models, and it is spreading fast through vision: multimodal models that sample-and-vote, that lay out a visual chain-of-thought before answering, that draw several plans and verify. It gives a new axis to improve on — compute at inference, not just parameters or data — and it comes with the honest caveats you saw: diminishing returns, over-thinking that hurts, and a hard dependence on the quality of the vote or the verifier.

Mixture-of-experts — the capacity of a giant at the cost of a small model

The biggest models — the ones behind the best language and multimodal systems — are mostly not one dense network. They are a crowd of smaller experts with a tiny router that wakes only a few of them for each input. This page is the plain story of why that trick works, built on a real run where you can watch the experts specialize — and watch the one failure that haunts every mixture-of-experts: a router that lets most of its experts quietly die.

1 · The problem — a dense model spends everything on everything

A normal neural network runs its entire self on every input. To make it know more, you make it bigger — but then every input, however simple, pays for all those extra parameters. You want a model that holds a huge amount of specialized knowledge yet only spends a little of it on any one input. A dense network cannot do both: capacity and cost rise together.

The problem, stated once: grow a model's knowledge without growing what it costs to run on each input — hold a lot, but spend only a little per example.

2 · The reframe — many experts, a router, a few woken per input

The idea: replace one big layer with many small expert layers and a router that picks a few. Instead of one fat feed-forward block, keep a set of smaller ones — the experts. Add a tiny router that looks at each input and picks the top one or two experts to handle it; the rest stay asleep. The model now holds the combined knowledge of all the experts, but any single input only wakes a couple, so the compute per input barely moves. Capacity goes up; cost stays flat.
output = router picks top-k experts, then blends only those     # 8 experts held, 1-2 woken per input — a big brain on a small bill

It only pays off if two things hold: the experts must actually specialize (otherwise you have eight copies of the same thing), and the router must use all of them (otherwise you paid for eight and speak through two). The run shows both — and shows what happens when the second one fails.

3 · Seen on a real run — specialization, and collapse

1 · Left alone, the router collapses

A gentle push to spread the load revives the dead experts — all 8/8 are used and the effective count rises to 7.35. The capacity you paid for is finally doing work.
Expert collapse is MoE's signature failure — the exact mirror of codebook collapse in a tokenizer. Buy eight experts, and without care the model quietly speaks through two. Much of MoE research is, at bottom, different ways of keeping every expert alive and used.

4 · Why it matters — the engine under the biggest models

Mixture-of-experts is how modern frontier models get enormous without getting enormously expensive to run — route each token to a few experts and the bill stays flat while the knowledge grows. In vision it has become something subtler than a scaling trick: a way to hand different experts different jobs — one modality, one degradation type, one task — so a single unified model stops fighting itself. The recurring enemy it is deployed against is interference between sub-problems that a shared network would blur together.

5 · The surprise — one body, many jobs that fight

In vision, MoE buys cheap capacity. In robotics it solves a different problem: one robot has to walk on stairs and gravel, pick and pour, merge and park — jobs that actively fight each other when crammed into one dense network, because the gradient that improves one un-trains another.

Experts as peacekeepers. Give each skill, terrain, or scenario its own expert and route to the right one, and they stop cannibalizing each other. The win is not a cheaper benchmark — it is a robot that can switch skills at all, instead of face-planting when the ground changes. Vision uses MoE to grow the brain without growing the bill; robotics uses it to keep a body's many jobs from destroying each other. The in-the-wild page lays out both sides.

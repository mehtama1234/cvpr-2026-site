Test-time compute — why it works, mathematically

Spend inference compute to make a fixed model more accurate without retraining

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

Test-time compute means spending extra thinking after the input arrives. The model may sample many answers, check them, refine them, or choose the best one.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology appears in the search space. Extra compute explores nearby possible answers or action paths. Good search should move through meaningful neighbors, not random guesses.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

It appears in reasoning, coding, theorem proving, image understanding, planning, and agent behavior.

Why Test-time compute is worth learning

It matters because some problems are not solved by one quick guess. But extra thinking only helps when the checker is honest.

The problem, as a quantity

A trained model is fixed: its weights do not change. Normally it produces one answer per query. But inference time is flexible — you can run the model many times, draw multiple candidates, and pick wisely. The question is sharp: does the model's own distribution encode the truth? If the correct answer is actually likely under the model, then sampling many times and aggregating will concentrate probability on it by the law of large numbers (as you draw more samples, the frequency of correct answers approaches the true probability). But if the model confidently favors a wrong answer, or if your aggregation method is broken, then spending more compute amplifies the error instead. The real problem has three edges: make each sample cheap enough that you can afford many, make sure the aggregation is honest (the true answer must be findable in the samples), and diagnose when compute is helping versus when it is cornering you further into a mistake.

The core ideas — and why they work

Self-consistency: the law of large numbers concentrates on the mode

ŷ = argmax_y # {i : y_i = y}, y_i ~ model(·|x), i = 1…K
If the correct answer is the single most likely outcome under the model's distribution (the peak of probabilities), then each of K samples lands on that mode with probability p > 0.5. The fraction of samples at the mode converges to p as K → ∞ (the law of large numbers: as you draw more and more samples, the fraction staying close to the true probability grows toward 1). Wrong answers scatter randomly across low-probability tails (rare, unlikely regions); each tail gets a smaller, more fragmented share of votes. The right answer accumulates. Accuracy → 1 as you draw more samples. But this rests on a premise: the mode of the model's distribution must be the correct answer. If a wrong answer is more likely than the right one — if the model's own peak is wrong — then majority vote amplifies the error. As K grows, you draw more samples of the same mistake, accuracy does not improve, and may collapse further.

Verify-and-rerank: exponential scaling when the verifier is sound

ŷ = argmax_{y_i} V(y_i, x), V : (answer, input) → ℝ, y_i ~ model(·|x)
With a perfect verifier — one that always ranks the correct answer highest — you only need one correct sample to appear anywhere in N tries. If the model generates the right answer with probability p per sample, the probability that at least one of N samples is right is 1 − (1−p)^N. This scales exponentially fast toward 1 — a few correct samples compound into near certainty. But the real world has noisy verifiers: a verifier learns on training data, sees only limited views of correctness, and often mistakes confidence (how certain the model is) for correctness (whether the answer is actually right). A noisy verifier assigns high scores to answers the model generated confidently (even if wrong) and low scores to uncertain-but-right answers. As N grows, you draw more samples, and the verifier increasingly selects the confident errors. Accuracy plateaus below the oracle (the best you could do with a perfect verifier) and can even degrade — you are trading inference compute for worse accuracy.

Scaling law: diminishing returns and the oracle ceiling

accuracy(K) ≤ oracle(V), d(accuracy)/dK > 0 but decreasing as K → ∞
Each additional sample costs compute, and its marginal benefit shrinks. For self-consistency, once the correct answer is the clear mode (most likely result), extra samples add confirmation but no new insight — you are re-voting the same majority. For verify-and-rerank, each new sample has to beat the highest-scoring sample so far; as you draw more, the bar gets higher, and marginal improvement becomes logarithmic (grows slower and slower). The accuracy curve is bounded above by the oracle — the best you can do if the verifier were perfect or the model's mode were always right. Reaching that oracle requires the foundation to be sound: the true answer must be findable in the samples (not blocked by low model probability), and the aggregation must be honest (not gaming a spurious metric). The practical scaling depends entirely on whether the foundation holds. A flawed voting scheme or a broken verifier does not benefit from more samples — it hits a ceiling fast or crashes backward.

Over-thinking hurts: the honest failure mode

bad_aggregation + increased_samples = amplified_error, lim_{K → ∞} accuracy → wrong_answer
If the model's distribution does not have the correct answer at its mode, or if the verifier is miscalibrated (measuring the wrong thing), then spending more compute on aggregation does not help — it backfires. Self-consistency on a model that confidently prefers the wrong answer will converge to the wrong answer as K → ∞ (as you draw more and more samples, you get locked into that wrong answer). A noisy verifier trained on a distribution shift (different data patterns than what it sees at test time) learns spurious signals (e.g., the model's own confidence, not true correctness) and selects increasingly wrong samples as you draw more. These are not failures of test-time compute itself — they are failures of the premise. The lesson is stark: test-time scaling only works when the foundation is honest. The model must put non-negligible mass on the right answer (assign it meaningful probability), and the verifier must actually measure correctness, not a correlated signal. Spending more compute on a broken foundation is like chasing a wrong objective harder — you optimize in the wrong direction.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Self-consistency draws multiple samples and votes by majority, leaning on the law of large numbers (as you draw more samples, the fraction staying near the true probability grows) to concentrate on the mode (the most likely outcome). Verify-and-rerank (best-of-N with a learned or heuristic verifier — a judge that scores candidates) selects the highest-scoring sample — exponentially fast scaling if the verifier is sound, but degrading if it is noisy or calibrated to the wrong signal. Visual chain-of-thought makes intermediate reasoning visible (e.g., step-by-step equations or concept choices), allowing verifiers to grade sub-steps, not just the final answer — a richer, finer-grained signal for aggregation. Adaptive reasoning routes compute to hard examples: if the model is already confident (high entropy — high uncertainty or spread-out beliefs), skip verification; if uncertain, run deeper verification or draw more samples. Test-time tuning or training-free adaptation refines the model on a small in-context batch (a few examples shown at test time without retraining) at inference, tightening the distribution without retraining. All of these dial the same knobs: cheaper samples (so you can afford many), sound aggregation (so the true answer is findable and rises to the top), and diagnostics (so you know when the foundation is broken). The honest failure: over-thinking. If the model favors the wrong answer or the verifier is gamed, more compute locks you in tighter, a miniature of optimization where you optimize a broken metric harder and fall further from the true goal.

A worked numeric example

If one sample has a 0.4 chance of being correct and a perfect verifier can recognize it, best-of-8 succeeds with probability 1-(1-0.4)^8≈0.983. If the verifier rewards confidence instead of truth, those extra samples can select a confident wrong answer instead.

1Sample and vote: self-consistency and multi-sample reasoning
Sample K answers, take the mode: answer = mode{y_1..y_K}. Accuracy → 1 as K grows if the correct answer is the single most likely outcome.
The law of large numbers says that if the model's distribution peaks at the right answer, drawing K independent samples concentrates probability mass on that peak. Wrong answers scatter randomly across distractors and cancel out; the right answer accumulates. But this ONLY works if the mode IS correct: if the model favors a wrong answer, majority vote amplifies it.
This group shows how drawing many samples and picking the most common answer helps models find the right output. The key insight: if the correct answer is the one the model is most likely to produce, drawing more samples and voting by majority will concentrate on that answer. This works because rare, wrong answers stay scattered while the true answer accumulates votes.

2Visual chain-of-thought: thinking with images before answering
Cycle back to the image during reasoning: interleave visual grounding steps. Each reasoning unit validates via textual logic; visual information is injected dynamically, not computed from static embeddings once.
Intermediate visual reasoning binds saliency (where the model's attention lands) to semantics (what it says), enforcing faithfulness (making sure what it says matches where it looks). By examining the image repeatedly rather than reasoning from a frozen embedding (a one-time snapshot), models escape the trap of pure language priors (defaults from text patterns) and maintain fine-grained spatial grounding (detailed understanding of where things are) throughout the chain.
This group shows how models can reason better by repeatedly looking at the image during their thinking process, not just at the start. Instead of summarizing the image once and then reasoning from that summary, models check the image at each step. This keeps their reasoning anchored to what they actually see.

3Adaptive and efficient reasoning: spend compute where it helps
Allocate reasoning depth and frame budget per sample: depth = f(task_complexity); frames = policy(query_similarity + task_importance). Skip reasoning for confident predictions; spend extra compute only on uncertain or structurally complex cases.
Unbounded reasoning chains (unlimited step-by-step thinking) degrade perception (make visual understanding worse); uniform sampling (treating all inputs the same) wastes compute on irrelevant frames. The insight is surgical: identify where reasoning actually helps—per-token (per word) and per-sample (per input)—then allocate compute there. Stopping over-thinking is sometimes better than thinking harder everywhere.
This group shows how to avoid wasting compute on easy examples while spending extra thinking on hard ones. Instead of treating all inputs the same, these methods check how confident the model is or how complex the task is, then decide how much reasoning to do. The payoff: better answers without always thinking harder.

4Verify and rerank: best-of-N with a critic
Sample N candidates, score each with a verifier: answer = argmax_k V(y_k). With a perfect verifier, accuracy = 1 − (1−p)^N, scaling exponentially fast in N.
A sound verifier identifies the correct answer reliably, so you only need ONE correct sample somewhere in N tries. But a noisy verifier assigns high scores to confident-but-wrong answers and low scores to uncertain-but-right ones. As N grows, you draw more samples, and the verifier increasingly selects errors—the same failure mode as reward hacking (optimizing the wrong objective) in reinforcement learning.
This group shows how sampling many candidate answers and ranking them with a trained judge can concentrate probability on the right answer. The key is that a perfect judge only needs one correct answer somewhere in the pool; the exponential math says probability of finding it grows very fast. The catch: if the judge is wrong about what makes an answer good, more samples make things worse.

5Training-free scaling: capability added purely at inference
Iterative refinement at test time: refine logits, crop regions, backprop-guide selection, or adapt via per-sample feedback—all without updating model weights. Cost: extra forward passes per input.
Test-time compute unlocks capability without retraining by trading latency (time) for accuracy. On frozen weights (fixed, unchanging model), models can escape distribution shift (data that looks different than training), adapt to fine-grained tasks, or refine sparse-feedback control (learning from few corrective signals) by working harder on each test input via uncertainty-guided augmentation (adding variations strategically when unsure) or gradient-based selection (using math to point toward good answers).
This group shows how models can improve without retraining, by simply working harder at test time. Methods adjust how they process each input, correct their choices, or adapt to what they see. No weight updates—just smarter inference-time strategies that cost extra computation per input.

The through-line. A fixed model has a probability distribution over answers (assigns probabilities to different possible outputs). Inference-time compute — drawing many samples and aggregating them — can amplify the true signal if the correct answer is findable in the distribution and the aggregation is sound (honest, not gaming a spurious metric). Majority vote works when the true answer is the mode (the most likely outcome); verify-and-rerank works when the verifier is reliable and measuring correctness, not a correlated proxy (a correlated signal that looks like correctness but isn't). Both scale with compute but hit a ceiling (the oracle accuracy — the best you can theoretically do), and both fail catastrophically if the foundation is broken — a misaligned verifier (measuring the wrong thing) or a model that confidently favors the wrong answer. The scaling law is brutal and simple: accuracy rises with inference compute under honest aggregation, but hits diminishing returns, bounded by the soundness of the scheme. Spend compute wisely, or you are simply getting better at the wrong thing.

Segment Anything — cutting out any object you point at

For years, a segmentation model was a specialist: it knew a fixed list of things — car, road, person — and was blind to everything else. Segment Anything replaced that with one general skill: you point at something and it cuts out its exact shape, even a kind of object it never saw a label for. This page is the plain story of that shift, built on a real run — including the deep little problem at its heart, that a single click can mean several things at once, and the neat trick that answers it.

1 · The problem — a fixed list of things is a cage

Classic segmentation learns to label every pixel with a class from a fixed list. Train it on cars and roads and it will never help you with a coral, a surgical tool, or a part it was not told about — it cannot even represent the request. Every new kind of object means new labeled masks and retraining. What you actually want is to point at a thing — any thing — and get its exact shape back, no matter whether the model has a name for it.

The problem, stated once: cut out the precise shape of whatever a user indicates — by a click, a box, a scribble — including objects never seen in training, without naming them or retraining.

2 · The reframe — a promptable, class-agnostic cutter

The idea: don't classify pixels; answer a prompt. Feed the model an image and a prompt — a point, a box — and train it to output the mask of the object the prompt indicates. Because it is trained on a huge variety of masks and keyed on the prompt rather than on a class label, it learns the general skill of cutting out the thing you pointed at, and that skill transfers to objects it never saw. It does not tell you what the object is; it isolates its shape, and something else can name it.
image + prompt (a point) → the mask of the object under the prompt     # class-agnostic: no fixed label list; the prompt, not a category, says what to cut

But a single point is ambiguous. Click on a shirt pocket and you might mean the pocket, the shirt, or the whole person. A model that returns exactly one mask has to gamble on which. The run shows the fix.

3 · Seen on a real run — one click, three answers

We trained a small promptable segmenter on synthetic scenes of nested shapes and prompted it with a single point. Two things came out — it cuts out shapes it was never specifically shown, and it handles the ambiguity of a click by offering several masks:

→
mask 1
mask 2
mask 3

One click (the red dot) sits inside nested shapes — a small square inside a circle inside a rectangle — so it is genuinely ambiguous: the model cannot know whether you meant the little part, the middle object, or the whole thing. So, like the real Segment Anything, it returns three masks. Look at them: they are not three tries at the same answer — they are the three interpretations, one per level.

The three masks specialize; together they cover what you meant

We measured this on hundreds of clicks. When the click is ambiguous, the three masks together cover the right interpretation almost perfectly (best-of-three IoU 0.99) — but any single one of the heads is a specialist: it is sharp only when its own granularity happens to match, and off otherwise. Head one alone, for instance, scores 0.23 on clicks where its scale is the wrong answer. The heads have divided the labour of ambiguity.

ambiguous click (nested)plain click (one shape)
best of the three masks0.99 — covers the interpretation you meant0.99
any single head alone0.94 (only when its scale fits)0.23 (wrong when its scale does not)
This is why a promptable segmenter offers several masks. A single output can only ever hand you one of the things a click might mean — so the moment the answer is ambiguous, it has to guess which level you wanted. Emitting a small set of masks, each locked to a different granularity (part / whole / scene), lets you — or a downstream model — pick the one you meant. Ambiguity is not a bug to suppress; it is answered by covering it.

4 · Why it matters — vision's reusable brick

A click-in / mask-out foundation model turned out to be something nearly every other vision task snaps onto. The dominant recipe of the year is to freeze the segmenter, bolt on a light adapter or a smarter prompt, and carry it into a new domain or into video — and, just as often, to harvest its class-agnostic masks as free supervision: clean object boundaries for depth, isolated objects for 3D reconstruction, auto-labeled data for training other models. Point-at-anything became a component you build with.

5 · The surprise — in a robot, the mask is a handle

In vision, the mask is the answer — a cutout you compare to a ground-truth mask and score. In robotics it is never the answer; it is a handle. The robot cuts an object out so it can do something to it: close a gripper on exactly those pixels, keep a camera locked on it while an arm swings, drop it into a map, or flag it as an obstacle with no name.

A good mask is a successful grasp. The quality of a segment in a robot is not an overlap number — it is whether the robot could act on it. A bad mask is not a lower score; it is a dropped object, a lost target, a collision. That is exactly why robots reach for promptable, open-world segmentation: they have to act on things no benchmark ever labeled. The in-the-wild page lays out both sides.

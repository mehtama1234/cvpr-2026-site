Knowledge distillation — pouring a big model into a small one

The best model is often too big, too slow, or too power-hungry to actually deploy — on a phone, a camera, a robot. Knowledge distillation is the trick that lets a small, fast model inherit a big model's skill: instead of training the small one on plain labels, you train it to imitate the big one's answers, including the subtle shading of its second-guesses. This page is the plain story of that idea — what really transfers, why soft answers beat hard labels, and how a 64×-smaller model closes most of the gap, all on a real run.

1 · The problem — the best model is too big to use

You train a large network and it is excellent. Now you have to run it somewhere real — and it is too slow, too large, or too hungry for the device you have. Shrinking it by just training a small network from scratch loses accuracy, because a small model learning from raw labels has to rediscover everything alone. And often you do not even have many labels. Can the small model somehow benefit from the big one's hard-won knowledge?

The problem, stated once: get the accuracy of a big model out of a small, fast, deployable one — especially when true labels are scarce.

2 · The reframe — learn from the teacher's answers, not the labels

The idea: don't train the small model on the labels — train it to copy the big model. Run the big teacher and look at its full output: not just its top guess, but the whole distribution — how much it thinks a shirt looks like a t-shirt, a coat, a pullover. Train the small student to reproduce that whole distribution. Those runner-up probabilities (the dark knowledge) carry the teacher's sense of how classes relate, which a one-hot label cannot. And because the teacher can generate this signal for any image, it can label mountains of unlabelled data for the student for free.
student loss = match( student(x) , teacher(x) ) over a big pile of (mostly unlabelled) x     # copy the teacher's soft probabilities, softened by a temperature, not just its top-1 label

It is a deceptively simple move — imitate a model instead of the ground truth — and it works startlingly well. The run shows how well, and what exactly is doing the work.

3 · Seen on a real run — a 64× smaller student catches up

1 · A tiny student, taught by a big teacher

We trained a big, accurate teacher network (92% on FashionMNIST). Then a small student — 64× fewer weights (26,698 vs 1,701,130) — that is only allowed 1,000 true labels. Trained on those labels alone it manages 76%. But the teacher can label an unlimited pile of unlabelled images with rich soft predictions — so we let it soft-label 20,000 more, and trained the student to match those:

The same tiny student jumps from 76% to 87% — a +11 point gain — without a single extra true label. All of it came from the teacher's soft targets on data nobody labelled. That is the power of distillation: the teacher's knowledge, not just the labels, is what transfers.

2 · What actually transfers — the dark knowledge

3 · The softening knob — temperature

You can dial how much to soften the teacher's probabilities before the student copies them. On this task the student does best near T=1; push the temperature too high and the signal washes out toward a flat, uninformative smear. Every setting still beats the 76% scratch baseline (dashed) — the teacher helps across the board.

4 · Why it matters — big-model quality, small-model price

Distillation is how frontier quality reaches real devices: train a giant once, then distill it into something small enough to ship. The same move now powers the fast-image-generation wave — a slow, many-step diffusion model is distilled into a student that paints in a single step — and it is how one model teaches another to stop hallucinating or to forget something on purpose. Whenever you see a small model punching far above its weight, a teacher is usually behind it.

5 · The surprise — the teacher that sees what the robot can't

Robotics uses distillation with a beautiful twist. Train a teacher in simulation, where it can cheat — it knows the exact terrain, the true friction, the object's full 3D shape, the future. That privileged teacher learns a great policy. Then distill it into a student that only gets what the real robot can actually sense from its onboard cameras and joints. The student inherits the skill without ever needing the cheat sheet.

Same trick, the teacher knows more than the student can. This privileged-teacher-to-onboard- student distillation is a workhorse of sim-to-real transfer — humanoid controllers, legged locomotion, dexterous hands — precisely because the gap being bridged is not big-to-small but all-knowing-to-what-a-robot-can-feel.

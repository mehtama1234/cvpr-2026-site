Normalizing flows — why it works, mathematically

Build a reversible pipe between an easy cloud of random points and complicated real data. Run it one way to make new samples; run it the other way to read off exactly how likely any given sample is — both directions exact, no guessing.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

A normalizing flow starts with a simple random source and bends it into the data distribution using reversible steps. Because each step can be undone, the model can track how density changes.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology is central: a reversible bend cannot tear or glue space. That is powerful when the data has the same connected shape as the source, and limiting when it does not.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

Flows appear in density estimation, simulation, science, anomaly detection, compression, and generative modeling.

Why Normalizing flows is worth learning

It matters because it gives exact probability, not just samples. The price is that the transformation must stay reversible.

The problem, as a quantity

Two things you often want from a model of some data: to generate fresh realistic samples, and to say exactly how likely any particular sample is. Most methods do one of these well and only estimate the other.

A normalizing flow aims for both at once by a single trick: connect an easy starting cloud of random points — arranged in a simple bell shape, whose density you know everywhere — to the complicated data, using a smooth, fully reversible reshaping. The hard part is keeping the probabilities honest. When you reshape a cloud, you stretch some regions and squeeze others, and that changes how densely the points are packed. So the real problem is to build a reshaping powerful enough to turn a plain bell into complicated data, reversible enough to run both ways, and simple enough that you can exactly track how much it stretches the density at every point — because without that last piece the likelihoods are meaningless.

The core ideas — and why they work

Reshape an easy cloud into the data, and track how the density stretches

log p(x) = log p_base(z) - log|det ∂f/∂z|, where x = f(z) and z ~ 𝒩(0,I)
Start with an easy cloud of random points in a simple bell shape, where you know exactly how densely packed the points are everywhere. Push every point through a smooth, reversible reshaping f to make a complicated new shape — the data.

Here is the essential bookkeeping. Reshaping spreads some regions out and packs others tighter, and the number of points is conserved: if the reshaping spreads a tight clump over twice the room, the points there end up half as dense. So the density of a reshaped point is the original density divided by how much the reshaping stretched the volume right at that spot. Because the reshaping runs both ways, you can go forward — turn easy random points into new data samples — or backward — take any real point and, using how much the reshaping stretched things, read off exactly how likely it is. That 'how much the volume stretched' term is the whole subtlety, and the next idea is about computing it cheaply.

Making the stretch factor cheap to compute

Block-triangular Jacobian: det(J) = ∏ᵢ diag(J_ii), computed in O(d) instead of O(d³)
The snag is that 'how much a reshaping stretched the volume' is, for a general reshaping in many dimensions, ferociously expensive to work out — the honest calculation grows with the cube of the number of dimensions, hopeless for anything as large as an image.

The way around it is to build the reshaping in a special staged form. Split the numbers into two halves; leave the first half completely untouched; and reshape the second half using only the first half as a guide. With that structure, the total volume stretch is just a handful of simple per-number stretches multiplied together — cheap, growing only in step with the number of dimensions rather than its cube. The move is deliberate: you give up the freedom to use any reshaping, restricting to this staged kind, precisely so that its stretch factor is trivial to read off. The next idea recovers the lost power.

Stacking many gentle reshapings into one powerful one

Single layer: smooth deformation only. Stacked K layers: approximates any smooth diffeomorphism to the data.
Each staged reshaping from the last idea is, on its own, weak: because it must hold half the numbers fixed, it can only bend the cloud a little. A single such bend cannot turn a plain bell into a complicated, many-lumped data shape.

The fix is to stack many of them in a row, swapping which half is held fixed from one step to the next so that, over the whole stack, every number gets reshaped. Dozens of gentle bends composed one after another can mold the cloud into almost any smooth shape you like. And the costs stay tame: since each step is reversible with a cheap stretch factor, the whole stack is reversible with a cheap stretch factor — the total stretch is just each step's stretch multiplied together. Strength adds up through depth without the expense blowing up.

Both directions come out exact, with no approximation

Forward x = f(z): z ~ 𝒩(0,I) → sample x. Backward: given x, evaluate log p(x) directly (no approximation).
Because the pipe is fully reversible, two abilities that are usually separate become the very same thing read in opposite directions.

Run it forward — draw an easy random point and push it through — and you have generated a brand-new data sample. Run it backward — take any real point, undo the reshapings step by step, and fold in the known stretch factors — and you get the exact likelihood of that point: a precise number for how probable it is under the learned model, not an estimate. Most generative models can generate well but can only roughly score how likely a given sample is, or vice versa. A flow does both exactly, for free, because generation and scoring are literally one reversible map traversed in each direction.

An exact 'how likely' is a built-in strangeness detector

Flag x as anomalous if log p(x) < threshold learned on clean training data.
That exact likelihood is immediately useful beyond generation. Train the flow on normal, healthy data and it learns to assign high likelihood to things that look normal.

Now feed it something new. If the flow reports a very low likelihood — this point sits in a region where the learned model says almost nothing should ever be — that is a strong signal that the input is unlike anything seen in training: a defect on a production line, a diseased scan, a fraudulent transaction, all show up as improbable points. You get a principled strangeness detector for free, because 'improbable under the data' is exactly what 'anomalous' means, and unlike most models a flow can measure that improbability precisely rather than by a proxy.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Flow designs turn a few dials on the same idea.

What the staged reshaping looks like. The split-and-guide form is the classic; other forms allow a bit more bending per step while keeping the stretch factor cheap to read off. How deep the stack is. More gentle steps mean more expressive power at more cost — you buy strength with depth. Discrete steps versus a continuous flow. Instead of a fixed stack, the reshaping can be run as one smooth continuous motion, which turns the stretch bookkeeping into a simple running total. What the exactness is used for. Generation, exact likelihood scoring, compression, or flagging strange inputs — all fall out of the same reversible map. The thread never changes: conserve probability through a reversible reshaping, and restrict the reshaping's form just enough that its stretch factor stays cheap.

A worked numeric example

For a one-dimensional flow x=2z+1 with z~N(0,1), density changes by p_x(x)=p_z((x-1)/2)/2. The factor 1/2 is the Jacobian correction: stretching space lowers density exactly enough to conserve probability mass.

1The invertible generative engine: exact-likelihood modeling
log p(x) = log p_base(f^{-1}(x)) + log|det J_{f^{-1}}|. Invertibility guarantees both sampling (apply f to noise) and density scoring (evaluate the change-of-variables formula) work on the same model, with no auxiliary decoder or lower bound.
Reversible transformations preserve probability mass exactly, so the Jacobian determinant becomes a precise cost on density, not a guess. This lets flows combine exact likelihood with single-step sampling on the same model. Scaling to high-dimensional photographs requires pairing flows with factorization—dividing dimensions into predictable and remaining subsets—so the Jacobian stays triangular and cheap to compute.
These four papers all solve the same core problem: how to create images and videos by bending a simple noise distribution into the data distribution while keeping the exact probability computable. One unifies flows with factorization structure; another handles video by splitting space and time; another adds multiple bell curves to capture branching futures; and another relaxes strict reversibility for more design freedom.

2Invertibility as a feature: reversible networks
f is bijective, so f^{-1} exists and is equally tractable. Forward and reverse paths carry identical information; the architecture enforces information conservation, making reconstruction lossless and latent alignment verifiable.
Reversibility acts as a hard constraint on what the network can learn: it cannot discard information, only reshape it. This built-in bias cleans up the loss landscape and forces multi-task models to align features explicitly. In medical and robotic tasks, reversibility also means the learned transformation respects shape and smoothness—critical for alignment and real-world control where tearing or folding violates physics.

3Density is the signal: flows for anomaly detection and uncertainty
p(x) from the flow is exact and defined everywhere. Anomaly score = -log p(x); low density flags out-of-distribution points without requiring labeled negatives or a separate classifier.
Unlike reconstruction-based anomaly detection, exact probability is a principled signal: high-probability points cluster on the learned surface, low-probability points are intrinsically unusual. This works on high-dimensional photographs, point clouds, and sensor data because the flow assigns meaningful probabilities to the full input space. Uncertainty quantification follows naturally: detections in low-probability regions are harder to predict.

The through-line. The core idea is to build a reversible pipe between an easy random cloud and complicated data, honestly tracking how the reshaping stretches the density along the way. Restricting the reshaping to a staged form makes that stretch factor cheap to compute; stacking many gentle staged reshapings makes the pipe powerful; reversibility hands you both exact generation and exact likelihood as the same map read two ways; and exact likelihood doubles as a strangeness detector. Two plain ideas carry the whole method: conserve probability through a reversible reshaping so the density stays exact, and trade away some freedom in the reshaping's form in return for a stretch factor you can actually compute.

Normalizing flows — why it works, mathematically

Transform a simple density through an invertible map to capture complex data; probability mass stays constant, so the Jacobian determinant keeps exact likelihood computation tractable.

The problem, as a quantity

Generative models need to assign probabilities to data, but real distributions have complex shapes that simple Gaussians cannot capture. Explicit likelihood computation (fitting a Gaussian to images) fails because it wastes probability mass on regions where data never appears. We need a way to warp simple densities into data-shaped ones while keeping the exact log-likelihood computable, not an approximate lower bound.

The core ideas — and why they work

Invertible change of variables

The foundation: any invertible function f defines a valid transformation between densities.
If x = f(z) where z ~ Gaussian, the density at x follows from conservation of probability mass. A region in z-space gets stretched or squeezed by f, so the density must shrink or grow inversely. The exact rule is log p(x) = log p_base(z) - log|det ∂f/∂z|, where det|∂f/∂z| measures how much the local volume element is scaled. No approximation, no lower bound—this is exact because probability mass cannot vanish; it can only move or concentrate.

Triangular Jacobians via coupling layers

Invertibility plus computational efficiency through a clever structure.
Coupling layers (used in RealNVP) keep half the coordinates unchanged and transform the other half as a smooth function of the first half. This creates a block-triangular Jacobian matrix, whose determinant is just the product of diagonal elements. Computing a full Jacobian determinant naively costs O(d³) via LU decomposition; triangular structure drops this to O(d). The invertibility is automatic: swap the roles of halves and reverse the transformation to invert the layer.

Depth and expressiveness trade-off

A fundamental tension between what one layer can do and what the model needs to do.
A single invertible layer can only smoothly deform space—it cannot create sharp boundaries or isolated modes without infinite stretching. Many weak layers stacked together can approximate any diffeomorphism, so arbitrarily complex distributions become reachable with enough depth. The cost: each layer adds computation and parameters. Practitioners stack 8–32 coupling layers to capture natural image distributions; shallow flows fail on multimodal or highly non-Gaussian targets.

Sampling and likelihood are dual

Invertibility makes both directions equally tractable.
Sampling is straightforward: draw z ~ Gaussian and apply x = f(z); the invertible f ensures x is well-defined. Computing likelihood is equally direct: given x, evaluate log p(x) using the change-of-variables formula. This symmetry is unique to flows—autoregressive models are efficient samplers but slow at likelihood, diffusion models are slow at both. The price is that f must be invertible, which limits architectural flexibility compared to non-invertible generative models.

Flows as density estimators for anomaly detection

Exact likelihood enables out-of-distribution detection without classification.
In-distribution points cluster where the learned density is high; out-of-distribution points fall in low-density regions. A test point is flagged as anomalous if log p(x) falls below a threshold learned on clean training data. This works because flows assign meaningful probabilities everywhere, not just on the manifold where data lies. Contrast with reconstruction error in autoencoders, which conflates corrupted-data and out-of-distribution; exact likelihood is a cleaner signal.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Start with a standard Gaussian base distribution; compose multiple invertible transformations (coupling layers, autoregressive masks, or spline maps) to progressively reshape it toward the data. Each layer's Jacobian determinant contributes multiplicatively to the overall log-likelihood. Train by maximizing log p(x) directly; gradients flow through the determinant term via automatic differentiation. At test time, sample by running the inverse map on noise, or score points by evaluating the exact likelihood. Extend to continuous flows by parameterizing the instantaneous transformation (velocity field) and solving an ODE; this connects to flow matching and removes the need to count layers. Combine flows with autoregressive factorizations or other models to trade off computational cost and expressiveness.

The through-line. Invertible transformations preserve probability mass, so log-likelihood stays exact and computable. Triangular Jacobians make the computation fast. Stacking layers builds expressiveness at the cost of depth.

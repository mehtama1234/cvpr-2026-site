Normalizing flows — why it works, mathematically

Build a reversible map that bends a simple bell curve (Gaussian) into the shape of your actual data. Probability does not vanish or appear—it only moves and compresses. So the exact likelihood stays computable through this bending; the Jacobian determinant (a number from the map's shape) is the exact cost you pay.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

A normalizing flow starts with a simple random source and bends it into the data distribution using reversible steps. Because each step can be undone, the model can track how density changes.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology is central: a reversible bend cannot tear or glue space. That is powerful when the data has the same connected shape as the source, and limiting when it does not.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

Flows appear in density estimation, simulation, science, anomaly detection, compression, and generative modeling.

Why Normalizing flows is worth learning

It matters because it gives exact probability, not just samples. The price is that the transformation must stay reversible.

The problem, as a quantity

A generative model must say which data points are likely and which are impossible. A simple bell curve wastes probability on empty regions—places where your data never shows up—and assigns low probability to places where your data clusters tightly. Just scaling or shifting the bell curve does not fix this mismatch. We need a producer that can bend a simple curve into any data shape and measure how likely each point is exactly, not with a rough estimate. Two tensions pull against each other: you want the map to be reversible (go both directions: create fake data from noise, score any real point), but reversibility costs computation; you want it to capture complex distributions (multiple peaks, high dimensions), but layering simple maps involves trade-offs. The answer must honestly pay the cost of bending, via the Jacobian determinant—a number that measures how much a map stretches or squeezes space.

The core ideas — and why they work

Reversible change of variables—probability mass is conserved

log p(x) = log p_base(z) - log|det ∂f/∂z|, where x = f(z) and z ~ 𝒩(0,I)
Probability does not vanish or appear from thin air—it only moves and compresses. If you apply a reversible map f: z ↦ x to random noise drawn from z ~ 𝒩(0,I), then every tiny region in the noise space gets stretched or squeezed by the map. A region that shrinks must have its probability density grow to keep total probability the same. The Jacobian matrix (the grid of partial derivatives) ∂f/∂z measures that exact local stretching: its determinant is the ratio of areas (or volumes) after and before the map. So the probability density at x is the base density at z, divided by how much the map changed the volume: log p(x) = log p_base(z) - log|det ∂f/∂z|. This is not a rough guess or a lower bound—it is exact, because probability mass conserves.

Triangular Jacobians—cheap determinant via coupling structure

Block-triangular Jacobian: det(J) = ∏ᵢ diag(J_ii), computed in O(d) instead of O(d³)
Computing a full d × d Jacobian determinant costs O(d³)—very slow for high-dimensional data. Coupling layers avoid this by freezing half the coordinates and transforming the other half based on the first half. This creates a block-triangular Jacobian whose determinant is just the product of the diagonal block determinants, computed in linear time O(d). Reversibility stays automatic: to undo the transformation, you simply reverse the transformation on the second half using the (now-fixed) first half. You get speed without losing reversibility. The cost is expressiveness: triangular structure means each layer can only smoothly bend space along certain directions; one layer alone cannot create sharp boundaries.

Depth vs. expressiveness—weak layers stacked into strength

Single layer: smooth deformation only. Stacked K layers: approximates any smooth diffeomorphism to the data.
One reversible layer can only do smooth, continuous bendings of the input space—like stretching or compressing rubber. It cannot create sharp boundaries, isolated peaks, or the intricate folds that real data shows. But mathematics tells you something powerful: compose enough reversible layers and you can approximate any smooth, reversible map from the noise space to data space. So deep flows are expressive; shallow flows are not. The cost is real: each layer adds O(d) parameters and one forward and backward pass. In practice, 8–32 coupling layers capture photographs and natural images, but a 2-layer flow will fail completely on multi-peaked or highly non-Gaussian data. Depth is not optional; it is fundamental to capturing complexity.

Sampling and likelihood are symmetric—bidirectional efficiency

Forward x = f(z): z ~ 𝒩(0,I) → sample x. Backward: given x, evaluate log p(x) directly (no approximation).
Sampling from flows is fast: draw noise z and apply x = f(z); reversibility ensures x is well-defined and exactly distributed as p. Computing how likely a point is also fast: given any x, compute log p(x) via the change-of-variables formula with no guessing. This two-way speed is rare in generative models. Both directions are equally tractable. The cost is architectural: f must be reversible, which forbids many clever designs (dense attention, non-reversible pooling). You trade design flexibility for two-way efficiency.

Exact likelihood for anomaly detection—low probability flags unusual points

Flag x as anomalous if log p(x) < threshold learned on clean training data.
Normal points cluster where the learned flow assigns high probability; unusual points fall into low-probability regions. Because flows assign meaningful probabilities everywhere (not just on the surface where training data lives), you can threshold on log p(x) directly. This gives you a principled signal for the job: it measures how well the learned density model fits the point, which is exactly what you want.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Start with a simple bell curve p_base(z) = 𝒩(0,I). Compose K reversible transformations (coupling layers, autoregressive masks, spline maps that preserve monotonicity) to progressively reshape it toward the data distribution. Each layer fₖ has a computable determinant; multiply them together to get the full Jacobian log-determinant. Train by maximizing log-likelihood directly: max_θ 𝔼ₓ[log p(x)] = 𝔼ₓ[log p_base(f⁻¹(x)) + log|det ∂f/∂z|]. Gradients flow through both terms via automatic differentiation—no rough estimates needed. At test time, sample by running the generative direction x = f(z), or measure point-wise likelihood via the inverse and the Jacobian term.

A worked numeric example

For a one-dimensional flow x=2z+1 with z~N(0,1), density changes by p_x(x)=p_z((x-1)/2)/2. The factor 1/2 is the Jacobian correction: stretching space lowers density exactly enough to conserve probability mass.

1The invertible generative engine: exact-likelihood modeling
log p(x) = log p_base(f^{-1}(x)) + log|det J_{f^{-1}}|. Invertibility guarantees both sampling (apply f to noise) and density scoring (evaluate the change-of-variables formula) work on the same model, with no auxiliary decoder or lower bound.
Reversible transformations preserve probability mass exactly, so the Jacobian determinant becomes a precise cost on density, not a guess. This lets flows combine exact likelihood with single-step sampling on the same model. Scaling to high-dimensional photographs requires pairing flows with factorization—dividing dimensions into predictable and remaining subsets—so the Jacobian stays triangular and cheap to compute.

2Invertibility as a feature: reversible networks
f is bijective, so f^{-1} exists and is equally tractable. Forward and reverse paths carry identical information; the architecture enforces information conservation, making reconstruction lossless and latent alignment verifiable.
Reversibility acts as a hard constraint on what the network can learn: it cannot discard information, only reshape it. This built-in bias cleans up the loss landscape and forces multi-task models to align features explicitly. In medical and robotic tasks, reversibility also means the learned transformation respects shape and smoothness—critical for alignment and real-world control where tearing or folding violates physics.

3Density is the signal: flows for anomaly detection and uncertainty
p(x) from the flow is exact and defined everywhere. Anomaly score = -log p(x); low density flags out-of-distribution points without requiring labeled negatives or a separate classifier.
Unlike reconstruction-based anomaly detection, exact probability is a principled signal: high-probability points cluster on the learned surface, low-probability points are intrinsically unusual. This works on high-dimensional photographs, point clouds, and sensor data because the flow assigns meaningful probabilities to the full input space. Uncertainty quantification follows naturally: detections in low-probability regions are harder to predict.

The through-line. Reversible maps preserve probability mass, so likelihood never requires approximation—the Jacobian determinant is the exact cost of reversibility. Triangular structure makes that cost tractable in O(d) time instead of very slow cubic time. Depth buys expressiveness: weak layers stacked into strong ones can approximate any smooth warp from noise to data. Flows stand alone in making sampling and likelihood equally fast, and in giving you exact probabilities everywhere, enabling both creation and anomaly detection from one model. The throughline is compression and tractability: you capture complex distributions via reversible structure, and you pay only what reversibility costs.

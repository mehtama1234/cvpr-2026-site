1 · Make the blobs cheaper and sharper

A splatted scene is a cloud of fuzzy 3D blobs that you flatten onto the screen and blend front-to-back. The trouble is that the standard recipe is wasteful and fragile. It grows the cloud by a jittery gradient rule that keeps splitting blobs whenever a region looks under-detailed, so it burns through millions of Gaussians and takes hours to settle, often blurring the very edges it was trying to sharpen. Its screen math cuts corners too, so fine texture aliases and shimmers. This cluster attacks all three weak spots at once: it fixes how the blobs are born (better initialization instead of slow growth), how the optimizer grows and prunes them, and how the rasterizer sorts and blends them. And the reason any of this matters is that fast, real-time rasterization is exactly why splatting overtook NeRF, whose slow per-ray neural network made every frame crawl.
CVPR'26EDGS: Eliminating Densification for Efficient Convergence of 3DGS
Problem
Standard splatting starts from a sparse smattering of points and then slowly densifies — repeatedly splitting and nudging blobs over many rounds until detailed regions fill in. This drags out training and, because the split rule keys off a crude gradient signal, it often fails right where the scene has the finest high-frequency detail.
Approach
EDGS throws out the slow densification loop entirely and instead starts from a dense, well-placed cloud. It matches the same feature across several photos, then uses the known camera positions to triangulate where each matched point sits in 3D — basically letting the multiple views vote on real geometry. Every blob is handed a sensible starting position, color, and size from that triangulation, and all of them are created at once in parallel. With the cloud already near-correct on iteration one, the optimizer just polishes instead of hunting.
Why it matters
Splatting won over NeRF by rendering in real time instead of firing a slow neural network down every ray, but it paid for that speed with a fragile, hours-long training loop; fixing the blobs at birth reaches full quality in about 15% of the training time and ends up sharper.

CVPR'26FastGS: Training 3D Gaussian Splatting in 100 Seconds
Problem
Splatting gives gorgeous free-viewpoint renders, but training one scene still takes tens of minutes to hours. That latency kills any interactive use — you cannot capture a scene and tweak it live, or run the whole thing on a phone, if every attempt costs an hour.
Approach
FastGS squeezes the training loop from several directions at once rather than inventing one new trick. It starts from a smarter coarse guess of the scene so the optimizer begins closer to the answer, then drives it with adaptive learning rates and early stopping so it quits as soon as a region is good enough. It moves the grow-and-prune bookkeeping onto GPU-friendly operations and cuts the total number of iterations by tuning away redundant work. The net effect is finding and deleting wasted computation in the standard optimizer while leaving the final blob cloud essentially unchanged.
Why it matters
Splatting only beat NeRF because it renders instantly, so shrinking the last slow part — training — from hours to about 100 seconds (for roughly 1–2 PSNR of quality) is what finally makes it usable for live capture and rapid iteration.

CVPR'26Turbo-GS: Accelerating 3D Gaussian Fitting for High-Resolution Radiance Fields
Problem
Fitting a scene at 4K resolution can still take hours, and the usual culprit is not the pixel shading. At high resolution the real bottleneck is the rasterizer's own bookkeeping: duplicating each blob into every screen tile it touches and then sorting all those copies front-to-back becomes more expensive than the actual rendering.
Approach
Turbo-GS attacks that sort-and-duplicate bottleneck instead of micro-tuning CUDA kernels. Its main idea is dilated rendering: during training it only computes a sparse subset of the pixels for supervision, exploiting the fact that neighboring pixels inside a tile are highly redundant at high resolution, and it maps those coordinates cleanly so no GPU thread sits idle. It adds a densification signal driven by both position and appearance error to keep gradients from vanishing, plus a convergence-aware budget that balances adding new blobs against refining the ones already there.
Why it matters
Splatting overtook NeRF's slow per-ray network precisely because of its tile-based rasterizer, so relieving that rasterizer's own high-resolution bottleneck yields a 14x speedup — 4K scenes in about 13 minutes — with no loss in quality.

CVPR'26Exact-GS: Mathematically Rigorous and Accurate 3D Gaussian Splatting
Problem
To go fast, standard splatting cheats on the math. When it flattens a 3D blob onto the screen it uses a linearized, affine approximation of the projection, and it blends colors with a simplified screen-space rule — small shortcuts that add up to artifacts and lost sharpness in complex scenes.
Approach
Exact-GS replaces those approximations with the real math. It computes each blob's true per-pixel footprint on the screen rather than the linearized stand-in, so the projected shape is correct even where the fast approximation would distort it. It also does exact alpha-compositing when blending overlapping blobs front-to-back, instead of the simplified linear blend. Because the rendering now matches the true model, the optimizer is no longer fighting its own approximation error, and fine structure comes out cleaner.
Why it matters
Splatting traded NeRF's exact-but-slow per-ray integration for a fast approximate rasterizer, and this paper shows you can recover the accuracy the approximation gave away while keeping the speed that made splatting win.

2 · Predict the blobs in one shot

The standard way to build a splat scene is to start from noise and polish it against your photos, nudging every blob for thousands of steps. That works, but it takes minutes to hours per scene, and the effort is thrown away the moment you point the camera somewhere new, because nothing was learned that carries over. The papers in this theme replace that grind with a learned network that predicts the whole cloud of Gaussians in a single forward pass from just a handful of images. Because the network has seen thousands of scenes during training, it can hand you a usable 3D scene for one it has never met, in a fraction of a second instead of an afternoon. This is the fastest-moving axis in splatting right now, and the papers keep chipping away at the same weak spots: the lazy habit of emitting exactly one blob per pixel, sensitivity to shaky camera poses, and the lack of any dial to control how many blobs come out. The emerging frontier is hybrid: predict a strong first guess in one shot, then optionally refine it a little, keeping the speed of feed-forward while recovering some of the polish of full optimization.
CVPR'26iLRM: An Iterative Large 3D Reconstruction Model
Problem
Feed-forward reconstructors built on transformers choke as you add views or resolution, because every image patch attends to every other one and that cost grows quadratically. Worse, the common trick of gluing one Gaussian to every pixel spits out a flood of redundant blobs that scales with image size, so a bigger, sharper input just means a heavier, slower scene. The result is that fast, one-pass reconstruction stops being practical exactly when you want it most.
Approach
iLRM keeps the speed of a single forward pass but borrows the shape of optimization. Instead of tying the scene to the pixels, it holds a separate, compact scene representation and refines it layer by layer, letting the image patches act like gradient-style feedback that corrects the current guess at each step. This decouples the number of Gaussians from the input resolution, so the blob budget no longer explodes with picture size. It also splits the expensive all-patches-see-all-patches attention into a cheaper two-stage scheme, and re-injects high-resolution detail at every layer so the compact representation does not go blurry. The net effect is an internal loop that mimics per-scene optimization, but runs once and generalizes.
Why it matters
It is the clearest statement of the theme's frontier: rather than choosing between slow optimization and shallow one-shot prediction, it folds an optimization-like refinement inside a single feed-forward pass, getting both speed and quality on unseen scenes.

CVPR'26TokenGS: Decoupling 3D Gaussian Prediction from Pixels with Learnable Tokens
Problem
Most feed-forward splatting predicts each blob as a depth pushed out along a camera ray, which quietly assumes the camera poses are perfect and the views agree; when they do not, the model cannot fix it. And because there is one Gaussian per pixel, the blob count is chained to resolution, producing piles of overlapping, redundant primitives. Trying to clean this up with plain optimization tends to wreck the good priors the network already learned.
Approach
TokenGS cuts the blobs loose from the pixels entirely. It uses an encoder-decoder where a set of learnable Gaussian tokens cross-attends to compact image features, so the number of blobs is a design choice rather than a side effect of image size or view count. Instead of predicting depth along a ray, each token directly regresses a 3D coordinate, trained only with a self-supervised rendering loss and no depth labels, which frees it to correct noisy poses and inconsistent views. Because the scene now lives in this token space, you can refine it cheaply at test time by nudging the tokens rather than re-running a full optimization. Interestingly, this looser formulation makes scene motion and static-versus-moving structure fall out on their own.
Why it matters
It shows that once you stop pinning one blob to one pixel, you get a knob on the target blob count plus optional token-level refinement, landing squarely on the hybrid feed-forward-then-polish frontier instead of pure slow optimization.

CVPR'26EcoSplat: Efficiency-controllable Feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting from Multi-view Images
Problem
One-pass splatting normally emits one blob per pixel per view, so with many dense views you get a huge, unpredictable pile of primitives whose size depends on the scene. Earlier clean-up tricks like voxel merging or opacity thresholds give you no way to say how many blobs you actually want out, which means unpredictable memory, bandwidth, and frame rate. In practice a scene is reconstructed once on a server but rendered on phones and headsets with wildly different budgets, and nobody could hit an exact target count.
Approach
EcoSplat is trained to answer a request of the form: given these images and a target blob count K, hand back the K blobs that render best. It first trains an ordinary pixel-aligned predictor, then finetunes it to be count-aware by feeding in a learnable importance signal derived from the fraction of blobs you want to keep, and teaching it to rank every Gaussian by importance and dim the opacity of the unimportant ones. Importance is supervised with a pseudo-ground-truth mask built from where the image and surface detail are busy, so blobs on rich, detailed regions survive and blobs on flat regions get merged away. At inference you simply keep the top-K by opacity, with detail-heavy views granted a bigger share of the budget, giving a genuine dial on the number of Gaussians.
Why it matters
It reframes the feed-forward win as controllable deployment: one server-side pass produces a scene that can be streamed at any device's budget, whereas per-scene optimization gives neither the speed nor the built-in count control.

CVPR'26TokenSplat: Token-aligned 3D Gaussian Splatting for Feed-forward Pose-free Reconstruction
Problem
Feed-forward splatting usually needs accurate camera poses handed to it, or it falls back on slow iterative refinement to recover them. Pose-free attempts tangle together what the scene is and where the camera is, and the one-blob-per-pixel head again piles up overlapping Gaussians as views grow, smearing geometry and washing out color.
Approach
TokenSplat does the whole thing in one pass from unposed images, jointly guessing the cameras and the scene. It aligns matching content across views in feature space rather than pixel space, so corresponding parts of different photos are reasoned about together and the redundant duplicates are collapsed. A dual-flow decoder lets camera tokens and image tokens talk in a deliberately one-directional way, keeping pose reasoning cleanly separated from scene reconstruction while still letting each help the other. Crucially it uses a one-to-many token-to-Gaussian mapping, so the blob density is set by the tokens, not by the pixel grid, breaking the resolution-count coupling. All of this replaces the usual iterative refinement loops with a single forward pass.
Why it matters
It pushes the one-shot idea to the hardest setting, no poses at all, and still avoids per-scene optimization, using token-level prediction to decouple blob count from pixels while recovering the cameras for free.

3 · Make the scene move

A cloud of static blobs can only paint a frozen instant. The moment the world moves — a dancer, a waving hand, a passing car — that frozen cloud is wrong for every timestamp except the one it was fit to. The fix is to give each blob a 4th dimension: attach a little model of how it lives in time, whether that is a full 4D shape that also spans when, a deformation or motion field that pushes the blob to its right place at any moment, or a lifespan saying which stretch of time it is even alive for. Then a single explicit representation can be replayed at any timestamp and still render in real time, instead of storing a fresh scene per frame. The hard part is the tension in that 4th dimension: model motion too loosely and moving parts blur or drift; model it too richly and the blob count, memory, and storage explode — especially over long clips. So the real contest here is compressing 4D without losing the motion detail that made the scene worth capturing.
CVPR'26Layered 4D-Rotor Gaussian Splatting: A Compressed Representation for Long Dynamic Scenes
Problem
4D Gaussian splatting can make dynamic scenes look great, but it does not survive long videos. As the clip runs on, the number of blobs keeps ballooning, so even a clip under ten seconds can demand hundreds of megabytes and heavy GPU memory — far too much for phones or streaming.
Approach
Each blob is a genuine 4D primitive that spans both space and time, so the same representation can be sampled at any moment. The key move is to sort the blobs into layers by how long they persist and slice time into buckets, so a given instant only touches the blobs that are alive then instead of the whole soup. On top of that, the covariance (the blob's shape) is split apart and quantized separately, each layer is compressed according to its own temporal character, and a residual codebook lets huge blob populations share a small dictionary. A tuned C++/CUDA renderer keeps it playing at 500+ FPS.
Why it matters
This is the compression side of the 4D tradeoff done well: it hits 22.3x smaller storage (372.8 MB down to 16.7 MB) at 28.92 dB PSNR while rendering at 545.7 FPS, showing you can shrink long dynamic scenes without throwing away the motion.

CVPR'26MoVieS: Motion-Aware 4D Dynamic View Synthesis in One Second
Problem
Turning an ordinary single-camera video into a moving 3D scene normally means slow per-scene optimization, and depth, new views, and motion are usually solved as separate jobs. Existing methods handle look and shape but struggle to fold motion into view synthesis, often needing extra supervision or several passes.
Approach
MoVieS builds the scene as dynamic splatter pixels — one Gaussian per pixel that is allowed to deform and move over time. A transformer reads each frame, then attention combines them through three heads: a depth head placing each pixel in 3D, a splatter head giving each blob its appearance, and a motion head that predicts, conditioned on a query time, how each blob should move toward that timestamp. Because the motion is explicitly supervised rather than left to chase pixels, the whole thing runs feed-forward: no per-scene fitting, just one forward pass that already knows how the blobs travel through time.
Why it matters
By making motion a first-class prediction it reconstructs a 4D scene in about one second — orders of magnitude faster than iterative methods — and gets scene flow and moving-object segmentation for free as byproducts of view synthesis.

CVPR'26SharpTimeGS: Sharp and Stable Dynamic Gaussian Splatting via Lifespan Modulation
Problem
In existing 4D Gaussian methods, a blob's presence fades in and out over time, which forces the optimizer to spawn extra redundant blobs to keep things visible. Worse, static background and fast-moving foreground get tangled into one motion formula, so still points slowly drift and neither the static nor the dynamic parts are represented cleanly.
Approach
SharpTimeGS gives every blob a learnable lifespan — a per-Gaussian window saying exactly which stretch of time it is alive for. Instead of a soft decay, the blob holds a flat, stable opacity across its intended duration and then stops, which kills the redundant densification. That same lifespan then modulates the motion: long-lived blobs (the static world) are held nearly still to stop drift, while short-lived blobs keep full freedom to move fast, cleanly decoupling how much a blob moves from how long it exists. A lifespan-and-velocity-aware densification finally pours extra blobs into the busy moving regions while keeping still areas compact.
Why it matters
Treating lifespan as the single temporal knob that governs both visibility and motion yields sharp temporal edges and stable backgrounds at state-of-the-art quality, running up to 4K at 100 FPS on an RTX 4090.

CVPR'26MoRGS: Efficient Per-Gaussian Motion Reasoning for Streamable Dynamic 3D Scenes
Problem
When a dynamic scene is reconstructed live from a streaming camera, the blobs never learn how the scene actually moves. Trained only to match pixel colors, each blob's motion just chases whatever residual is left in the image rather than the true 3D geometry, so the motion looks plausible per frame but is physically wrong.
Approach
MoRGS adds a cheap but explicit motion cue: sparse optical flow measured on a few key views is used to regularize how the blobs move, tying their motion to real observed pixel displacement instead of appearance noise. Each blob carries a per-Gaussian motion offset field that reconciles its projected 3D motion with the observed flow across views and time. A per-Gaussian motion confidence then decides which blobs are genuinely dynamic and should update, while suppressing pointless motion in static regions — keeping the whole thing light enough to run online in real time.
Why it matters
Separating true per-Gaussian motion from appearance change with lightweight flow supervision beats prior streaming methods (3DGStream, QUEEN) on both rendering quality and motion fidelity at comparable or faster training speed.

4 · Conjure and animate

Splatting stores a scene as a cloud of explicit little blobs you can move, delete, and re-color directly, and that editable output is exactly what pairs so naturally with 2D and video diffusion models. A diffusion model already knows what plausible images and videos look like, so it can imagine the parts of a scene a single photo never showed; splatting then catches that imagination as real, movable 3D geometry rather than a flat clip. That combination attacks the oldest problem in 3D-from-a-prompt work, where too few views force the system to hallucinate wildly and inconsistently. It also lets you fold in physics and motion, so the generated blobs do not just sit there but fall, stretch, and flow like real materials. The other place this pairing pays off hardest is people, because humans are the highest-scrutiny target we have and the tiniest error reads as fake. Rigging the blobs to a body or face model, and giving each blob a richer set of dials, turns splatting into real-time, animatable, photoreal avatars that hold up under that scrutiny.
CVPR'26DIFF4SPLAT: Repurposing Video Diffusion Models for Dynamic Scene Generation
Problem
Making a moving 3D scene from a single picture has been a painful trade-off. The careful multi-stage pipelines take several GPU hours and stumble often, while the fast one-shot methods only ever hand you a flat 2D video or a frozen static scene with no real moving geometry underneath.
Approach
DIFF4SPLAT takes a video diffusion model, which already knows how believable video should unfold over time, and repurposes it to generate 3D instead of just pixels. A Video Latent Transformer reads the diffusion model's internal features and, in a single forward pass, predicts a field of deformable 3D Gaussian blobs that carry both shape and motion. It is trained on a large custom 4D dataset annotated with real-scale geometry and motion, using combined photometric, geometric, and motion supervision so the blobs land in the right place and move the right way. The upshot is one clean pass that fuses generation and 3D representation with no test-time optimization or cleanup afterward.
Why it matters
Because the diffusion model's imagination is caught as explicit deformable Gaussians rather than baked-into a flat clip, you get an actual editable moving scene, and it arrives in about 30 seconds instead of hours, a roughly 60-fold speedup.

CVPR'26PhysGM: Large Physical Gaussian Model for Feed-Forward 4D Synthesis
Problem
Adding believable physics to generated 4D content has meant slow per-scene optimization that first needs a dense multi-view 3D reconstruction and then either hand-tuned material settings or heavy distillation guidance. Worse, bolting a separate physics module onto a separate geometry module throws away the physical clues already hiding in how a surface looks.
Approach
PhysGM predicts both the 3D Gaussians and their physical material properties together, from a single image, in one feed-forward pass. A transformer reads image tokens plus camera-ray geometry, and two heads split the work: one outputs the per-pixel Gaussian blobs, the other predicts a material class along with stiffness and squishiness values (Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio). Those predicted materials feed a Material Point Method simulator that couples directly to the Gaussians, so the blobs actually fall, stretch, and flow like the material they represent. It is trained first on a new 50,000-plus-asset dataset with physics labels, then sharpened with preference learning that compares its simulations against real reference videos to favor the most realistic behavior.
Why it matters
Because physics rides on the same explicit, editable blobs, one minute of inference turns a single photo into a 4D object you can actually simulate and animate, roughly 30 times faster than the optimization baselines it beats on realism.

CVPR'26Large-scale Codec Avatars: The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Large-scale Avatar Pretraining
Problem
Photoreal 3D human avatars have been stuck in a hard trade-off. Studio-captured methods look stunning but only for the handful of people they were built from, while methods trained on messy in-the-wild footage generalize to anyone but come out low quality and rough.
Approach
This work borrows the pre-train-then-fine-tune recipe from large language models and applies it to avatars. It first pre-trains on a million in-the-wild videos to soak up broad priors about how human shape and appearance vary, then post-trains on high-end studio captures of 2,737 people shot with 200 synchronized 4K cameras to sharpen fidelity. The model tokenizes both the image and a canonical body mesh, runs them through a transformer, and decodes 3D Gaussian attributes with two decoders: one produces the static canonical blobs, the other predicts pose- and expression-dependent offsets driven by body pose, facial expression codes, and gaze. Those blobs are then posed with linear blend skinning, exactly rigging the Gaussians to the underlying body model, and splatted, with extra paths for loose clothing and relighting.
Why it matters
Because the avatar is explicit Gaussians rigged to a drivable body model, it animates in real time and can be created in seconds, and pre-training at scale finally lets one system be both photoreal and general across diverse people.

CVPR'26HyperGaussians: High-Dimensional Gaussian Splatting for High-Fidelity Animatable Face Avatars
Problem
Plain 3D Gaussian splatting renders a still face beautifully, but animatable face avatars driven from video still break down on the hard stuff. The nonlinear way skin folds, specular glints, thin structures like teeth, hair, and glasses frames, and fine high-frequency detail all slip through, leaving results stranded in the uncanny valley.
Approach
HyperGaussians fix this at the level of the primitive itself, giving each blob more dials to work with. Every Gaussian is extended from its 3 spatial dimensions to a higher-dimensional one and conditioned on a small learnable local embedding, so a single blob can express complex local deformations and view-dependent shine instead of staying rigid. An Inverse Covariance Trick keeps this cheap by reparameterizing the covariance so the method never has to invert big high-dimensional matrices. At render time only the 3D slice of each blob is splatted with ordinary alpha-blending, so it drops straight into existing face-avatar systems: it slots between the MLP and the rasterizer in methods like FlashAvatar and GaussianHeadAvatar, with the MLP predicting a latent code that the HyperGaussian turns into expressive parameters.
Why it matters
Because the richer expressivity lives inside the same explicit, editable primitives, it plugs into existing rigs with no architecture changes and sharpens eyes, teeth, wrinkles, and specular highlights while still rendering in real time at 300 FPS.

5 · Teach the blobs what they are

A raw splat cloud is a beautiful lie: millions of little colored blobs that know exactly where they are and what color they are, but have no idea what they are. The wall does not know it is a wall; the mug does not know it is a mug. To fix this, you take a 2D vision-language model — the kind that already knows the word mug matches a picture of a mug — and you distill its per-pixel meaning down into each Gaussian, so every blob carries a little tag of significance alongside its color. Now the 3D scene is open-vocabulary queryable: you can type a word it was never trained on and the matching blobs light up, because meaning is compared, not memorized. This turns a pretty reconstruction into something you can actually ask questions of — point at meaning instead of at pixels. And because the blobs are explicit and separable, once you know which ones are the mug you can grab, move, delete, or recolor exactly those. That last step is the bridge to robots, which cannot act on a color buffer — they need to act on the mug.
CVPR'26LightSplat: Fast and Memory-Efficient Open-Vocabulary 3D Scene Understanding in Five Seconds
Problem
The usual way to make Gaussians language-aware is to hang a full 512-dimensional vision-language feature vector on every single blob and then slowly optimize them, view by view, until they agree. That means minutes of waiting per scene and a huge memory bill — thousands of bytes per Gaussian, times a hundred thousand Gaussians. Worse, blending those features during rendering smears them together, so the meaning gets blurry right where you need it sharp.
Approach
LightSplat skips the optimization entirely. It runs an off-the-shelf segmenter on the input photos to cut out object masks, encodes each mask once with CLIP, and then uses each Gaussian's actual contribution to the pixels it paints (its alpha-blending weight) to decide which mask it belongs to. Instead of storing a fat feature per blob, it stores a tiny 2-byte index — a pointer into a small lookup table of mask meanings — cutting memory by roughly 64x. It then throws away masks that too few Gaussians actually support (killing floating artifacts), and builds a single graph that links masks when they overlap in 3D and mean similar things, so connected clumps become clean 3D objects. At query time your text is compared against only about a hundred cluster meanings rather than every blob, which is why it answers at 500 frames per second after just five seconds of setup.
Why it matters
Because the blobs are explicit and separable, meaning does not have to be baked in by expensive training — it can simply be injected by asking which blobs paint which pixels, and edited afterward by swapping a pointer. An implicit representation would force you to re-solve the whole field; explicit Gaussians let you attach a cheap tag and move on.

CVPR'26Uni3R: Unified 3D Reconstruction and Semantic Understanding via Generalizable Gaussian Splatting from Unposed Multi-View Images
Problem
Most pipelines treat building the 3D scene and understanding what is in it as two separate jobs, each needing its own slow per-scene optimization, and often needing to know exactly where each camera was. That split is expensive and it lets geometry and meaning drift out of agreement. It also does not generalize — point it at a brand new scene and it has to start over.
Approach
Uni3R does the whole thing in a single forward pass. A Cross-View Transformer looks at a handful of ordinary photos — no known camera poses required — and fuses them into one shared understanding of the scene. From that, it directly regresses 3D Gaussians that carry both shape and appearance and a distilled language-feature field, guided by a point-map geometry loss so the structure stays honest. Out of that one pass you get novel views, depth, and open-vocabulary segmentation at once, all consistent with each other because they came from the same set of blobs. Because it is feed-forward and trained across many scenes, it works on scenes it has never seen without any per-scene fitting.
Why it matters
When geometry and semantics live in the same explicit Gaussians, they cannot disagree — the thing you rendered and the thing you can name are literally the same blobs. That shared substrate is exactly what makes a single-pass, generalizable joint model possible.

CVPR'26REALM: An MLLM-Agent Framework for Open World 3D Reasoning Segmentation and Editing on Gaussian Splatting
Problem
Simple language queries like the red mug are one thing, but real instructions ask you to reason — the thing you would drink coffee from. Powerful 2D multimodal language models are great at that kind of reasoning but have no built-in sense of 3D space, and if you just hand them a rendered view they get thrown off by which angle you happened to pick.
Approach
REALM treats the Gaussian scene as a high-fidelity proxy that a language-model agent can drive. Each Gaussian gets an identity feature so blobs can be grouped into objects, and a segmenter fuses the language model's reasoning with an off-the-shelf mask model to turn an instruction into a 2D mask. The clever part is global-to-local grounding: the agent first surveys several viewpoints to figure out roughly where the target is, then synthesizes close-up views to pin it down precisely — sidestepping the viewpoint-sensitivity problem. Once the right blobs are identified, the same explicit handle lets it remove, replace, or restyle that object directly. It does all this without heavy 3D-specific retraining, borrowing the reasoning it needs from a frozen 2D model.
Why it matters
Because each object is a nameable, grabbable set of explicit blobs, understanding and editing collapse into one act — the instant you know which Gaussians are the mug, you can delete or swap exactly those, which is impossible when the object is dissolved into an implicit field.

CVPR'26GenSplat: Bridging the Generalization Gap in 3DGS Language Comprehension
Problem
Language-aware splatting has been stuck on a trade-off: methods that generalize across scenes only handle a fixed, predefined list of words, while methods that handle free-form natural language have to be re-fit to every new scene. The missing piece is a way to climb from low-level geometry up to the many levels at which language talks about a scene — a whole category, a specific instance, or a loose free-form description.
Approach
GenSplat learns that ladder on purpose. A Progressive Language Grounding Curriculum trains it in stages — first coarse semantic categories, then individual instances, then open free-form queries — so it never overfits to one level and keeps its cross-scene generality. An MLLM-guided reasoning module contributes semantic and spatial priors through a dedicated segmentation token, letting it handle instructions that need actual reasoning rather than keyword matching. A geometry-aware frame selector picks only the informative views, so it aligns language to blobs cheaply instead of grinding through every frame. The result is free-form language understanding that still transfers to scenes it was never optimized on.
Why it matters
Explicit Gaussians give language a concrete, multi-level thing to attach to — you can bind a word to a whole region, one object, or a fuzzy description without rebuilding the scene, which is what lets one model be both open-vocabulary and generalizable at once.

6 · A map the robot lives in

In the vision story, a splat cloud is judged by how pretty the final photo looks. A robot needs something different: a map it can build as it moves and then act inside. Gaussian SLAM grows the blob cloud live from a moving camera or LiDAR, dropping and refining Gaussians frame by frame so the robot always has dense, photoreal geometry in real time instead of waiting for an offline reconstruction. Once that cloud exists, a planner can treat it directly as a collision field: measure how much the robot blob overlaps the scene blobs and steer to keep that overlap near zero. Because the Gaussians are explicit shapes with real positions, these safety and distance checks run in microseconds on the robot itself, not in a slow neural query. And because the same cloud is photoreal, a policy can be trained against a Gaussian replica of the exact room and then dropped onto the real robot with almost no reality gap. The punchline: vision wants a prettier photo, robotics wants a map it lives in.
ROBOTICS'25Gaussian-LIC: Real-Time Photo-Realistic SLAM with Gaussian Splatting and LiDAR-Inertial-Camera Fusion
Problem
Earlier photoreal SLAM systems only worked indoors with a depth camera, and they fell apart outdoors, under changing light, or when the robot moved violently. That rules out most real robots, which drive fast through unbounded outdoor scenes.
Approach
The system grows the map live by tightly fusing three sensors as the robot moves: a LiDAR, an inertial motion sensor, and a camera. LiDAR points and triangulated visual points both seed new 3D Gaussians, so the blob cloud gets accurate metric geometry from the laser and rich color from the camera. Sky is modeled separately and camera brightness changes are compensated, so outdoor renders stay clean. The whole loop runs in real time in C++/CUDA, continuously spawning and refining Gaussians while tracking pose. The result is a photoreal map that reportedly beats competitors even when those competitors are handed ground-truth camera poses.
Why it matters
Fusing LiDAR into the splat cloud gives the robot true metric scale and survives fast motion outdoors, which a camera-only NeRF cannot deliver live. It is a photoreal map the robot builds on the move, not a reconstruction it waits for.

ROBOTICS'25Dy3DGS-SLAM: Monocular 3D Gaussian Splatting SLAM for Dynamic Environments
Problem
Live Gaussian-splat SLAM breaks when people or objects move through the scene, because moving pixels corrupt both the pose estimate and the map. Worse, prior systems all needed a depth sensor, so a plain single camera could not be used.
Approach
Dy3DGS-SLAM flags moving things by combining two cues into one probability score: how pixels flow between frames (optical flow) and how their depth changes. That score becomes a mask that hides moving objects. A special motion penalty then constrains camera-pose estimation from just a single ordinary camera, and dedicated rendering losses on the masked pixels stop transient movers from being baked into the map. The clean, static Gaussian cloud is grown live from monocular RGB alone. This makes it, per the paper, the first monocular 3DGS-SLAM that works in dynamic scenes without any depth sensor.
Why it matters
A robot in the real world shares space with moving people, so its live map must actively reject them rather than smear them into geometry. Doing it from one cheap camera, in real time, is what a static offline reconstruction can never give a moving robot.

ROBOTICS'25FOCI: Trajectory Optimization on Gaussian Splats
Problem
Once you have a Gaussian map, you still have to plan a safe path through it, and that was unsolved: prior methods wrapped everything in crude bounding boxes and ignored which way the robot was oriented. A legged robot squeezing through a gap needs to know its actual shape does not collide, not that a loose box does.
Approach
FOCI represents both the scene and the robot itself as collections of 3D Gaussians. It then writes down a math formula for how much two Gaussian clouds overlap and uses that overlap integral as a differentiable collision cost. Because the cost is smooth, a gradient-based planner can push the trajectory directly down the collision score, sliding the path away from where the robot blobs would intersect the scene blobs. The optimization runs natively on the splat map with no intermediate mesh or grid. It computes orientation-aware, collision-free paths for a real legged robot in seconds. A sibling result, Distance and Collision Probability Estimation from Gaussian Surface Models, shows the same idea can estimate crash odds and clearance in microseconds on embedded hardware.
Why it matters
The explicit Gaussians are the collision field, so the planner reasons about safety directly on the map the SLAM system already built, no slow neural query or grid rebuild. That is a map the robot acts inside, not just looks at.

ROBOTICS'25SplatSim: Zero-Shot Sim2Real Transfer of RGB Manipulation Policies Using Gaussian Splatting
Problem
A robot hand can learn to manipulate objects entirely in simulation, but policies that read raw camera images fail on the real robot, because simulated mesh renders look nothing like real photos. That visual gap wastes the whole point of training in sim.
Approach
SplatSim scans the real workspace and builds a photoreal Gaussian-splat replica of it, then swaps that splat cloud in for the physics simulator's usual mesh renderer. Now the images the policy sees during training in sim look like real camera frames of the actual scene, while the physics engine still handles contact and dynamics. Imitation-learning manipulation policies trained against these photoreal renders are dropped onto the real robot with no fine-tuning. This zero-shot transfer reaches 86.25% average real-world success, closing most of the gap to the 97.5% you get from training on real data. The companion simulator DISCOVERSE generalizes the idea into a full Real2Sim2Real platform, pairing 3DGS scene reconstruction with MuJoCo physics.
Why it matters
A Gaussian replica is photoreal enough that a vision policy cannot tell sim from real, so training transfers back with almost no reality gap, which mesh-rendered sim never manages. The robot practices inside a splat copy of its own world and then acts in the real one.

Reading it whole. Top to bottom, the themes walk the same object outward: first make the blobs cheaper and sharper, then predict them in one shot instead of optimizing, then let them move through time, then conjure and animate them from a prompt, then teach them what they are — and finally hand the whole cloud to a robot as a live map it builds and acts inside. The scenes differ wildly; the primitive — a cloud of little Gaussian blobs you splat and blend — never changes.

Uncertainty — how a model learns to doubt itself

Ask a neural network almost anything and it will answer with a confident number attached — 98% sure. The trouble is that the number is often a lie: models are overconfident when they are wrong, and worst of all, they stay confident about things they have never seen. A system that cannot say I don't know is dangerous the moment it leaves the lab. This page is the plain story of fixing that — making confidence mean what it says, and building a real signal for the unknown, all on a real run.

1 · The problem — confidence you can't trust

A classifier's output looks like a probability, so it is tempting to read it as one — 90% must mean right nine times in ten. It does not. Networks are systematically overconfident, and the failure gets dangerous at the edges: shown something outside everything it was trained on, a model does not get uncertain — it confidently picks whichever of its known answers is closest, with no hint that the question was foreign. The number tells you what it thinks; it does not tell you whether to believe it.

The problem, stated once: a model's confidence does not track its correctness — it is overconfident when wrong, and certain even about inputs it has never seen. How do you get a trustworthy sense of how much to believe an answer?

2 · The reframe — measure the doubt, don't assume it away

The idea: treat “how sure are you” as its own quantity to fix and to measure. Two moves. First, calibrate: adjust the confidence so that among all the times the model says 80%, it really is right about 80% of the time (temperature scaling is the simplest fix — one number, no retraining). Second, get a genuine uncertainty signal: don't trust one model's confidence — ask several, and treat their disagreement as the doubt. Where they agree, be confident; where they scatter, hesitate.
calibrated: P(correct | model says p) ≈ p unknown-input alarm: models disagree ⇒ uncertain     # fix the number so it means what it says; use disagreement as a real “I don't know”

Both moves are cheap, and both show up clearly on the run.

3 · Seen on a real run — overconfident, then honest

1 · Confidently wrong about things it has never seen

We trained a classifier on FashionMNIST — it knows ten kinds of clothing, and it is accurate (91%). Then we showed it handwritten digits, which it has never seen in its life. A sensible system would hesitate. This one does not — it slaps a clothing label on each digit with near-total confidence:

“Sandal”
100% sure
“Bag”
99% sure
“Sandal”
100% sure
On average it is 90% confident on inputs from a world it was never trained on. Raw softmax confidence is not a measure of how much to trust the answer — a network will happily be certain about nonsense. That is the whole problem.

2 · Fixing the number — calibration

raw network — ECE 0.018
temperature-scaled (T=1.323) — ECE 0.007
deep ensemble — ECE 0.006

First failure: even on data it does know, when the network says 90% it is right less often than that. Calibration error (ECE) measures the gap. A single free knob — temperature scaling, fit on held-out data — divides the logits by T=1.323 and cuts the error from 0.018 to 0.007 without changing a single prediction. Averaging a handful of independently trained models (a deep ensemble) does better still (0.006).

3 · A real “I don't know” — disagreement beats confidence

Second failure: can the model flag the digits as foreign? We scored how well two signals separate the in-distribution clothes from the out-of-distribution digits (higher is better; 0.5 is a coin flip):

one network's confidence → AUROC 0.60
an ensemble's disagreement → AUROC 0.79
A single network's confidence is nearly useless for spotting the unknown (0.60 — barely above a coin flip). But when several independently trained networks disagree, that disagreement is a far better alarm (0.79). The honest signal of “I have never seen this” is not one model's certainty — it is many models failing to agree.

4 · Why it matters — a number you can act on

The instant a model informs a decision — a diagnosis, a filter, a control loop — the confidence matters as much as the answer, because it tells you when to defer to a human, gather more data, or abstain. Calibrated uncertainty is what lets a system say “this one I'm sure of, that one send to review.” And OOD detection is the immune system that catches the input nobody anticipated before it becomes a silent, confident mistake. It is the difference between a demo and something you can deploy.

5 · The surprise — for a robot, doubt is a safety signal

In vision a miscalibrated score is a bad number. On a robot it is a bad action: a confidently wrong perception drives the gripper into the wrong place, the car into the wrong lane. So a robot uses uncertainty as a control input — grasp cautiously when unsure, bound the risk in a plan, and when the situation looks unfamiliar, slow down or ask a human rather than barrel ahead.

Same doubt, but now it steers the hand. Uncertainty-aware grasping, risk-bounded planning, and knowing-when-to-ask are a serious thread in robotics precisely because the cost of confident-and-wrong is physical. Vision asks the model how sure it is; a robot lets that answer change what it does. The in-the-wild page lays out both sides.

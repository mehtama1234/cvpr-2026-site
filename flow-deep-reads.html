1 · Estimating the motion field

The task sounds simple: feed in two frames of a video and, for every single pixel, draw an arrow saying where that bit of the world moved to. The winning recipe of the last few years, called RAFT, does this by building a giant lookup table of how well every patch in frame one matches every patch in frame two — an all-pairs cost volume — and then nudging its guess for the arrows over and over until they settle. It is remarkably accurate, but that all-pairs table is enormous and slow to build, which is the central tax on the whole approach. The 2026 work in this theme attacks that tax from several sides: making the cost volume dramatically cheaper by noticing almost none of it is ever used, making flow work in near-darkness and without any labelled training data, and re-framing motion not as a one-shot jump but as a smooth physical process that unfolds over time. Running underneath all of it is the same stubborn enemy: in a blank wall or along a plain straight edge, every possible match looks identical, so the arrow is genuinely unknowable there — the aperture problem — and honest methods have to admit it rather than guess. Together these papers push flow to be faster, more robust, and better grounded in what motion actually is.
CVPR'26Efficient All-Pairs Correlation Volume Sampling for Optical Flow Estimation
Problem
The accurate modern recipe for optical flow builds a table comparing every patch in one frame against every patch in the other, and the cost of that table grows with the square of the number of pixels — so it explodes on high-resolution footage. You can either pay for the whole table in memory, or compute pieces of it only when needed to save memory, but the memory-saving route is far slower. This forces most methods to shrink the images first, throwing away the fine detail that movie post-production and other high-end video work actually need.
Approach
The authors first measured what the flow network really touches, and found a startling fact: during all its refinement steps, only about 1.6% of that giant comparison table is ever looked at. So instead of building the whole thing, they store it in small blocks and decide block-by-block whether a block is worth computing at all, rather than deciding pixel-by-pixel. They rearrange the data so that patches sitting near each other in the image also sit near each other in memory, which makes the useful blocks cluster together and the wasted ones easy to skip. A single fused GPU routine then computes one strip of blocks and immediately reads from it without ever writing the intermediate table out to memory, using a lightweight vote among threads to figure out which blocks to do next. The net effect is that cost and memory grow merely in proportion to the number of pixels instead of its square.
Why it matters
This directly targets the cost-volume bottleneck that makes the accurate flow recipe unusable at full resolution — roughly a 99% cut in memory or a 92% cut in time for the sampling step — letting the same trusted method run on 8K frames instead of blurry downsized ones.

CVPR'26Optical Flow Matching: Reframing Optical Flow as Continuous Transport Dynamics
Problem
Almost every flow method treats the problem as a single discrete jump: it directly guesses where each pixel ended up in the second frame. But that captures only the destination, not the smooth journey the pixel actually took, so the estimate is detached from the fact that real-world motion follows continuous physical paths through time.
Approach
This paper re-frames flow as a continuous transport problem: rather than predicting a jump, it learns a time-varying velocity field that says how fast and in which direction each pixel is moving at every instant. Borrowing the idea of Flow Matching from generative modelling, it then integrates that velocity forward in small steps, letting each pixel coordinate flow smoothly from its position in frame one to its position in frame two — the way you would trace a moving object rather than teleport it. The tricky part is that the clean mathematical version of this idea and the practical training signal used for real flow do not line up, so the authors add a construction they call Triangle Velocities Synergy, which introduces auxiliary paths whose geometry reconciles the two. The whole thing is built on top of a familiar RAFT-style backbone, so it inherits the strong matching machinery while gaining a physically grounded notion of motion. The result is smoother, more temporally stable flow that also transfers better to datasets it was not trained on.
Why it matters
By restoring the link between velocity and displacement, it treats motion as the continuous physical process it really is rather than a disconnected snapshot, which pays off as steadier flow over time and stronger generalization across scenes.

CVPR'26FlowFM: Advancing Dark Optical Flow Estimation with Flow Matching
Problem
In near-darkness the frames are noisy and the motion cues are faint, so matching pixels between two dim images is genuinely hard. Ordinary flow networks get confused by the noise, and the generative diffusion models people tried instead are slow and tend to produce broken, discontinuous arrow fields.
Approach
FlowFM uses flow matching, a generative technique that is more robust to noise than diffusion and, crucially, can produce its answer in a single step rather than the many steps diffusion needs. During training it draws a straight line between pure noise and the true flow field and teaches the model to travel that line, so at test time it can jump from a rough initial guess straight to a clean flow field in one shot, guided by the cost volume and image context. Its key trick for the dark is to also reason in the frequency domain: a Fourier transform splits the flow into an amplitude part (roughly, how strong the motion is) and a phase part (roughly, where it is), and the model boosts the amplitude to recover motion that darkness had washed out while preserving the phase to keep the flow spatially consistent. A dual-branch encoder pulls features from the two dark frames and builds the matching table from their similarity. The frequency-enhanced result is then transformed back into an ordinary flow field.
Why it matters
It attacks the case where the images themselves barely carry the signal — low-light noise erasing the very structure needed to match points — and recovers accurate motion there far more cheaply than the multi-step generative alternatives.

CVPR'26U2Flow: Uncertainty-Aware Unsupervised Optical Flow Estimation
Problem
Training flow models without any labelled ground truth is attractive but leaves a blind spot: the model has no way to tell you which of its arrows to trust. That matters most exactly where flow is hardest — hidden (occluded) regions and blank, textureless areas — and downstream tools that build on the flow can quietly accumulate those errors into outright failures.
Approach
U2Flow is the first recurrent method to learn, without labels, both the flow and a per-pixel confidence in that flow at the same time. Its clever source of a training signal is consistency under augmentation: if you distort the input in a known way, the predicted flow should transform in the matching way, and where it fails to, the model learns to flag itself as uncertain — framed as a Laplace maximum-likelihood objective. That confidence map is then put to work rather than just displayed: it steers where the model spends its refinement effort and, importantly, it softens the smoothing in regions the model is unsure about. Concretely, in occluded or textureless spots where matching is hopeless, the confidence signal relaxes the usual assumption that neighboring pixels move alike, so a bad guess is not forced to spread to its neighbors. It also fuses forward and backward flow estimates using that same confidence to pick the more reliable direction.
Why it matters
This confronts the aperture problem head-on: in flat or edge-only regions every match looks identical and the true arrow is unknowable, so rather than confidently guessing wrong, the model measures its own doubt and eases off exactly where honesty is required.

2 · Follow one point through the whole clip

Optical flow only ever compares two neighbouring frames, so it hands you the tiny nudge each pixel makes from one moment to the next. Chain those nudges across a long clip and the errors pile up, the point drifts, and the moment it slips behind something the trail is simply lost. Point tracking asks a different question: pick one spot in one frame and report where that exact spot is in every other frame, even after it changes shape, dims, brightens, hides behind an object, and reappears later. The 2026 crop pushes this in four directions at once: longer range without losing the thread, training on messy real footage instead of clean synthetic renders, using several cameras at once, and doing it all with cheaper machinery. That last point matters because the old way of matching every pixel against every other pixel is expensive, and the field is now hunting for lighter designs. And point tracks are quietly becoming the teacher for 4D reconstruction, since knowing how each surface point moves over time is exactly the supervision a model needs to rebuild a scene in motion.
CVPR'26CoWTracker: Tracking by Warping instead of Correlation
Problem
The standard way to track a point is to build a cost volume that scores the chosen point against every location in every other frame. That all-against-all comparison grows quadratically with image resolution, so it eats memory and slows to a crawl exactly when you want dense, high-resolution tracks.
Approach
CoWTracker throws out the cost volume entirely and tracks by warping instead. Given its current guess of where the point went, it pulls (warps) the features from each target frame back toward the query point and checks how well they line up, then iteratively refines the guess. A transformer with alternating spatial and temporal attention reasons about all the tracks together, while a backbone plus a DPT upsampler push the result to high resolution. The striking part is that this one warping-only design handles both point tracking and ordinary optical flow with the same architecture.
Why it matters
Dropping the quadratic cost volume for linear warping is what makes dense long-range tracking affordable, letting a single model chase points across a full clip and set state-of-the-art on TAP-Vid-DAVIS and Kinetics while staying competitive on flow benchmarks like Sintel and KITTI.

CVPR'26AnthroTAP: Learning Point Tracking with Real-World Motion
Problem
Point trackers are mostly trained on synthetic video because that is where perfect ground-truth point labels come from, but synthetic clips miss the messiness of real human motion. Occlusions, appearance changes, and jerky motion discontinuities in uncontrolled footage look nothing like the clean rendered training data, so trackers stumble on exactly the cases that matter.
Approach
AnthroTAP closes that gap on the data side rather than the model side: it supplies point-tracking annotations for real-world human-motion sequences. The clips deliberately capture diverse anthropomorphic dynamics — bodies occluding each other, viewpoints shifting, and limbs articulating in complicated ways. Alongside the data it ships baseline trackers and an evaluation protocol so methods can be measured on natural motion instead of synthetic proxies. The result is a benchmark that tests whether a tracker actually survives the diversity of everyday human movement.
Why it matters
Training and testing on genuine human motion attacks the synthetic-to-real domain gap head-on, which is the single biggest reason point trackers fail once they leave the lab and meet real occlusion and appearance change.

CVPR'26MV-TAP: Tracking Any Point in Multi-View Videos
Problem
A single camera gives a tracker only one flat view of the world, so occlusion, motion blur, and depth ambiguity become hard walls — when a point vanishes behind something in that one view, there is nothing left to look at. Multi-camera rigs see the same scene from several angles, but ordinary single-view trackers have no way to borrow evidence from the other cameras.
Approach
MV-TAP tracks a chosen point across all the cameras at once, working directly in 2D pixel space rather than reconstructing full 3D. It encodes each camera's geometry and uses a cross-view attention mechanism so information from one view can inform tracking in another, letting a point occluded in one camera be pinned down by the cameras that still see it. Camera geometry ties the views together into consistent spatio-temporal correspondences. Because real multi-view tracking labels are scarce, the authors build a large synthetic training set plus real-world evaluation benchmarks like Harmony4D.
Why it matters
Sharing evidence across cameras resolves the geometric ambiguities — occlusion and depth confusion — that no single view can escape, giving clear gains over single-view baselines on multi-view scenes.

CVPR'26Real-World Point Tracking with Verifier-Guided Pseudo-Labeling
Problem
Top point trackers are trained on synthetic data like Kubric and FlyingThings and then degrade on real video because of the domain gap. The obvious fix — fine-tune on real footage — is blocked by the fact that real video has no ground-truth point tracks to learn from.
Approach
The idea is to let the models label their own real data, but only trust the labels a verifier approves. A Candidate Transformer takes the query point plus the outputs of an ensemble of six teacher trackers and, using per-frame cross-attention and temporal self-attention, scores how reliable each tracker's trajectory is. That verifier is trained purely on synthetic clips with a contrastive loss: the true trajectory is the positive, and deliberately corrupted trajectories (jittered, stuck, or made to disappear) are the negatives. On unlabeled real videos (TAO, OVIS, VSPW) the verifier picks the most reliable teacher's track as a pseudo-label, keeps only high-confidence clips, and fine-tunes the student. The resulting Track-On-R model improves consistently over its CoTracker3 baseline across Ego, RoboTAP, Kinetics, and DAVIS.
Why it matters
Vetting pseudo-labels before trusting them lets a tracker learn from real footage without any human annotation, turning the abundance of unlabeled real video into a cure for the synthetic domain gap.

3 · Match the same point across wide views

To recover where a camera stood, or to rebuild a scene in 3D from a handful of photos, you first have to solve a deceptively simple task: find the same physical point in two pictures taken from very different angles. When the viewpoints are far apart and a surface is bland or repetitive, there is often no reliable texture to anchor on, and a single patch could plausibly match many places. For years, systems relied on hand-crafted keypoints like SIFT, but modern detector-free semi-dense matchers replaced them, learning to lay down a dense field of correspondences directly and feeding structure-from-motion, SLAM, and localization. The 2026 theme pushes three fronts at once: efficiency, with linear-complexity backbones that scale to high-resolution images; robustness, holding up in textureless or wildly-shifted views; and unification, learning to match across 2D images and 3D point clouds with one model. This is the aperture problem at its worst — at a wide baseline, geometry alone leaves the answer ambiguous. So these papers borrow outside help: language descriptions, learned priors, and shared cues that break the tie where pixels cannot.

CVPR'26Scalable Feature Matching via State Space Modeling and Sparse Correlation
Problem

Semi-dense matchers are the workhorse behind 3D reconstruction and localization, but the popular transformer-based ones get expensive fast: their cost grows with the square of the image resolution. That makes them hard to run on high-resolution photos or on the latency-sensitive, resource-limited devices where SLAM and localization actually live.

Approach

SLiM swaps the heavy transformer context-modeling for a hybrid Conv-Mamba backbone — a state-space model whose cost grows only linearly with resolution, so it can extract cross-view and cross-scale features on big images without blowing up. To avoid computing a full, redundant correlation between every pair of locations, it adds a training-free sparse correlation step: a simple L2-norm filter picks out the geometrically salient regions worth matching and skips the rest. A lightweight recurrent refinement unit (about 816K parameters) then iteratively nudges each match to sub-pixel accuracy. Together this keeps the whole pipeline linear where it matters most while preserving precision.

Why it matters

Reconstructing 3D or recovering camera pose needs matches across wide, high-resolution views, and doing that within a real device's memory and time budget is the bottleneck — SLiM reports 45% less memory and 2.4x faster inference at high resolution while slightly beating Efficient LoFTR (AUC@5 degrees of 57.9 on MegaDepth).

CVPR'26TextFM: Robust Semi-dense Feature Matching with Language Guidance
Problem

Matching the same point across two images breaks down exactly where geometry gives you nothing to grip: textureless regions and large viewpoint changes. A blank wall or a repetitive facade looks the same everywhere, so a semi-dense matcher has no way to tell one candidate from another.

Approach

TextFM's insight is that a language description of the scene carries semantic knowledge that raw pixels lack. It brings in vision-language model features and uses them as guidance during semi-dense matching, so the matcher can reason about what a region is — a door, a window ledge, the left corner of a sign — rather than only how its texture looks. Those semantic cues help disambiguate correspondences in the challenging, feature-poor areas where appearance alone is uninformative, and they add stability when the two cameras see the scene from very different angles. The result is a matcher that stays robust across diverse scene types instead of collapsing on the hard cases.

Why it matters

Wide-baseline, textureless matching is precisely where the aperture problem bites hardest and where SfM and pose recovery most often fail — leaning on language lets the model break ties that geometry alone cannot resolve.

CVPR'26UniCorrn: Unified Correspondence Transformer Across 2D and 3D
Problem

The correspondence problem shows up in three flavors — matching image-to-image, image-to-point-cloud, and point-cloud-to-point-cloud — and each is normally handled by its own specialized model with a distinct architecture. That siloing blocks any knowledge sharing between the tasks and multiplies deployment complexity.

Approach

UniCorrn is a single transformer with shared weights that covers all three. Modality-specific encoders (a ViT for images, Point Transformer v3 for point clouds) extract initial features, which then flow into a shared fusion encoder of alternating self- and cross-attention layers so the model learns modality-agnostic correspondence. Its core trick is a dual-stream matching decoder: an appearance stream and a positional stream are kept side by side but updated by the same Gaussian attention matrix, so semantic and geometric cues reinforce rather than fight each other. Lightweight per-modality heads turn the decoder output into 2D-2D, 2D-3D, or 3D-3D match coordinates, trained with a multi-task mix of confidence-aware L1 and InfoNCE contrastive losses. Because the shared encoder and decoder are tied across tasks, learning to match in one modality transfers to the others.

Why it matters

2D-to-3D and 3D-to-3D matching are the backbone of visual localization and point-cloud registration — the geometric heart of pose recovery — and unifying them lifts all three at once (91.0% recall on 7Scenes, +10% on 3DLoMatch, 54.2% AUC@5 degrees on MegaDepth).

CVPR'26MARCO: Navigating the Unseen Space of Semantic Correspondence
Problem

Big semantic-correspondence models that stack DINOv2 and a diffusion backbone score well on benchmarks but generalize poorly: trained on a sparse set of annotated keypoints, they fall apart on query points and object categories they never saw. Real uses like pose estimation and image editing need matches that hold up on novel points, not just the ones in the training set.

Approach

MARCO is a lightweight DINOv2 adapter — bottleneck adapters plus a compact upsampling head add under 5% extra parameters on top of the frozen backbone. It trains with two complementary objectives. A coarse-to-fine objective steadily narrows a Gaussian target so the model first learns the right region, then pins down the exact sub-patch. A dense self-distillation step mines reliable mutual matches across different object instances and densifies them by piecewise-affine interpolation over Delaunay triangles, using the sparse ground-truth annotations as geometric anchors to filter out bad matches. This expands a handful of labeled keypoints into thousands of dense correspondences without needing any 3D templates, teaching the model to match points it was never explicitly supervised on.

Why it matters

The same textureless, ambiguous-matching pressure that plagues wide-baseline geometry shows up as unseen-keypoint failure here, and MARCO's learned prior generalizes where dual-encoder giants overfit — while running 3x smaller and 10x faster (87.2 fps) and improving +5.1 PCK on unseen keypoints.

4 · Motion in 3D, and where methods die

Optical flow lives in the flat image plane: it tells you where each pixel slid across the picture, but not whether it moved toward you, away, or sideways in the real world. Scene flow asks the harder question — where did each point actually move in full 3D space — which is exactly what a car or a robot needs, because a pixel drifting left could be a wall you are passing or a child stepping into the road. It is harder because depth and motion have to be solved together, and honest 3D ground truth is scarce, so methods must lean on structure and clever sampling rather than brute-force labels. Separately, every correspondence method — flow, stereo, tracking — tends to fail in the same three conditions: fast motion blur that smears a moving object into a streak, occlusion where a point simply vanishes behind something, and blank textureless surfaces where every possible match looks identical so the true one cannot be pinned down. That last failure is the aperture ambiguity: peering at a plain edge through a small window, you can tell it slid along its length but never how far, because nothing distinguishes one spot from the next. Against blur and blistering speed the favourite weapon is the event camera, which reports individual brightness changes at microsecond speed instead of whole frames, so it keeps seeing sharply exactly where an ordinary camera turns to mush.
CVPR'26SEA-Flow3D: Simplified, Efficient, and Accurate Scene Flow via Spatial Vector Sampling and Multi-scale Refinement
Problem
Leading dense scene-flow methods like RAFT-3D actually reason about motion by comparing flat 2D image features, and only sprinkle in depth at the very first step. That means all the iterative polishing that follows happens in a 2D world, so the rich 3D shape of the scene — which points are near, which are far, which way a surface faces — is never really used while the answer is being refined.
Approach
SEA-Flow3D fixes this by sampling correlations directly in 3D. Its Spatial Vector Sampling module looks at each point together with its 3D neighbours and builds a direction-aware match score that carries explicit spatial vectors — not just how similar two patches look, but which way they sit in space. This geometric hint is fed in continuously throughout a RAFT-style coarse-to-fine refinement loop, guiding every iteration rather than just the input. A context-guided upsampling step then keeps the answer consistent as it grows from coarse to fine. Crucially it does all this without heavy 3D convolutions or bulky point-cloud networks, so it stays light and fast.
Why it matters
Getting continuous 3D geometric guidance into the loop is what lets a driving or robotics system tell real forward-and-sideways motion from mere pixel drift, and doing it cheaply matters because these systems have to run in real time. It reaches state of the art on KITTI and Sintel while being faster and more compact than prior dense scene-flow methods.

CVPR'26ARES: Unifying Asymmetric RGB-Event Stereo for Probabilistic Scene Flow Estimation
Problem
Working out 3D scene flow from a stereo pair depends on matching the same point across cameras and time, and ordinary RGB cameras are great at this on textured surfaces but fall apart under motion blur and shifting light. Event cameras stay sharp through fast motion and lighting swings, but they hand you sparse, low-resolution flickers of change instead of full pictures. Fusing two sensors this asymmetric — one dense and slow, one sparse and fast — without one dragging the other down is the core difficulty.
Approach
ARES treats RGB-event stereo scene flow as a probabilistic inference problem rather than a single hard guess. It encodes the dense, texture-rich RGB stream and the sparse, high-speed event stream separately, then fuses their predictions while explicitly modelling uncertainty — so wherever RGB is blurred or blind, the confident event evidence is trusted more, and vice versa. This uncertainty-aware weighting is what makes the fusion robust instead of a naive average of two unequal signals. The result is scene flow that holds up on dynamic scenes with blur and occlusion where RGB-only or event-only baselines break down.
Why it matters
This directly targets the blur-and-lighting failure that kills camera-only 3D motion estimation, which is exactly where a fast-moving car or drone most needs to trust its perception. Modelling uncertainty means the system knows when to lean on the sensor that can still see.

CVPR'26When Lines Meet Textures: Spatial-Frequency Aligned Diffusion Features for Cross-Sparsity Correspondence
Problem
Matching a bare line drawing to a real detailed photograph is a brutal case of the same problem correspondence methods hit on textureless surfaces: one side is nearly blank while the other is packed with detail, so there is almost nothing to anchor a match. The two also differ in their frequency makeup — sketches are mostly flat empty regions with a few razor-sharp edges, while photos spread detail across all frequencies — and off-the-shelf diffusion features, tuned on textured images, simply go blank over the empty parts of a drawing.
Approach
The method aligns the two in both space and frequency so sparse line-art and dense photos can be matched. It fine-tunes a unified diffusion feature extractor with lightweight LoRA on sketch data, pulling both drawings and photos into one shared meaning-space while damping the noise unique to each. A Low-Frequency Feature Aggregation module then uses a wavelet transform to split features into coarse and fine bands and selectively boost the low-frequency parts — the broad, smooth structure that a sparse sketch and a busy photo genuinely share — before rebuilding the feature. Training pulls matching points together with a contrastive loss and pins down dense matches with a differentiable soft-argmax. The upshot is reliable correspondence even where one image is almost empty.
Why it matters
This is the aperture-ambiguity and textureless-matching failure met head-on: when there is little to match on, aligning the shared low-frequency structure gives the match something to grip. It sets state of the art on sketch-photo correspondence benchmarks.

CVPR'26MER-Tracker: Towards High-Speed 3D Point Tracking via Multi-View Event-RGB Hybrid Cameras
Problem
Following a specific point through 3D space during very fast motion — a sprinter, an insect wingbeat, a spinning object — defeats ordinary cameras, which shoot about 30 frames a second and so leave big gaps and smear fast movement into blur. Event cameras have the microsecond speed to catch that motion, but they carry no texture and go quiet on anything that is not moving, so neither sensor alone can do the job.
Approach
MER-Tracker fuses fast-but-sparse events with detailed-but-blurry RGB across multiple viewpoints. A dual-modal 2D extractor pulls motion-change cues from both the event and RGB streams, then a heterogeneous 3D fusion stage does temporal interpolation and anchor sampling to knit the multi-view evidence into consistent 3D motion features. A LoRA-tuned Transformer reads the temporal correlations and predicts smooth, high-frame-rate 3D trajectories at roughly 150 FPS — far denser in time than the RGB cameras alone could ever provide. A purpose-built rig with four RGB and two event cameras supplies the synchronized multi-view capture. The RGB gives texture the events lack, and the events give the temporal acuity the RGB lacks.
Why it matters
This is the blur-and-high-speed failure turned into a measurement tool, letting robotics and scientific analysis track fast phenomena in true 3D instead of losing them between frames. It outperforms prior methods on both real and simulated fast-motion datasets with stable trajectories.

5 · Correspondence as scaffolding

The biggest shift in this corpus is that flow stopped being the deliverable and became the scaffold. Dense 2D correspondence between neighboring frames is free supervision — you always have consecutive frames, so you never have to pay for labels — and that turns out to be exactly what is needed to reconstruct dynamic 3D and 4D and to steer generative video. These papers use flow and tracking to learn feed-forward geometry without any pose or depth labels, to reconstruct a scene's shape and motion together from a single ordinary video, to guide a video generator toward new camera angles, and to keep animated 4D assets from drifting over time. In each case the correspondence signal is not the output the user cares about; it is the invisible teacher or the control knob behind the real result. What the four share is a reframing: the thing that used to be the answer is now the infrastructure. Correspondence now sits quietly under the whole dynamic-scene and video-generation stack.
CVPR'26Flow3r: Factored Flow Prediction for Scalable Visual Geometry Learning
Problem
Systems that reconstruct 3D and 4D geometry in one forward pass are trained on dense geometry and camera-pose labels, and those labels are expensive and barely exist for messy real-world video of moving scenes. That label scarcity caps how much data these models can learn from, especially for in-the-wild and dynamic footage.
Approach
Flow3r throws away the need for geometry and pose labels and supervises the model with plain 2D flow — the pixel-to-pixel correspondence between two frames — which you can extract from any unlabeled video. Its core trick is a factored flow prediction module that is deliberately asymmetric: it takes the geometry latent from the source frame and the camera-pose latent from the target frame and combines them to predict the flow between them. Because flow is jointly explained by shape and camera motion, matching the predicted flow to observed flow forces the model to learn both scene geometry and camera motion at once. This asymmetric design beats the usual symmetric correspondence supervision, and it extends naturally to moving scenes. The team trains on roughly 800K unlabeled videos mixed with a smaller set of labeled 3D data.
Why it matters
Flow is used here as a pure teacher: it lets the model learn 3D from hundreds of thousands of ordinary videos it could never have labeled, with the largest gains exactly where labels are scarcest — in-the-wild dynamic video.

CVPR'26MotionCrafter: Dense Geometry and Motion Reconstruction with a 4D VAE
Problem
Recovering both a scene's 3D shape over time and the dense motion of every point from a single ordinary video is deeply under-determined, and temporal correspondence breaks down under occlusion and large motion. Earlier feed-forward methods still needed a slow per-scene cleanup pass afterward and lost track of long-range motion.
Approach
MotionCrafter borrows the muscle of a pretrained video diffusion model and adds a new 4D VAE that encodes point-maps (the geometry) and scene-flow (the motion) together into one shared latent, all defined relative to the first frame so shape and motion live in a single world-centric coordinate system. It trains in two stages — first a Geometry VAE, then a Motion VAE with geometry frozen — and then fuses them, so the diffusion network learns to denoise a combined geometry-plus-motion latent in one shot. A key finding is that the 4D latents do not need to be forced to match the original RGB video-model's distribution; a simple canonical normalization works better than the usual alignment. The result is joint 4D geometry and dense motion with no post-optimization step.
Why it matters
Here scene-flow is a first-class part of what gets reconstructed rather than a throwaway signal — encoding motion alongside geometry is what lets the model capture coherent point trajectories across a whole clip in a single feed-forward pass.

CVPR'26Scaling4D: Pushing the Frontier of Video Novel View Synthesis through Correspondence-Guided Generation
Problem
Generating a photorealistic video of a scene from a fresh camera angle, given only one monocular video, is badly under-constrained. Worse, models are trained on paired multi-view footage that barely exists at scale, while at test time they only ever get single-camera input — a training-versus-inference gap that stops the approach from scaling.
Approach
Scaling4D reframes novel-view video synthesis as correspondence-guided generation so that ordinary monocular video becomes free training data. It runs RAFT optical flow, warps each source frame along that flow to fake what a new viewpoint would look like, and feeds those flow-warped frames through a Correspondence Projector that turns them into control tokens. Those tokens condition lightweight attention-only VNVS Blocks inside a diffusion-transformer video generator, effectively replacing the usual cross-frame attention with a correspondence signal. Because the warped frames act as pseudo novel-view pairs, the model can be trained self-supervised on 3M monocular videos, with only a small synthetic component supplying true multi-view geometry. Flow, in other words, manufactures the supervision that multi-view datasets could not provide.
Why it matters
Flow is the control knob steering the generator: warped-frame correspondence both closes the multi-view data gap and tells the model which pixels should move where when the camera shifts.

CVPR'26Tracking-Guided 4D Generation: Foundation-Tracker Motion Priors for 3D Model Animation
Problem
Generating an animated 4D asset from sparse inputs has to stay consistent across many viewpoints and across time, but plain pixel- or latent-space video diffusion has no explicit sense of what point went where. Without that, the results drift and flicker — appearance wanders and motion loses coherence over the sequence.
Approach
Track4DGen fixes this by piping a foundation point-tracker into the generation loop as a source of dense temporal correspondence supervision. In stage one it runs CoTracker3 to get dense keypoint tracks across the generated frames, then enforces those tracks inside the diffusion generator through a Correspondence Tracking Loss operating at the feature level, so the model is explicitly told which features must correspond over time. In stage two it reconstructs a 4D Gaussian-Splatting asset using a hybrid motion encoding that concatenates the tracking-informed diffusion features with Hex-plane features and adds 4D spherical harmonics. The tracker's correspondences act as a motion prior that anchors the animation frame to frame. The payoff is temporally stable, text-editable 4D assets with cleaner motion.
Why it matters
The tracker's dense correspondences are the free supervision that keeps the 4D animation coherent — explicit tracking priors are what stop the drift and flicker that unsupervised video diffusion produces on its own.

6 · Correspondence a robot acts on

A moving robot keeps its eye on the same features frame to frame, and the way those features slide across the image reveals its own three-dimensional motion — this is the dominant use of matching in robotics, the engine behind visual odometry and SLAM. The whole story here is survival: the match has to hold up through darkness, blank textureless walls, the lurch of a legged gait, and motion blur, because a single bad match is not a bad number, it is a wrong pose. On a robot arm the same tracked point becomes something else entirely — a live steering signal that closes the loop between where the gripper is and where it should be, driven at control rate. And scene flow flips the frame around: it subtracts the robot's own motion from the scan so that what is left over is the true three-dimensional velocity of the things that could actually hit it. In none of these cases is the correspondence ever shown to a person or scored for its own sake. It becomes how the robot knows it moved, what is moving toward it, and which way to steer — computed onboard, in real time. The punchline of the whole theme: in vision the goal is to estimate the motion; in robotics a robot has to act on it.

ROBOTICS'25MAC-VO: Metrics-Aware Covariance for Learning-Based Stereo Visual Odometry
Problem

A robot with two cameras figures out where it moved by matching points between frames, but a learning-based matcher does not tell you how much to trust any single match in real-world units. So the system picks its tracking points badly and weights their errors badly, and the estimated path drifts.

Approach

MAC-VO trains a model to output, for every matched keypoint, an honest uncertainty in actual metric units — how far off that point's position could really be, and how that error correlates across the different axes. That per-point uncertainty is then put to work twice. First it filters the point set, throwing out matches whose error is globally inconsistent before they can poison anything. Then it becomes the weight each remaining point carries inside the pose optimization, so a crisp confident match pulls hard on the answer and a fuzzy one barely nudges it. This replaces the hand-tuned or scale-blind confidence values that older systems fell back on.

Why it matters

Egomotion is the deliverable here — the robot learns that it moved from how the matches shifted — and a match trusted more than it deserves drags the estimated pose off the true track, so the robot believes it is somewhere it is not.

ROBOTICS'25GeoFlow-SLAM: A Robust Tightly-Coupled RGBD-Inertial and Legged Odometry Fusion SLAM for Dynamic Legged Robotics
Problem

Tracking a four-legged robot's location from its cameras and motion sensors breaks down during fast motion or in front of bare, featureless walls, because the images stop matching cleanly and the position estimate drifts. The bouncing, jarring gait of a walking robot makes this far worse than for a wheeled or hand-held camera.

Approach

GeoFlow-SLAM welds several signals into one tightly-coupled estimate: color-plus-depth cameras, the inertial sensor, and leg odometry from the robot's own joints. The key trick is a dual-stream optical flow for matching features — one stream flows features from the map points and the other from the robot's predicted prior pose — so that when the picture alone is too blurred or too blank to match, the motion prior still supplies where the features should have gone. It also seeds each new pose from the inertial and leg odometry, refines it geometrically, and then locks the result against reality with a GICP fit that snaps the live geometry onto the map. All of these constraints are optimized together rather than in separate stages.

Why it matters

This is egomotion under the hardest conditions a robot faces — gait, blur, and blank walls — and if the flow matches the wrong feature the robot's whole sense of where it is jumps, so fusing the leg and inertial priors is what keeps a bad match from becoming a wrong step.

ROBOTICS'25CNSv2: Probabilistic Correspondence Encoded Neural Image Servo
Problem

Steering a robot arm by matching what its camera sees to a target image falls apart under bad lighting or on smooth, textureless surfaces, because reliably pinning down matching points there is simply too hard. A wrong match feeds the controller a false error and the arm moves the wrong way.

Approach

Instead of committing to one hard-chosen location for each matched point, CNSv2 keeps every match as a probability spread across several plausible locations — a soft, multi-peaked distribution that admits when it is unsure. Those soft correspondence scores, uncertainty and all, are fed directly into a neural servo policy rather than being collapsed into single points first. The controller therefore reasons with the full spread of where a feature might be, so a shaky match softens its influence rather than yanking the arm off course. This runs in real time and closes the visual loop continuously as the arm moves.

Why it matters

Here the match is not a picture to look at, it is a live steering signal closing the control loop, so a confident wrong match becomes a real wrong motion of the arm — carrying the uncertainty into the controller is what lets it stay steady when the light or the surface gives it almost nothing to grip.

ROBOTICS'25SSF: Sparse Long-Range Scene Flow for Autonomous Driving
Problem

Working out which way every point in a 3D sensor scan is moving gets far too slow and memory-hungry at long range, because the usual methods lay a dense grid over all of space and process every cell whether anything is there or not. But at highway speeds the motion that matters most is the distant, fast-approaching kind.

Approach

SSF processes only the sensor points that actually exist, using sparse convolution so empty space costs nothing. To compare scans taken at different moments — which never contain the same points in the same order — it introduces placeholder cells called virtual voxels that let the two time-sequential scans line up before their motion is estimated. A range-weighted training loss then deliberately pushes the network to care more about far-away points, which are exactly the ones dense methods handle worst. The result estimates a 3D velocity for every real point out to long range while staying fast enough to be practical.

Why it matters

Once the vehicle's own motion is subtracted, what is left is the genuine 3D velocity of everything around it — the obstacle-motion signal — and getting a distant point's velocity wrong is exactly the miss that turns a fast-closing hazard into a collision, which is why the range-weighting matters.

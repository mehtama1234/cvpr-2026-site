Value-based RL (Q-learning & Bellman) — why it works, mathematically

A robot faces thousands of moves ahead, each leading somewhere different. How can it learn to count the total future reward each move will bring, cheaply enough to actually make decisions?

← All themesEvery approachThe mathematics ↗

The problem, as a quantity

Imagine a robot that can move left or right, and every move leads to some reward — maybe a step left earns five points, a step right earns one, but only this turn. A naive choice is to chase the five points and step left every time. But what if stepping right one move leads to a corridor of ten-point rewards for the rest of time, while stepping left leads to a dead end?

The whole problem is that the best move today depends on where it leads tomorrow. A robot needs to know not just the immediate reward from a move but the total future reward: today's points plus tomorrow's best move plus the day after, all the way to the end. That total is what makes a move truly valuable.

Learning this by checking every possible path ahead is hopeless—there are too many. The solution is a trick: each move's value must agree with itself one step later. If you know the value of tomorrow's best move, you can compute today's value without looking past tomorrow. That one idea, repeated over and over, builds up the value of every move in a runnable algorithm.

The core ideas — and why they work

Score each move by the total future reward it leads to

Q(s,a) = expected total future reward from taking action a in state s
Imagine you are rating each move by how much future reward it brings. You look at a state — a moment where you stand in a hallway, say — and you have to score each possible action: left, right, up, down. The score you give is the expected total future reward you will collect if you take that action right now.

This is the core insight: instead of hoping for the best or guessing, you attach a single number to each state-action pair that represents the sum of all rewards you will earn from that moment onward if you follow that move. Call this the state-action value, written as the function that takes a state and an action and outputs a number. It is the single measurement that absorbs all the thinking: how good is this move, really?

Building this map is the heart of learning. Once you have it, the robot's job is simple: look at the state you are in, ask the map for the value of each move, and pick the one with the highest number. The map does all the reasoning about the future. The hard part is learning the map.

A value has to agree with itself one step later

Q(s,a) = r + γ · max_{a'} Q(s', a') (the Bellman rule)
Here is the key realization: a value does not stand alone. It has to agree with itself one step later.

When you are at a state and take an action, two things happen: you get a reward immediately, and you land in a new state. From that new state, the best future you can have is the highest value of any action you can take there. So the value of your current move must equal the immediate reward plus the discounted value of the best move from where you land.

This is the self-consistency rule. If your current move's value is higher than this rule says it should be, then you are over-counting the future. If it is lower, you are under-counting. Enforce this rule everywhere, and the values must be correct. The formula says: the value is the immediate reward, plus a fraction (the discount factor) of the best value waiting in the next state. That fraction accounts for the fact that a reward tomorrow is worth a little less than a reward today.

Learn it by nudging each guess toward its own one-step lookahead

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α·[ r + γ·max_{a'} Q(s',a') − Q(s,a) ]
Here is how to actually learn. You start with a guess at each move's value — maybe all zeros, maybe random numbers. Then, over and over, you nudge the guess toward the self-consistency rule.

For each move you try, you compute what the rule says the value should be: the reward you got, plus the discount factor times the best value in the next state. That is the target. Then you ask: how far off was my guess from this target? Take a step toward the target, proportional to how wrong you were. The step size (the learning rate) is tiny so you do not overshoot.

Repeat this for thousands of moves, and the guesses converge to the true values. You are not learning a formula; you are learning a lookup table where each entry holds the value of one state-action pair. Each time you update, you are nudging that entry a little closer to what the rule says it should be, given the move you just took.

Explore or exploit: you can't value a move you never try

mostly take the best-known move, sometimes a random one (ε-greedy)
There is one more trap: you cannot learn the value of a move you have never tried. If you always play it safe and take the move that looks best so far, you will never explore the moves that might actually be better. You will get stuck in a local habit instead of finding the globally best move.

The solution is to sometimes act at random. Most of the time, take the move with the highest known value — that is exploiting what you have learned. But occasionally, pick a random move instead — that is exploring the unknown. This is called epsilon-greedy: a fraction of the time (epsilon), you act randomly; the rest of the time, you exploit. As learning progresses and you become more confident in your values, you can lower epsilon and explore less, but you can never stop exploring entirely, or you might miss a better option hiding in an unexplored corner.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Work here turns a few dials on the same learning loop. When to explore. Always random (simple), or smart exploration that probes moves you are uncertain about. How to learn the value. Update one move at a time (on-policy), or learn from moves you watched someone else take (off-policy). How to initialize. Guess zero for all values, or use an optimistic guess to encourage exploring promising-looking moves early. How often to update. After every single move, or batch many moves together to smooth out noise. The thread never changes: the value of a move must match the immediate reward plus the best future from where it lands, and learning means nudging each guess toward that match—at the honest cost of having to explore moves that look bad today but might lead to riches tomorrow.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

One notch deeper: each of the theme's 4 approach-families and the specific mathematical mechanism that makes its papers work — the exact knob turned on the core relations above. Under each, the specific mathematical note for every paper in that family.

1On-policy vs. off-policy learning
SARSA: Q̂(s,a) ← Q̂(s,a) + α[r + γQ̂(s',a') − Q̂(s,a)] vs. Q-learning: Q̂(s,a) ← Q̂(s,a) + α[r + γmax(Q̂(s',·)) − Q̂(s,a)]
SARSA learns from the action you actually took next; Q-learning learns from the best possible action. Q-learning optimizes faster because it doesn't wait to discover good moves—it immediately assumes you'll pick the best one. SARSA is safer in risky environments because it learns what actually happens when you follow your current policy, not an imagined best policy.
2Tabular vs. function approximation
Store Q(s,a) in a lookup table for each state-action pair, or learn a single function Q(s,a;θ) parameterized by weights θ (e.g., neural network)
A lookup table works perfectly when you have a small state space, but explodes when states are images or continuous. A learned function like a neural network compresses the same information into weights, so it generalizes—a similar state reuses the same weight values. The tradeoff: lookup tables are stable; learned functions need careful tuning to avoid blowing up.
3Target network stabilization
Use a separate, slowly-updated network for the one-step lookahead: target ← r + γQ̂_old(s',best_a) using frozen weights, not Q̂_new(s',best_a)
When you improve your Q-estimate, the one-step lookahead target changes too, chasing a moving goalpost. Freezing old weights for the target breaks this feedback loop—you compare your guess against a stable reference, then update only one side. This dramatically shrinks the noise in training and makes learning converge.
4Exploration policy (ε-greedy, UCB, counts)
ε-greedy: pick best move with probability 1−ε, random move with probability ε. UCB: pick move a that maximizes Q̂(s,a) + c√(ln(N(s))/(N(s,a))), where N counts visits.
Pure greedy wastes moves—you lock onto one action and never discover better ones. ε-greedy forces random tries at rate ε. UCB is smarter: it picks moves you haven't tried much (high visit-count bonus) plus moves with high value estimates. Both solve the same tension: exploring unmapped territory vs. exploiting what you know works.

The through-line. The core puzzle is that the best move today depends on tomorrow's best options, which you have not seen yet. You could check every path ahead, but there are too many. The trick is self-consistency: if you know tomorrow's best value, you can compute today's value by a simple rule, without looking past tomorrow. Build a map of values by repeatedly enforcing this rule, and the map converges to the truth. But you have to try moves you have not tried before to even know their values, so you need a tension: most of the time exploit the best move you know, sometimes explore the unknown.

Two plain ideas carry it: a move's value must agree with itself one step later via a simple addition rule, and learning means nudging your guesses toward that rule by moving in the direction that cuts the error.

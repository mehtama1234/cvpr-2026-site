Gradient descent & optimizers — why it works, mathematically

A network makes an answer wrong. To fix it, you nudge each adjustable number downhill toward a better answer—but the space has billions of dimensions, and you need to do it cheaply, over and over, without getting stuck or overshooting.

The key turn — the principle at work

The stuck point. Knowing the error is not enough. The model needs a rule for how to move its settings so the next error is lower.

The mathematical move. The principle is local slope as a guide to movement. Gradient descent follows the direction where the loss rises fastest, but steps the other way. Momentum and adaptive methods change how steps are carried across uneven ground.

What changes. Training becomes controlled movement through a loss surface. The model does not guess new settings; it uses measured slope, step size, and memory of past slopes.

The problem, as a quantity

A network turns an input into an answer by pushing it through a long chain of small numeric operations, each governed by adjustable numbers—often billions of them. Every time the answer comes out wrong, you need to nudge each number a little bit in a direction that would have made the answer slightly better. That means measuring how wrong the answer is, then figuring out which way to nudge each number.

But here is the knot: you cannot just tweak every number and measure again—that would take a billion separate test runs per update, which is impossibly slow. And even if you knew which way to go, taking huge steps might overshoot the best answer, or get stuck thrashing around. You need a cheap way to find the slope in every dimension at once, then take a careful step that does not waste effort on dimensions that barely matter.

The core ideas — and why they work

Feel the slope, take a small step downhill

θ ← θ − η·∇L(θ) (η = step size)
The core idea is to compute the slope—the direction in which a small change to the answer makes the error bigger or smaller. That slope tells you which way to nudge each number to reduce the error. You start with a guess for all the numbers, compute the slope at that guess (this is the price you pay once per step), then move a small distance down the slope. The step size, often written η (eta), controls how far you go—too small and learning crawls; too large and you bounce past the answer and crash into worse answers. This one sweep—measure slope, step downhill—repeats hundreds of thousands of times until the answer stops improving much.

Use a small random handful of examples each step

∇L_batch = ∇L_full + noise, E[noise] = 0
The slope is expensive to compute if you use all the data every time. A shortcut is to use only a small random handful of examples—perhaps 32 or 128 out of millions—and compute the slope on just those. Because you pick them randomly, the slope you measure on this handful is almost the same as the true slope on all the data, but with a little random noise added in. The magic is that the noise is balanced—on average it points nowhere—so over many steps the random wiggles cancel out and you still drift reliably downhill. This way you get fast steps without re-examining all the data each time.

Momentum: keep a running direction to power through

v ← μ·v − η·∇L, θ ← θ + v
A problem with pure downhill stepping is that the landscape has ravines—narrow valleys where the slope points sideways more than downward. Momentum fixes this by giving the step a memory: instead of stopping at the slope, you keep a running average of all the slopes you have seen, and step in that accumulated direction. This is like rolling a heavy ball downhill—inertia carries it straight through the noise and wiggles, and it picks up speed on long consistent slopes. Momentum is written as a new running direction (v, often called velocity) that pulls from the old direction and the new slope, and you step by adding that v to your numbers rather than subtracting the slope directly.

Give each direction its own step size

θ ← θ − η · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε) — per-direction (Adam)
Different dimensions of the answer-space are not all equal. Some change easily with small nudges; others need big pushes. If one dimension has been changing reliably downhill, it has proven it knows where to go—so you can afford to step boldly in that direction. If another dimension keeps switching back and forth, the signal is noisy—so you step timidly. Adaptive methods like Adam keep a separate running total of recent changes in each dimension (m, called the first moment), and a separate running total of recent noise in each dimension (v, called the second moment). Then for each dimension you compute a step size by dividing the signal by the noise. Dimensions with consistent signal get large steps; noisy dimensions get small ones. A tiny number ε is added to the denominator just to avoid dividing by zero.

The approaches, as variations on the math

The core machine is the same for all optimizers: find the slope, take a step downhill. But different versions tune how much history to remember (vanilla stepping forgets instantly; momentum keeps a rolling average; Adam keeps two rolling averages), what counts as the slope (all data versus a random handful), and what step size to use (fixed for all dimensions versus per-dimension adaptive). Each dial trades off between speed (fewer steps to reach a good answer), robustness (not overshooting or getting lost in noise), and the cost of bookkeeping. The thread never changes: measure how the error depends on each number, take a careful step in the direction that reduces it, repeat.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

One notch deeper: each of the theme's 4 approach-families and the specific mathematical mechanism that makes its papers work — the exact knob turned on the core relations above. Under each, the specific mathematical note for every paper in that family.

1Adaptive step sizes
g_t = m_1 / sqrt(v_t + ε) (where m_1 = slope average, v_t = variance)
Instead of using the same step size for every direction, this approach gives each coordinate its own step size based on how much the slope bounces around. A slope that thrashes up and down gets smaller steps (less trust); a slope that stays consistent gets bigger steps (more trust). This stops the optimizer from taking too-large steps on the wild directions and missing good answers.
2Learning-rate schedules
α_t = α_0 × schedule(t) (rate shrinks with time t)
Early in training, take bigger steps to move fast through the landscape. As time passes, shrink the step size so the optimizer can settle into fine details without bouncing past the minimum. Common schedules divide the rate by a fixed amount after N steps, or decay it smoothly. This prevents the late-stage thrashing that ruins convergence.
3Second-order curvature
x_new = x_old - H^{-1} * g (use Hessian H, the second slope)
The ordinary slope tells you which direction is downhill, but not how steeply the ground is curving. The second slope (called the Hessian—the curvature at each point) tells you how fast the first slope is changing. Using this curvature, you can jump closer to the minimum in fewer steps. The downside is computing the Hessian is expensive, so methods like BFGS approximate it with a running memory.
4Variance reduction
Δ = past_slope_average + small_noise (SAG/SAGA family)
Random batches save compute but add noise to each step—the optimizer jerks around instead of moving smoothly. Variance reduction keeps a memory of slopes from past steps on each data point, so instead of using only this batch's noisy slope, you blend it with the historical average. This steadies the search and lets you use bigger steps without losing control.

What breaks without this

What goes wrong. If you only know that the model is wrong, nothing tells the numbers where to move. Random changes may help once, hurt next time, and waste most of the work.

Why it goes wrong. Training is a search over settings. The error surface may slope gently in one direction and sharply in another. A step that is safe on the sharp side may be painfully slow on the flat side.

What this idea gives you. Optimization turns error into movement. The slope gives a direction, and the optimizer decides how large the step should be for each kind of ground.

The through-line. The core problem is updating billions of numbers efficiently when every update costs money—either in computation or in bumping into a worse answer. Two plain ideas carry it: first, the slope in all directions can be computed in one backward sweep (via backpropagation, not written here but the reason these methods work at all), not a billion separate measurements. And second, you do not need the exact truth about the best step—a noisy estimate from a handful of examples, smoothed by memory and per-dimension adaptation, drifts toward the answer reliably and stays cheap.

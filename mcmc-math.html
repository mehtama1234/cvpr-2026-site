Sampling by random walk (MCMC) — why it works, mathematically

To get samples from a hard-to-work-with spread, take a random walk that accepts or rejects steps by checking how likely each position is relative to its current spot. After enough steps, the walk settles into visiting each state exactly as often as it appears in the true spread.

The problem, as a quantity

Imagine you have a map of probabilities—a spread—but there's no easy way to pick points directly from it. For instance, the spread might be the shape of a learned network's beliefs about the world, or the long-tail output of a Bayesian calculation; you can evaluate how likely any specific point is, but you cannot invert the formula to generate a sample the way you would from a coin flip or bell curve. You need to draw many independent samples, all in proportion to their real probabilities, because that's the only way to estimate what the spread actually looks like.

The direct approach—try random guesses until one lands in the right probability zone—fails catastrophically in high dimensions. There are too many possible number-lists, and the true-probability zones are tiny islands in an ocean of worthless space. You would wait forever.

The solution is to stop trying to find the zones by blind guessing. Instead, take a walk: start at any point, then take small random steps, but accept or reject each step by checking how likely it makes you. This way, the walk gets stuck in the high-probability zones where it belongs, and spends its time there proportionally to how probable each part is.

The core ideas — and why they work

You can't sample a hard spread directly, so take a guided random walk

x_{t+1} drawn from x_t by a proposal, then kept or rejected
Instead of generating a full new sample from scratch, build it one step at a time. You are currently at some position x. Propose a new position x' by taking a small random step away from x. Then ask: is this new spot more likely or less likely than where I am now? If it is more likely, move there. If it is less likely, flip a weighted coin—if heads, move anyway; if tails, stay put. Over many steps, this walk will naturally spend more time in probable regions and less time in improbable ones, because the walk is always biased toward accepting uphill steps and sometimes accepting downhill steps. The key is that you do not need to know the overall shape of the probability map, just how to compare two nearby spots.

Accept moves by a ratio that never needs the impossible total

accept with prob min(1, p(x')/p(x)) — the normalizer cancels
Here is the magic: you need to accept a step with a probability that depends on the ratio of how likely the new spot is to how likely the current spot is—call that ratio r. You accept with probability equal to the minimum of one and r. This simple rule has a hidden power: the probability formula usually has a normalizing constant Z at the front—a number you must divide by to make all probabilities sum to one. But when you compute the ratio r, that constant Z appears in both the numerator and denominator and cancels out. So you can accept or reject without ever computing Z, even though finding Z directly would be impossible. The constant vanishes the moment you take the ratio, leaving you free to check acceptability from incomplete information.

Given enough steps, the walk visits each state in proportion to its probability

fraction of time spent at x → p(x) as steps → ∞
If you run the random walk for many, many steps, something beautiful happens: the fraction of time the walk spends at any particular position converges to that position's true probability. If a state is twice as likely as another, the walk visits it roughly twice as often. This is true as long as your proposal step is flexible enough to eventually reach all parts of the map (you cannot get permanently stuck). The walk will wander, backtrack, and dawdle in probable zones, but its time-spent spread asymptotically matches the true probability map. That is, in the limit of infinite steps, the empirical spread of visited states equals the true spread.

The catch is mixing: correlated steps and slow exploration

nearby steps are correlated; rugged landscapes mix slowly
The catch is that the random walk is sluggish when the landscape is jagged or when moves between different regions are rare. Each new step is correlated with the previous step—you did not jump to an independent new sample, you took a baby step from where you were—so consecutive samples are not independent; they tell you roughly the same thing. Second, if the probability map has steep valleys or narrow corridors, the walk gets trapped in local high plateaus and takes a long time to wander to other equally probable regions. This means you may need thousands or millions of steps before the walk has explored enough to represent the true spread fairly. The remedy is to design the proposal step carefully: step size too small means you get stuck locally; step size too large means you reject most proposals and waste steps. The overall mixing time—how long until the walk forgets its starting point—is the central practical challenge.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Different practical recipes tune the same machine for different shaped maps. Step size: how far each proposal jumps from the current spot—too big and most jumps are downhill enough to reject; too small and you explore glacially. Proposal symmetry: whether hopping from A to B has the same probability as hopping from B to A—this shape determines what the acceptance ratio becomes. Mixing diagnostics: checks like "has the walk forgotten its starting point?" and "do multiple parallel walks agree?"—these tell you when you have run long enough. Thinning and burn-in: discarding early steps before the walk settles, and skipping samples to break correlation. The thread never changes: a walk that biases its steps toward higher probability, tuned so that it spends time at each state in proportion to its true probability, in a way that needs only local likelihood comparisons, never the impossible global constant.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

One notch deeper: each of the theme's 4 approach-families and the specific mathematical mechanism that makes its papers work — the exact knob turned on the core relations above. Under each, the specific mathematical note for every paper in that family.

1Acceptance ratio
accept if random() < min(1, π(x_new)q(x_old|x_new) / (π(x_old)q(x_new|x_old)))
This is the decision rule: you compare how likely the new state is (π(x_new)) against the old one (π(x_old)), weighted by how easy it is to get back (q(x_old|x_new)) versus how easy it was to get there (q(x_new|x_old)). The π terms are proportional odds—you never need to know the total probability π sums to. The ratio automatically biases you toward high-probability states without ever computing that sum.
2Proposal distribution geometry
step size σ in q(x_new|x_old) = Normal(x_old, σ²I)
The proposal q determines how far each random walk step goes. If σ is too small, you take tiny steps and explore very slowly (high correlation between consecutive samples). If σ is too big, most moves get rejected and you stay stuck (also high correlation). The right σ makes your acceptance rate around 20–40%, balancing exploration against wasted rejected steps. You tune this knob to make the walk actually cover the space you're trying to sample from.
3Burn-in and transient removal
discard first M samples, use samples M+1, M+2, … after chain equilibrates
When you start the walk, its position is arbitrary and doesn't yet reflect the target spread. For the first M steps, the chain hasn't 'warmed up,' so those samples are biased and should be thrown away. M is chosen large enough that the chain forgets its starting point—typically 10% to 50% of the total run. The remaining samples then come from the true spread, in proportion to their probability.
4Autocorrelation and thinning
keep every k-th sample (x_0, x_k, x_2k, …), effective sample size ≈ N_raw / (1 + 2*ρ_lag)
Samples from a random walk are not independent—step i is correlated with step i+1, i+2, and so on, decaying slowly. This means N raw samples give you fewer independent pieces of information than N would if they were uncorrelated. You can either discard samples (keep every k-th one) to break correlation, or measure the autocorrelation function ρ_lag to estimate how many truly independent samples you actually have. Both reduce waste: either you use fewer raw steps to get the same independence, or you realize your run already gave you enough useful information.

What breaks without this

What goes wrong. Without sampling, some probability questions stay stuck. You may know what makes a state likely, but not the huge total needed to turn every score into an exact probability.

Why it goes wrong. In large spaces, adding up all possible states is often impossible. But you may still be able to compare two nearby states.

What this idea gives you. MCMC walks through the space so that common visits stand in for probability. It uses nearby comparisons to answer a question about the whole space.

The through-line. The core problem is drawing samples from a spread you can evaluate pointwise but cannot invert: you need to get many independent-looking samples spread across the probability landscape in proportion to their true probabilities, without ever having to compute the global normalizing constant that makes the probabilities sum to one.

Two plain ideas carry it: take a random walk that judges each step by a probability ratio of the two nearby spots (so the normalizer cancels), and run it long enough that the time it spends at each state converges to that state's true probability, asymptotically washing away dependence on the starting point and correlating only with the immediate past.

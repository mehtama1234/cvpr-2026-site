The problem, as a quantity

Any sequence has a joint probability p(x₁, x₂, …, x_n). To generate it we need to sample from that joint. But the joint is high-dimensional and entangled. The chain rule of probability offers a way out: factor the joint into a product of conditionals, each depending only on what came before:

p(x) = p(x₁) · p(x₂|x₁) · p(x₃|x₁,x₂) · … = ∏_i p(x_i | x_

This factorization is exact—no approximation. But it unlocks a generation strategy: sample left-to-right, one token at a time, feeding each choice back as context for the next. Training becomes a sum of log-probabilities of true next tokens given true prefixes (teacher forcing).

The price: generation is now sequential and fragile. Errors in early tokens drift forward and compound. A model trained on truth can fail when it sees its own mistakes during decoding.

The core ideas — and why they work

Chain rule factorization

foundation
The chain rule guarantees that any joint p(x₁, …, x_n) equals ∏_i p(x_i | x_x_ and model it with a causal transformer that masks future tokens. The model never sees the answer to its own prediction task at training time. This structure makes the problem tractable: one supervised classification problem per position, solvable with cross-entropy.

Teacher forcing and exposure bias

training-generation gap
At training, feed the model true prefixes and predict the true next token: L = Σ_i log p(x_i | x_its own sampled outputs, not ground truth. Early mistakes go unobserved during training. Small errors become amplified: wrong token at position t becomes noisy context for t+1, pushing the conditional further from training distribution. Exposure bias: model has never learned to recover from mistakes.

Sequential generation as bottleneck

speed
Sampling one token per forward pass means n forward passes for a length-n sequence. With long horizon (video, code, long-form text) this is slow. Parallel decoding schemes (drafting, speculative, iterative refinement) break the sequential constraint by predicting multiple tokens or different levels of structure at once, sacrificing the exact left-to-right factorization for speed.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Coarse-to-fine: Generate a low-res or high-level summary first (rough shape, key points), then iteratively refine each region or detail, conditioned on coarser structure. Trades sequential full-resolution generation for hierarchical steps. Escaping one-token-at-a-time: Parallel decoding (medusa heads, draft-verify), non-autoregressive decode (discrete diffusion, iterative masking), or multi-token lookahead (SpecDecode). Predict 2–many tokens in one step, losing the exact chain-rule guarantee but gaining speed. Unify read and write: Instead of sampling then feeding back, learn to condition on a sketch or plan (separate from generation). Reasoning models add thought tokens; planning models roll out coarse action sequences then detail them. Roll out fast: Use smaller models to draft, large model to verify; or use cached key-value tensors to avoid recomputing prefixes on each step.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

1Coarse thumbnail, then finer
p(x) = ∏_s p(x_s | x_<s, Markov(s)) where Markov(s) ≪ full history; next-scale prediction shortens conditioning window from all prior scales to dominant coarse + local context only.
Standard AR over all scales suffers O(S²) dependencies; Markovian factorization cuts this to O(S) by observing that fine details are locally correlated to their immediate coarser context, not the entire history. Papers trade full conditioning for speed by exploiting scale-locality in vision.
2Escaping the one-token-at-a-time bottleneck
p(grid) ≈ ∏_spatial ∏_order p(x_i | neighbors), or p(x) = flow(x | latent codes) where latent marginalizes batch-dimension correlations; Jacobi/flow bypass strict left-to-right token order.
Standard AR processes one location at a time in a fixed order, creating a sequential wall. Parallel decoding and normalizing flows exploit 2D/3D spatial correlation structure: nearby pixels are predictable jointly, so you can guess multiple positions in parallel or model continuous distributions directly without discretization overhead.
3Where drift is deadliest
p(v_1..T) → predict next framerate τ_t instead of next pixel; or p(x) = local_piece · global_assembly where local generations are short + stitched; or condition on semantic face not raw coordinates: p(face_params | context).
Long sequences amplify compounding error (exposure bias × length). Switching representation—framerate, local chunks, semantic face-space—radically shrinks the factorization chain length. Each strategy reduces the number of autoregressive steps before information must propagate globally, lowering drift accumulation.
4Read and write in one model, and stop the drift
p(mixed | history) = unified_decode_only_transformer(raw_patches + text + past) where encoder-free latent merges perception + generation; or p_gen(image | reasoning_tokens ∘ image_context) where reasoning interleaves predictions.
Separate vision encoders and tokenizers create a bottleneck and mismatch between training (ground-truth input) and test (model predictions). Decoder-only unified models condition generation on the same representation they learned to produce, closing the exposure-bias loop. Interleaved reasoning lets the model correct its own predictions mid-sequence.
5Write the action like the next word
p(action_seq) = ∏_t p(a_t | obs_t, action_history); where action_tokenizer(continuous_motion) → discrete codes smoothly preserving dynamics; chunked actions τ_a reduce AR steps: ∏_chunk p(chunk | history).
Robot trajectories are continuous; treating them as language tokens naively creates jitter. Smooth action tokenizers and action chunking discretization map continuous control into a token stream that preserves temporal structure, so autoregressive prediction over actions behaves like predicting motion primitives, not pixel tokens.
6Roll out the plan, but fast
p(trajectory) = decoder_language_model(scene_tokens + progress); or ∏_i p(agent_i | joint_obs) where closed-loop rolls forward one prediction at a time; or ∏_chunk p(action_chunk | state) where chunk decoding replaces per-step AR.
Planning trajectories or multi-agent rollouts one step at a time incurs latency and drift. Casting planning as language modeling (scene→trajectory like prompt→completion) and chunking actions both reduce AR iterations. Closed-loop multi-agent simulation reuses the same autoregressive policy repeatedly, amortizing computation cost.
The through-line. The chain rule is exact—any joint factors into left-to-right conditionals. But it forces sequential generation: each step is a classification task given all prior choices. Errors drift forward, and the model never trained on its own mistakes. Faster and more robust generation breaks the strict left-to-right order through hierarchy, parallelism, or planning, at the cost of approximation or added components.

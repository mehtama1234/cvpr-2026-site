Autoregressive generation — why it works, mathematically

Write a sentence one word at a time, each word chosen from all the words so far. That is how these models make text, pictures, and sound — one piece at a time — and it explains both why they are so good and why generation is slow.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

Autoregression is the habit of making one choice, then using that choice to make the next one. Instead of trying to create a whole sentence, image token stream, or action list in one impossible jump, the model turns the job into a chain of small bets.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology enters when the chain must preserve a larger shape. A mesh, road, room layout, or molecule cannot be built as unrelated pieces. Each new piece has to keep the object connected in the right way, avoid impossible holes, and respect which parts touch.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

The same idea appears in language, music, time-series forecasting, planning, and code generation. A weather forecast, a melody, and a program all have the same basic problem: the next step depends on what has already happened.

Why Autoregressive generation is worth learning

It matters because many hard objects become manageable when ordered as a sequence. The weakness is drift: one bad early choice becomes context for later choices.

The key turn — the principle at work

The stuck point. A large object such as text, an image token grid, or a video cannot be predicted all at once without modeling how its parts depend on earlier parts.

The mathematical move. The principle is chain-rule factorization. A joint probability is broken into a product of next-step probabilities: each new piece is predicted from the pieces already made.

What changes. Generation becomes repeated conditional prediction. This makes huge outputs tractable, but mistakes can accumulate because each new step depends on the previous ones.

The problem, as a quantity

Making a whole picture, or a whole sentence, in one shot means choosing among an astronomically huge number of possible wholes — far too many to handle head-on. You need a way to break that one giant choice into small, manageable pieces without giving anything up.

There is an exact way to do it, and most of the behaviour of these models — their strength, their slowness, their tendency to drift — follows from that one choice and its consequences. The problem is to turn 'produce a whole' into 'produce the next piece given everything so far,' learn that next-piece step well, and then live with the fact that generating for real means feeding the model its own output, one piece after another, in strict order.

The core ideas — and why they work

Build the whole by predicting one piece at a time — exactly

p(x₁,…,x_n) = ∏ᵢ p(xᵢ | x₁,…,xᵢ₋₁) — an exact identity, no approximation
The chance of a whole — a whole sentence, a whole image — can be rewritten exactly as: the chance of the first piece, times the chance of the second given the first, times the chance of the third given the first two, and so on to the end. This is not an approximation or a modelling assumption; it is simply what it means for chances to chain together, true for any sequence at all.

What it buys is everything. One impossible choice among astronomically many wholes becomes a long line of small, ordinary choices: at each step the model only has to guess the next piece given everything before it. The entire craft collapses to learning that single next-piece guess as well as possible — and the same trained guesser handles any length by just being run more times.

Train on the true past, so every step can be learned at once

L = -Σᵢ log p(xᵢ | x₁*,…,xᵢ₋₁*) where x* = ground truth, not model samples
To train the next-piece guesser you show it real examples and reward it for placing high chance on the piece that actually came next. A subtle but important choice: when it is learning to predict piece five, do you feed it the model's own guesses for pieces one through four, or the true pieces one through four? You feed the true ones.

This has a big practical payoff. Because every step is handed the real, known past, all the steps of a training example can be checked at once in a single pass — you do not have to wait for the model to generate each piece before you can score the next. Training is fast and fully parallel across positions. But this convenience plants a hidden cost that only surfaces at generation time, when the true past is no longer available — the subject of a later idea.

One dial sets how boldly each piece is chosen

p_T(xᵢ | x&lt;ᵢ) = softmax(zᵢ / T), T→0: argmax, T→∞: uniform
At each step the model produces a spread of chances over the possible next pieces. Before you actually draw one, you can reshape that spread with a single dial.

Turn the dial toward zero and the spread sharpens to a spike: the model always takes its single most-likely piece — safe, but repetitive and flat. Turn it up and the spread flattens toward treating all pieces as nearly equally likely — surprising and varied, but increasingly prone to nonsense. In between lies a sweet spot where the output is both coherent and not robotic. The dial changes nothing the model learned; it only sets how much you are willing to gamble on the less-likely pieces at the moment of drawing each one — a boldness knob, tuned to taste.

Why small early mistakes snowball during generation

error at step t shifts p(x_t | x&lt;t); the shifted context then shifts p(x_{t+1} | x&lt;{t+1}), so error ∝ O(t) over the sequence
Here is the cost hidden in training on the true past. While learning, the model always saw a flawless history. But when it generates for real, it must feed on its own previous pieces — and if it produces one slightly-off piece, the next guess is now based on a history unlike any it trained on.

That off piece nudges the next piece a little further off, which makes the one after that worse still — small errors compound as the sequence runs, and output that started crisp can drift into garbled the longer it goes. The root is the mismatch between the two situations: always handed a perfect past while learning, but forced to live with its own imperfect past while generating. This gap is the built-in weakness of building a whole one piece at a time, and much practical work goes into softening it.

Why generation is unavoidably slow

cost = n × (one forward pass + KV-cache lookup), each step depends on all prior outputs → no parallelism
Training could check every step at once, but generation cannot. Each new piece depends on the pieces before it, so you must produce piece one, then use it to produce piece two, then use those to produce piece three — strictly in order, with no way to leap ahead.

Making a whole of n pieces therefore takes n full passes through the model, one after another, and no amount of parallel hardware can fold them together, because each step genuinely needs the previous step's output before it can begin. This is the standing price of the exact rewrite: the chain of next-piece guesses is perfect and lossless, but it forces generation to walk the sequence a single step at a time — which is why producing long outputs takes real time even on fast machines.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Autoregressive models turn a few dials on the same idea.

What a 'piece' is. A word, a small image patch, an audio snippet, or a whole coarse-then-fine layer of an image — bigger pieces mean fewer steps but a harder guess each time. How the past is fed at generation. Plainly one piece at a time, or with tricks that let the model draft several pieces and check them together to claw back some speed. How boldly pieces are drawn. The temperature dial, plus rules that forbid the rarest options, trade variety against coherence. How the training–generation gap is softened. Occasionally training on the model's own imperfect past so it learns to recover from its own mistakes. The thread never changes: factor a whole into a chain of next-piece guesses, and reckon with the difference between the perfect past seen in training and the self-made past faced at generation.

A worked numeric example

For three tokens, p(x)=p(x₁)p(x₂|x₁)p(x₃|x₁,x₂). If probabilities are 0.8, 0.7, and 0.9, the sequence probability is 0.504. Autoregression turns one impossible joint choice into a chain of smaller choices.

1Coarse thumbnail, then finer
p(x) = ∏_s p(x_s | x_<s, Markov(s)) where Markov(s) ≪ full history; next-scale prediction shortens conditioning window from all prior scales to dominant coarse + local context only.
Standard autoregression over all scales creates dependencies that scale quadratically with the number of scales: each scale looks back at all prior scales. Instead, use a Markovian factorization: each fine level only looks at the coarsest level above it plus nearby context at the same scale, not the entire history. This cuts dependencies from quadratic to linear. Papers in this family exploit the fact that fine details in images and other data cluster near their immediate coarser context, not scattered throughout. By training only on local coarse plus nearby same-scale context, generation runs much faster without losing quality.
These papers generate images or point clouds by starting with a low-resolution or low-detail version and progressively adding finer features. Each level of detail only needs to look at the coarse level above it and nearby context at the same scale, avoiding the quadratic blowup of having every fine detail look back at every coarse detail.

2Escaping the one-token-at-a-time bottleneck
p(grid) ≈ ∏_spatial ∏_order p(x_i | neighbors), or p(x) = flow(x | latent codes) where latent marginalizes batch-dimension correlations; Jacobi/flow bypass strict left-to-right token order.
Standard autoregression processes one location at a time in a fixed left-to-right order, creating a sequential wall: you must choose pixel 1, then pixel 2, then pixel 3, and so on. Parallel decoding methods exploit the fact that nearby pixels in a grid are predictable together: you can guess multiple positions at the same time using an iterative solver (Jacobi method), checking each guess against its neighbors and refining. Normalizing flows (smooth invertible transformations) go further: instead of discretizing space into tokens, they model continuous distributions directly, avoiding the token overhead entirely.
Standard autoregression processes one location or position at a time, creating a sequential wall. These papers exploit spatial or temporal structure to predict multiple positions in parallel using iterative solvers or continuous models, bypassing strict left-to-right ordering.

3Where drift is deadliest
p(v_1..T) → predict next framerate τ_t instead of next pixel; or p(x) = local_piece · global_assembly where local generations are short + stitched; or condition on semantic face not raw coordinates: p(face_params | context).
Long sequences compound errors: if each step has a small error and those errors cascade, the error grows roughly linearly with sequence length. When drift is dangerous — like in video, where one bad frame corrupts everything after it — switch the representation. Instead of predicting raw pixels step by step, predict the frame rate (how dense sampling is). Instead of one long sequence, generate short chunks and stitch them together. Instead of pixel coordinates, represent things at a higher level, like facial pose, where the space is smaller and errors cannot cascade as easily. Each strategy shrinks the number of autoregressive steps before information must propagate globally, reducing compounding error.
Long autoregressive sequences compound errors: one mistake early on corrupts everything that follows. These papers switch representations or factorizations to reduce the number of sequential steps, controlling how much error can accumulate.

4Read and write in one model, and stop the drift
p(mixed | history) = unified_decode_only_transformer(raw_patches + text + past) where encoder-free latent merges perception + generation; or p_gen(image | reasoning_tokens ∘ image_context) where reasoning interleaves predictions.
When you use a separate encoder (a fixed network that turns images into compressed codes) and then a separate generation network, you create a bottleneck: the encoder was trained on real images, but at generation time the generation network sees codes from its own predictions. This mismatch is a hidden form of exposure bias. Unified models (where the same network reads and writes in the same representation) avoid the mismatch: the generation network conditions on the same kind of codes it is learning to produce. Interleaved reasoning lets the model stop and think at any point, giving it a way to recover from mistakes before they compound.
Separate encoders create a mismatch: they were trained on real images, but at generation time they see codes from the model's own predictions. These papers unify reading and writing in one representation, avoiding the hidden exposure bias.

5Write the action like the next word
p(action_seq) = ∏_t p(a_t | obs_t, action_history); where action_tokenizer(continuous_motion) → discrete codes smoothly preserving dynamics; chunked actions τ_a reduce AR steps: ∏_chunk p(chunk | history).
Robot trajectories are continuous: joint angles smoothly vary over time. Treating a continuous trajectory as a sequence of discrete tokens naively creates jitter and breaks the smooth flow of motion. Smart action tokenizers (learned codes for motion) convert continuous control into a token stream that preserves the smooth structure: consecutive frames have similar or nearby tokens, so the autoregressive model predicts motion primitives, not individual jerky positions. Action chunking groups several timesteps into one token, so you predict motion chunks instead of position tokens, further shortening the autoregressive sequence.
Robot trajectories are continuous motion, but treating them as discrete tokens creates jitter. These papers tokenize continuous actions smoothly or in chunks, so autoregressive prediction captures motion primitives rather than individual jerky positions.

6Roll out the plan, but fast
p(trajectory) = decoder_language_model(scene_tokens + progress); or ∏_i p(agent_i | joint_obs) where closed-loop rolls forward one prediction at a time; or ∏_chunk p(action_chunk | state) where chunk decoding replaces per-step AR.
Planning trajectories or multi-agent rollouts one step at a time is slow and accumulates drift. You can recast trajectory planning as a language problem: the scene description plays the role of a prompt, and the trajectory plays the role of the completion. This lets you use language-model tools and training recipes for planning. Alternatively, divide actions into chunks and predict chunk by chunk instead of step by step, reducing the number of autoregressive iterations. Closed-loop multi-agent simulation (where each agent's prediction feeds back as input to the next step) reuses the same autoregressive policy repeatedly, spreading the cost across multiple uses.
Planning trajectories or multi-agent rollouts one step at a time is slow and drifts. These papers cast planning as language modeling, chunk actions, or reuse policies repeatedly, reducing the cost of sequential prediction.

The through-line. The core move is an exact rewrite: the chance of a whole is the product of next-piece chances, which turns one impossible giant choice into a chain of small, learnable ones. Training on the true past lets every step be learned at once and fast, at the cost that generation — fed on the model's own pieces — lets small errors snowball; a single dial trades boldness against coherence; and the very exactness forces generation to run strictly one step at a time. Two plain ideas carry it: factor a whole into a chain of next-piece guesses, and reckon honestly with the gap between the perfect past seen in training and the self-made past faced at generation.

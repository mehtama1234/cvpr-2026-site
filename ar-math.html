Autoregressive generation — why it works, mathematically

Factor the joint distribution into a left-to-right product of conditionals — p(x) = ∏ᵢ p(xᵢ | x<ᵢ) — turning one impossible global sample into n tractable single-step classifications. Exact, sequential, and drift-prone.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

Autoregression is the habit of making one choice, then using that choice to make the next one. Instead of trying to create a whole sentence, image token stream, or action list in one impossible jump, the model turns the job into a chain of small bets.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology enters when the chain must preserve a larger shape. A mesh, road, room layout, or molecule cannot be built as unrelated pieces. Each new piece has to keep the object connected in the right way, avoid impossible holes, and respect which parts touch.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

The same idea appears in language, music, time-series forecasting, planning, and code generation. A weather forecast, a melody, and a program all have the same basic problem: the next step depends on what has already happened.

Why Autoregressive generation is worth learning

It matters because many hard objects become manageable when ordered as a sequence. The weakness is drift: one bad early choice becomes context for later choices.

The problem, as a quantity

An image or a sequence is a joint distribution over thousands of entangled variables. You cannot sample them independently — every pixel or token must agree with all the others, so the space of coherent objects is a vanishingly thin slice of the full product space. Direct sampling from the joint is intractable: the joint has no closed form, and learning it without structure would require exponentially many examples.

The mathematical tension has two edges at once. First, the modelling edge: how do you parameterize a distribution over a thousand-dimensional space without an exponential number of parameters? Second, the training edge: how do you define a loss that pushes the model toward the right joint without ever being able to evaluate the joint directly?

The chain rule of probability dissolves both edges in one move — but introduces a third: sequential generation that accumulates errors forward through time.

The core ideas — and why they work

Factor the joint by the chain rule

p(x₁,…,x_n) = ∏ᵢ p(xᵢ | x₁,…,xᵢ₋₁) — an exact identity, no approximation
The chain rule of probability is a mathematical identity that holds for any joint distribution. When you repeat the rule that says 'p(A and B) equals p(A given B) times p(B)', you can break down any joint distribution into a product of conditionals. This works because probability is just a counting rule over outcomes, and you can always rearrange that count by peeling off one variable at a time from left to right. What makes autoregression powerful is that this identity transforms one unsolvable global problem — generate from p(x₁,…,x_n) — into n simple local problems: at each step, pick which token comes next using only a fixed context. A causal transformer (a network where each position can only look at earlier positions) handles the context with a single attention pass, and the training loss is just the sum of the individual prediction errors: L = -Σᵢ log p(xᵢ | x<ᵢ).

Teacher forcing — parallel training on sequential data

L = -Σᵢ log p(xᵢ | x₁*,…,xᵢ₋₁*) where x* = ground truth, not model samples
At training time, you know the entire true sequence x*. You can evaluate every conditional p(xᵢ | x<ᵢ*) all at once — all positions in one pass through the network — because each position's context is the true prefix, not one the model made up. This is called teacher forcing: you feed the model the ground truth at every step during training. The speed gain is real: what looks like a sequential generation process becomes a fully-parallel supervised prediction task where all positions are trained simultaneously. The hidden cost is serious: the model never sees its own errors during training. Every context it conditions on comes from the real data; when generating, every context comes from the model's own output, which starts wrong and gets worse. This gap — between contexts from the data during training and contexts from the model during generation — is called exposure bias. The model learns to predict perfectly when all the past is correct; it then operates in a world where the past is whatever it chose, possibly wrong.

Temperature — sharpness of the conditional at each step

p_T(xᵢ | x&lt;ᵢ) = softmax(zᵢ / T), T→0: argmax, T→∞: uniform
At each step the model outputs a raw score for each token in the vocabulary — think of it like a list of 'match strengths'. To turn these raw scores into probabilities (numbers between 0 and 1 that sum to 1), apply softmax: turn scores into shares that add up to 1. Dividing by a temperature T before softmax changes how concentrated the distribution is. When T is very small, the single largest score dominates and the distribution collapses to a single token (take the top choice every time). When T is very large, all scores become nearly equal and the distribution becomes uniform (each token equally likely). Temperature is not just a cosmetic adjustment — it controls how much uncertainty the distribution has at every step, and since each step feeds into the next, a small change in T affects the entire sequence. Low temperature produces predictable outputs because the model always picks its most confident guess, but these outputs often repeat; high temperature produces varied outputs but they often make no sense because randomness early on cascades forward. The optimal value is usually below 1, where the model's confidence scores map most accurately to reality.

Exposure bias — the compounding gap between training and generation

error at step t shifts p(x_t | x&lt;t); the shifted context then shifts p(x_{t+1} | x&lt;{t+1}), so error ∝ O(t) over the sequence
At training, each position gets a context (the true prefix). At generation, each position gets a context made entirely from the model's own output. Even if the model is nearly perfect — making an error only once per thousand steps — the context at step t is wrong with probability roughly εt (where ε is the error rate per step). A wrong context shifts what the model predicts at the next step, introducing a new error, which further corrupts the context after that. Errors compound. This is not a bug in any particular implementation — it is a structural property of the chain rule: a left-to-right chain has no way for a later step to reach back and fix an earlier one. The model is exactly right for contexts from the data, and progressively worse at contexts from itself. Any method that tries to close this gap — like mixing in model-generated contexts during training, or training on rewards from the model's own outputs, or the dataset aggregation technique DAgger — works by forcing the model to experience and handle what it will actually see.

Sequential decoding — the O(n) generation wall

cost = n × (one forward pass + KV-cache lookup), each step depends on all prior outputs → no parallelism
The chain rule says that token t must be decided before token t+1 can be predicted, because the prediction for step t+1 depends on what was chosen at step t. This is not a flaw in how a system is built — it comes directly from the math. You literally cannot evaluate p(xₜ | x<ₜ) until x₁,…,xₜ₋₁ are already chosen. So generating a sequence of length n requires exactly n steps executed one after another: you cannot run them in parallel. A key-value cache (storing the network's memory for all prior tokens so you do not have to recompute it) reduces the cost per step from O(n) to O(1), but does not eliminate the fact that you must run n steps. Every trick that breaks this wall — like having a fast model draft k tokens and a slow model verify them all at once, or predicting multiple future tokens simultaneously, or using a model that outputs all tokens at once instead of one by one — is trading away the mathematical exactness of the chain rule for speed. There is no magic: the chain rule gives you a perfect log-likelihood but forces you to generate sequentially.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Every variant in this family chooses where to apply the chain-rule factorization and how to handle the sequential cost. Coarse-to-fine (hierarchical AR): factorize at two levels — first generate a rough sketch, then fill in fine details that know the sketch. This turns one long sequential chain into two shorter ones. Speculative decoding: a small model proposes k tokens quickly; the big model checks all k in one parallel pass and accepts or rejects each one. The chain-rule identity holds; only the wall-clock time changes. Scheduled sampling and DAgger: during training, sometimes feed the model its own output instead of the ground truth, so it learns to handle its own mistakes before deployment. Non-autoregressive decoding: predict all tokens at the same time, then refine them iteratively. Loses the exact chain rule but gains full parallelism — a direct speed-for-exactness trade.

A worked numeric example

For three tokens, p(x)=p(x₁)p(x₂|x₁)p(x₃|x₁,x₂). If probabilities are 0.8, 0.7, and 0.9, the sequence probability is 0.504. Autoregression turns one impossible joint choice into a chain of smaller choices.

1Coarse thumbnail, then finer
p(x) = ∏_s p(x_s | x_<s, Markov(s)) where Markov(s) ≪ full history; next-scale prediction shortens conditioning window from all prior scales to dominant coarse + local context only.
Standard autoregression over all scales creates dependencies that scale quadratically with the number of scales: each scale looks back at all prior scales. Instead, use a Markovian factorization: each fine level only looks at the coarsest level above it plus nearby context at the same scale, not the entire history. This cuts dependencies from quadratic to linear. Papers in this family exploit the fact that fine details in images and other data cluster near their immediate coarser context, not scattered throughout. By training only on local coarse plus nearby same-scale context, generation runs much faster without losing quality.
These papers generate images or point clouds by starting with a low-resolution or low-detail version and progressively adding finer features. Each level of detail only needs to look at the coarse level above it and nearby context at the same scale, avoiding the quadratic blowup of having every fine detail look back at every coarse detail.

2Escaping the one-token-at-a-time bottleneck
p(grid) ≈ ∏_spatial ∏_order p(x_i | neighbors), or p(x) = flow(x | latent codes) where latent marginalizes batch-dimension correlations; Jacobi/flow bypass strict left-to-right token order.
Standard autoregression processes one location at a time in a fixed left-to-right order, creating a sequential wall: you must choose pixel 1, then pixel 2, then pixel 3, and so on. Parallel decoding methods exploit the fact that nearby pixels in a grid are predictable together: you can guess multiple positions at the same time using an iterative solver (Jacobi method), checking each guess against its neighbors and refining. Normalizing flows (smooth invertible transformations) go further: instead of discretizing space into tokens, they model continuous distributions directly, avoiding the token overhead entirely.

3Where drift is deadliest
p(v_1..T) → predict next framerate τ_t instead of next pixel; or p(x) = local_piece · global_assembly where local generations are short + stitched; or condition on semantic face not raw coordinates: p(face_params | context).
Long sequences compound errors: if each step has a small error and those errors cascade, the error grows roughly linearly with sequence length. When drift is dangerous — like in video, where one bad frame corrupts everything after it — switch the representation. Instead of predicting raw pixels step by step, predict the frame rate (how dense sampling is). Instead of one long sequence, generate short chunks and stitch them together. Instead of pixel coordinates, represent things at a higher level, like facial pose, where the space is smaller and errors cannot cascade as easily. Each strategy shrinks the number of autoregressive steps before information must propagate globally, reducing compounding error.
Long autoregressive sequences compound errors: one mistake early on corrupts everything that follows. These papers switch representations or factorizations to reduce the number of sequential steps, controlling how much error can accumulate.

4Read and write in one model, and stop the drift
p(mixed | history) = unified_decode_only_transformer(raw_patches + text + past) where encoder-free latent merges perception + generation; or p_gen(image | reasoning_tokens ∘ image_context) where reasoning interleaves predictions.
When you use a separate encoder (a fixed network that turns images into compressed codes) and then a separate generation network, you create a bottleneck: the encoder was trained on real images, but at generation time the generation network sees codes from its own predictions. This mismatch is a hidden form of exposure bias. Unified models (where the same network reads and writes in the same representation) avoid the mismatch: the generation network conditions on the same kind of codes it is learning to produce. Interleaved reasoning lets the model stop and think at any point, giving it a way to recover from mistakes before they compound.
Separate encoders create a mismatch: they were trained on real images, but at generation time they see codes from the model's own predictions. These papers unify reading and writing in one representation, avoiding the hidden exposure bias.

5Write the action like the next word
p(action_seq) = ∏_t p(a_t | obs_t, action_history); where action_tokenizer(continuous_motion) → discrete codes smoothly preserving dynamics; chunked actions τ_a reduce AR steps: ∏_chunk p(chunk | history).
Robot trajectories are continuous: joint angles smoothly vary over time. Treating a continuous trajectory as a sequence of discrete tokens naively creates jitter and breaks the smooth flow of motion. Smart action tokenizers (learned codes for motion) convert continuous control into a token stream that preserves the smooth structure: consecutive frames have similar or nearby tokens, so the autoregressive model predicts motion primitives, not individual jerky positions. Action chunking groups several timesteps into one token, so you predict motion chunks instead of position tokens, further shortening the autoregressive sequence.
Robot trajectories are continuous motion, but treating them as discrete tokens creates jitter. These papers tokenize continuous actions smoothly or in chunks, so autoregressive prediction captures motion primitives rather than individual jerky positions.

6Roll out the plan, but fast
p(trajectory) = decoder_language_model(scene_tokens + progress); or ∏_i p(agent_i | joint_obs) where closed-loop rolls forward one prediction at a time; or ∏_chunk p(action_chunk | state) where chunk decoding replaces per-step AR.
Planning trajectories or multi-agent rollouts one step at a time is slow and accumulates drift. You can recast trajectory planning as a language problem: the scene description plays the role of a prompt, and the trajectory plays the role of the completion. This lets you use language-model tools and training recipes for planning. Alternatively, divide actions into chunks and predict chunk by chunk instead of step by step, reducing the number of autoregressive iterations. Closed-loop multi-agent simulation (where each agent's prediction feeds back as input to the next step) reuses the same autoregressive policy repeatedly, spreading the cost across multiple uses.

The through-line. The chain rule of probability is an exact algebraic identity, not a modeling assumption — autoregression is the choice to use it all the way: parameterize every conditional, train all of them at once using ground truth context (teacher forcing), and sample left-to-right. That commitment gives you a mathematically exact likelihood, a training objective that is stable and simple, and full compatibility with all language-model infrastructure built in the past decade. But the same commitment locks you into two structural costs: exposure bias (training always sees true context, generation never does) and sequential decoding (each step depends on the last, so generation cannot be sped up by parallelism). The whole research field — scheduled sampling, speculative decoding, hierarchical generation, distillation into parallel models — is trying to keep the exactness of the chain rule while routing around one or both costs. The temperature dial, the key-value cache that reuses computations, the softmax at each step that turns scores into probabilities — these are all engineering choices inside the same mathematical frame. And that frame is shared: normalizing flows use the chain rule to factorize probability distributions, world models use it to factor sequences of world states, video generation uses it to factor frames — each finds its own way to turn a global structure into a tractable left-to-right pass.

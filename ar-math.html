Autoregressive generation — why it works, mathematically

Factor the joint distribution into a left-to-right product of conditionals — p(x) = ∏ᵢ p(xᵢ | x<ᵢ) — turning one impossible global sample into n tractable single-step classifications. Exact, sequential, and drift-prone.

The problem, as a quantity

An image or a sequence is a joint distribution over thousands of entangled variables. You cannot sample them independently — every pixel or token must agree with all the others, so the space of coherent objects is a vanishingly thin slice of the full product space. Direct sampling from the joint is intractable: the joint has no closed form, and learning it without structure would require exponentially many examples.

The mathematical tension has two edges at once. First, the modelling edge: how do you parameterize a distribution over a thousand-dimensional space without an exponential number of parameters? Second, the training edge: how do you define a loss that pushes the model toward the right joint without ever being able to evaluate the joint directly?

The chain rule of probability dissolves both edges in one move — but introduces a third: sequential generation that accumulates errors forward through time.

The core ideas — and why they work

Factor the joint by the chain rule

p(x₁,…,x_n) = ∏ᵢ p(xᵢ | x₁,…,xᵢ₋₁) — an exact identity, no approximation
The chain rule of probability is not a modelling choice — it is an algebraic identity that holds for any joint distribution, always. Repeated application of the definition p(A,B) = p(A|B)·p(B) decomposes the joint into a product of conditionals. The universe permits this because probability is just a measure over outcomes, and any measure factors left-to-right. What makes autoregression powerful is that this identity collapses a single intractable global problem — sample from p(x₁,…,x_n) — into n tractable local problems: at each step, classify which token comes next given a fixed context. A causal transformer handles the context with a single masked self-attention pass, and the training loss is simply the sum of cross-entropies: L = -Σᵢ log p(xᵢ | x<ᵢ). Same primitive elsewhere: this is the Factorize the joint by the chain rule primitive — the identical decomposition drives normalizing flows (where each factor is an invertible function), world models (where each step conditions on a compressed state), and video generation (where frames are conditioned on all prior frames). Every method that converts a global structure into a left-to-right pass is using this same identity.

Teacher forcing — parallel training on sequential data

L = -Σᵢ log p(xᵢ | x₁*,…,xᵢ₋₁*) where x* = ground truth, not model samples
At training time, you know the entire true sequence x*. You can therefore evaluate every conditional p(xᵢ | x<ᵢ*) simultaneously — all positions in one forward pass — because the context at each step is the true prefix, not a sampled one. This is called teacher forcing, and it is why autoregressive training is so efficient: what looks like a sequential generative process becomes a fully-parallelizable supervised classification task. The efficiency is real. The cost is hidden: the model never sees its own mistakes during training. Every context it conditions on is drawn from the data distribution; at generation time, every context is drawn from the model's own distribution, which is subtly different from day one and diverges further with each step. This gap — between the training distribution over contexts and the generation distribution over contexts — is exposure bias. The model is optimally tuned for a world where the past is always correct; it then operates in a world where the past is whatever it chose. The enemy it defeats: this is the same tension that arises whenever you train on demonstrations but deploy with self-generated rollouts — the same gap that DAgger (dataset aggregation) and RLHF (training on the model's own outputs) are designed to close.

Temperature — sharpness of the conditional at each step

p_T(xᵢ | x&lt;ᵢ) = softmax(zᵢ / T), T→0: argmax, T→∞: uniform
At each step the model emits a vector of logits zᵢ — one raw score per token in the vocabulary. To turn these into a probability distribution, apply softmax. Dividing by a temperature T before softmax rescales how peaked the distribution is. When T → 0, the largest logit dominates infinitely and the distribution collapses onto a single token (greedy decoding). When T → ∞, all logits become equal and the distribution is uniform. Temperature is not a cosmetic knob — it controls the entropy of the conditional at every step, and because each step feeds back into the next, a small change in T compounds across all n steps. Low temperature produces coherent but repetitive outputs because the model always picks its most confident guess; high temperature produces diverse but incoherent outputs because noise in early steps cascades forward. The sweet spot is typically not T=1 but slightly below, where the model's calibration is best. Same primitive elsewhere: this is the Softmax primitive — turning a set of scores into a probability over discrete choices. The identical operation runs inside attention (to weight values), inside mixture-of-experts (to route tokens), and inside CLIP (to select matching pairs). Every time the field needs a differentiable argmax, it reaches for the same softmax.

Exposure bias — the compounding gap between training and generation

error at step t shifts p(x_t | x&lt;t); the shifted context then shifts p(x_{t+1} | x&lt;{t+1}), so error ∝ O(t) over the sequence
At training, the context fed to position t is the true prefix x₁*,…,xₜ₋₁*. At generation, the context is the model's own output x̂₁,…,x̂ₜ₋₁. Even if the model is almost perfect — say it makes a small mistake with probability ε per step — the context at step t is wrong with probability roughly 1-(1-ε)^t ≈ εt. The model was never trained to recover from such contexts, so the wrong context shifts the conditional distribution, which produces a slightly wrong token, which further corrupts the next context. The error compounds. This is not just a practical concern — it is a structural consequence of the factorization: a left-to-right chain has no mechanism for a later step to correct an earlier one. The boundary is sharp: the model is exactly optimal for contexts drawn from the data, and increasingly suboptimal for contexts drawn from itself. Methods that try to close this gap — scheduled sampling (mix in model outputs during training), RLHF (train on rewards from model rollouts), or DAgger — all work by injecting model-generated contexts into training so the model learns to handle what it will actually see.

Sequential decoding — the O(n) generation wall

cost = n × (one forward pass + KV-cache lookup), each step depends on all prior outputs → no parallelism
The chain rule factorization demands that token t be sampled before token t+1 can be conditioned on it. This is not a flaw in the implementation — it is baked into the math. The conditional p(xₜ | x<ₜ) literally cannot be evaluated until x₁,…,xₜ₋₁ are known. So generating a sequence of length n requires exactly n sequential forward passes: you cannot parallelize them the way training parallelizes over positions. A key-value cache (storing attention keys and values for all prior tokens so they need not be recomputed) reduces the per-step cost from O(n) to O(1) in recomputation, but does not change the fundamental n-step sequential dependency. Every method that breaks this wall — speculative decoding (draft fast, verify in parallel), parallel decoding (predict multiple future tokens at once), or switching to a non-autoregressive model (predict all tokens simultaneously, lose the exact chain-rule guarantee) — is trading the exactness of the factorization for speed. There is no free lunch: the chain rule gives you an exact log-likelihood at the cost of sequential generation.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Every variant in this family is a choice about where to apply the chain-rule factorization and how to handle the sequential cost. Coarse-to-fine (hierarchical AR): factorize at two levels — first generate a low-resolution summary, then condition the fine-detail tokens on both the summary and their local left context. This replaces one long sequential chain with two shorter ones. Speculative decoding: a small draft model proposes k tokens in one step; the big model verifies all k in a single parallel forward pass and accepts or rejects each. The chain-rule identity still holds; only the wall-clock cost changes. Scheduled sampling and DAgger: inject model-generated prefixes during training so the model conditions on its own distribution, closing the exposure-bias gap at the cost of making training slightly non-stationary. Non-autoregressive decoding: predict all tokens simultaneously, then iteratively refine with masked diffusion or parallel refinement steps. Loses the exact factorization but gains full parallelism — a direct trade between exactness and speed.

A worked numeric example

For three tokens, p(x)=p(x₁)p(x₂|x₁)p(x₃|x₁,x₂). If probabilities are 0.8, 0.7, and 0.9, the sequence probability is 0.504. Autoregression turns one impossible joint choice into a chain of smaller choices.

Standard AR over all scales suffers O(S²) dependencies; Markovian factorization cuts this to O(S) by observing that fine details are locally correlated to their immediate coarser context, not the entire history. Papers trade full conditioning for speed by exploiting scale-locality in vision.

2Escaping the one-token-at-a-time bottleneck

Standard AR processes one location at a time in a fixed order, creating a sequential wall. Parallel decoding and normalizing flows exploit 2D/3D spatial correlation structure: nearby pixels are predictable jointly, so you can guess multiple positions in parallel or model continuous distributions directly without discretization overhead.

STARFlow-V: End-to-End Video Generative Modeling with Autoregressive Normalizing Flows — Splits flow per temporal frame: models video as joint factorization across time and flow dimensions simultaneously.
3Where drift is deadliest
p(v_1..T) → predict next framerate τ_t instead of next pixel; or p(x) = local_piece · global_assembly where local generations are short + stitched; or condition on semantic face not raw coordinates: p(face_params | context).
Long sequences amplify compounding error (exposure bias × length). Switching representation—framerate, local chunks, semantic face-space—radically shrinks the factorization chain length. Each strategy reduces the number of autoregressive steps before information must propagate globally, lowering drift accumulation.
TempoMaster: Efficient Long Video Generation via Next-Frame-Rate Prediction — Predicts frame rate (fps) not pixels: adapts sampling density per timestep, concentrating detail where motion is high.

4Read and write in one model, and stop the drift

Separate vision encoders and tokenizers create a bottleneck and mismatch between training (ground-truth input) and test (model predictions). Decoder-only unified models condition generation on the same representation they learned to produce, closing the exposure-bias loop. Interleaved reasoning lets the model correct its own predictions mid-sequence.

5Write the action like the next word

Robot trajectories are continuous; treating them as language tokens naively creates jitter. Smooth action tokenizers and action chunking discretization map continuous control into a token stream that preserves temporal structure, so autoregressive prediction over actions behaves like predicting motion primitives, not pixel tokens.
ICRT: In-Context Imitation Learning via Next-Token Prediction — Treats (obs, action) tuples as tokens in a causal LM: P(a_t | obs_1, a_1, ..., obs_t) via standard transformer softmax.

Quart-Online: Latency-Free Multimodal Large Language Model for Quadrupedal Locomotion — Action Chunk Discretization: groups T timesteps into one discrete action token, compressing motion into fixed vocabulary.

6Roll out the plan, but fast
p(trajectory) = decoder_language_model(scene_tokens + progress); or ∏_i p(agent_i | joint_obs) where closed-loop rolls forward one prediction at a time; or ∏_chunk p(action_chunk | state) where chunk decoding replaces per-step AR.
Planning trajectories or multi-agent rollouts one step at a time incurs latency and drift. Casting planning as language modeling (scene→trajectory like prompt→completion) and chunking actions both reduce AR iterations. Closed-loop multi-agent simulation reuses the same autoregressive policy repeatedly, amortizing computation cost.

The through-line. The chain rule of probability is an exact algebraic identity, not a modelling assumption — and autoregression is the decision to trust it all the way: parameterize every conditional, train on all of them at once with teacher forcing, and sample left-to-right. That purity gives you an exact log-likelihood, a stable training objective, and full compatibility with everything the language-model world has built. But the same purity locks you into two structural costs: exposure bias (the training context is always true, the generation context never is) and sequential decoding (each step depends on the last, so you cannot parallelize generation). The whole research frontier — scheduled sampling, speculative decoding, hierarchical generation, distillation into parallel models — is the field trying to keep the exactness of the chain rule while routing around one or both of those costs. The softmax at each step, the KV-cache that amortizes recomputation, the temperature dial that trades entropy for coherence — these are all engineering moves inside the same fixed mathematical frame. And that frame is shared: the chain-rule factorization is the same primitive that normalizing flows, world models, and video generation all build on, each in their own way trying to turn a global structure into a tractable left-to-right pass.

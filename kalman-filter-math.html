Kalman filtering & state estimation — why it works, mathematically

A robot's GPS tells you it's at position 100 meters, but the sensor is unreliable — the real position could be anywhere in a messy cloud. The Kalman filter is the trick that uses both what the model predicts and what the sensor just said to sharpen that cloud into the single best guess — moment by moment.

The problem, as a quantity

Imagine a delivery robot navigating a warehouse using GPS. Every second, the GPS spits out a position reading — but GPS is noisy. The real position is hidden; all you see is a measurement corrupted by random glitches. You need to know where the robot actually is, not just what the sensor guesses.

Your only tools are two imperfect sources: a model that predicts where the robot should go next (based on physics and the last position), and a fresh sensor reading that you know is contaminated by noise. Neither one is trustworthy on its own. The sensor might drift. The model might have lag. But together, playing them off each other, you can build something better than either alone.

The Kalman filter is the method that automatically balances these two sources at every step, using them to maintain a running best guess and track how confident that guess is. It answers a simple but powerful question: given what you predicted and what you just measured, what should you believe right now?

The core ideas — and why they work

Track a hidden state you only see through noisy glimpses

hidden state x_t; you only observe y_t = x_t + noise
The core problem is that the real thing you want to know — the position, the temperature, the price — is hidden. You never see it directly. All you get is a measurement, which is the true value plus garbage. That garbage is noise: random errors baked into every reading by the sensor itself or by interference.

This noise is not a small mistake you can ignore. It is real, and it is everywhere. A GPS reading might be off by 10 meters. A thermometer might jitter by a full degree. When you use that measurement to update your belief, you are using corrupted information. The Kalman filter solves this by never trusting the measurement alone. Instead, it carries two things: a best guess for the hidden state, and a measure of how uncertain that guess is.

That uncertainty number is the key. It tells the filter how much the noise is expected to be, and it decides, at every moment, whether to lean more on the model's prediction or more on the new measurement. The hidden state stays hidden — you never learn it for certain — but you can build an ever-better guess.

Predict forward, then correct with the new measurement

predict: x̂ ← A·x̂; update: x̂ ← x̂ + K·(y − x̂)
At every time step, the filter does two things. First, it predicts: it takes the current best guess and runs it through the model to see where things should go next. The model is a rule, often simple — "the position follows the velocity" or "tomorrow's temperature is close to today's" — and it is baked into a table of numbers called A. Multiply the current guess by A and you get the prediction.

Then a new measurement arrives. It is corrupted, but it is fresh information. The filter compares the prediction to the measurement. The difference, or error, is called the innovation: if the model predicted 100 meters but the sensor says 110 meters, the innovation is 10. This innovation is your wake-up call. Something went wrong — either the model drifted or the sensor had a glitch.

Now comes the balancing act. The filter adjusts the prediction in the direction of the measurement, but not all the way. It moves by a fraction of the innovation. That fraction is determined by a gain called K, the Kalman gain. If K is 0.5, you move halfway to the measurement. If K is 0.1, you barely budge. That gain is not chosen by hand — it is calculated from the uncertainties so that the balance is always optimal.

Weigh prediction against measurement by which you trust more

K leans toward the sensor when the model is unsure, and vice-versa
The Kalman gain K is the automatic knob that decides how much to trust the new measurement versus sticking with the prediction. It is not a constant; it changes at every step based on the uncertainties.

Here is the logic: if the model is very trustworthy and the sensor is very noisy, then K should be small — you move only a little toward the measurement and mostly stick with the prediction. If the sensor is very clean and the model is unreliable, then K should be large — you move a lot toward the measurement. The Kalman filter computes K from two numbers: the uncertainty in the model and the uncertainty in the sensor. This calculation is built into the math, so the gain adjusts itself automatically as the world changes.

This is why the filter is so powerful. You do not have to guess the right balance. The filter figures it out from the data and the assumptions you give it. If the sensor starts to fail, K will shrink. If the model breaks down, K will grow. The filter adapts.

Track your own uncertainty, not just the best guess

carry a spread Σ: it grows on predict, shrinks on update
The filter does not just carry a single number — the best guess. It also carries the spread, or uncertainty, around that guess. Think of it as a range of possibilities, centered on the best guess, that represents how confident you are. A narrow spread means you are confident; a wide spread means you are unsure.

This uncertainty follows a simple rule: predictions make it wider, measurements make it narrower. When you run the prediction step, you are using the model, which is not perfect. The model might miss something or have hidden factors. So the uncertainty grows — your confidence drops. You are less sure where you will be next than where you are now.

Then the measurement arrives. It is noisy, but it is real data about the current state. Using that data to update your guess shrinks the uncertainty — you become more confident. The spread narrows. Over time, if the model and sensor are both reasonable, the uncertainty settles into a steady state, bouncing between growing on prediction and shrinking on update.

This uncertainty number is what drives K. The Kalman gain is built from the current uncertainties, so as your confidence changes, K changes with it. A system that tracks its own confidence can adjust its behavior automatically.

The approaches, as variations on the math

The Kalman filter is one idea, but it lives in a family of variants. Change the model — add acceleration, or non-linear physics — and you have to rethink the math. Add multiple sensors (GPS and odometry together) and the filter fuses them all into one belief. Swap the noise assumptions (sensors have different noise than you thought) and the gains recalibrate. Run it offline on recorded data versus live on streaming data and the flow changes — offline, you can run the filter backward too, to refine your whole history at once.

The thread never changes: carry a best guess and an uncertainty, predict forward, correct with new data, update the uncertainty. The dials are the model, the sensors, the noise values, and whether you run forward or both directions.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

One notch deeper: each of the theme's 4 approach-families and the specific mathematical mechanism that makes its papers work — the exact knob turned on the core relations above. Under each, the specific mathematical note for every paper in that family.

1Gain weighting
K = P_pred H^T (H P_pred H^T + R)^(-1)
The Kalman gain K automatically decides how much to trust the new measurement versus your prediction. It computes the ratio of your prediction's uncertainty (P_pred) against the measurement noise (R): when your measurement is very clean (small R), the gain increases and you believe it more; when your prediction is already confident, the gain shrinks. This one equation balances the two sources of information without manual tuning.
2Uncertainty growth and shrinkage
P_pred = F P F^T + Q; P_corr = (I − K H) P_pred
Your uncertainty spreads as time passes: F P F^T lets the state dynamics F expand your uncertainty, and Q adds process noise you can't model. When a measurement arrives, it shrinks: the term (I − K H) reduces P_corr. You're tracking not just your best guess but the confidence band around it—this dance of growing then shrinking uncertainty is the heartbeat of filtering. Without it, you'd treat a month-old guess the same as a second-old one.
3Linearization for curved worlds
F_jacobian = ∇f(x̂), H_jacobian = ∇h(x̂)
Real systems are usually curved, not straight lines. The Extended Kalman Filter replaces the fixed system model F and measurement model H with their local slopes, computed at your current estimate x̂. This is like steering a car: you don't predict the whole road as a line, you follow the local tangent. For mildly curved systems this works well; for sharp turns (like a robot spinning fast), you may need sigma points or particles instead.
4Smoothing with hindsight
x_smooth = x_fwd + C(x_bwd − x_pred)
Online filtering only looks backward in time, so new measurements lag behind your state estimate. Smoothing runs a second pass backward through your recorded data to push future measurements back into past estimates. The term C (the smoother gain) blends your forward pass x_fwd with the backward information x_bwd. This is why video de-noising works better offline than live: you get to use tomorrow's frame to clean up today's.
The through-line. The core problem is that reality is hidden behind noisy measurements, and no single source of information — not the model, not the sensor — is trustworthy on its own. You need a way to combine them that adapts to which one is more reliable right now, and that shrinks your uncertainty as fresh data comes in.

The Kalman filter solves this with two plain ideas: carry not just a best guess but also a measure of your uncertainty, and use that uncertainty to automatically compute how much to lean on the prediction versus the measurement at every step. The rest is just the math that falls out when you ask the question: what single update gets you the best guess if you assume the noise is random and you want to minimize the expected error?

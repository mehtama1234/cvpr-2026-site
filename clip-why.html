CLIP — teaching a model to connect words and pixels

How does a model recognize a corgi it was never trained to label — just because you typed the word? CLIP's answer is disarmingly simple: put pictures and the words that describe them into the same space, and pull each picture toward its own caption. Do that at scale and something remarkable falls out for free — you can name anything by writing it down. This page is the plain story, built on a real run where zero-shot recognition emerges from alignment alone, with no labels — and where we are honest about what a tiny version can and cannot show.

Mathematical core

Hidden quantity. The page is really about estimating the shared meaning coordinate where text and image evidence meet.

Evidence. The model sees caption-image pairs, region features, negatives, prompts, and similarity scores.

Rule. The fundamental rule is to pull matching text and visual examples together while pushing mismatches apart.

Failure test. Language can overpower seeing, so the model may retrieve or ground a likely concept instead of the visible one.

1 · The problem — a model only knows what it was labeled to name

Train an ordinary classifier on a fixed list of categories and it is blind to everything off that list. Show it a thing it has a label for and it works; ask for anything else and it cannot even represent the question. That fixed vocabulary is a cage: every new category means new labeled images and retraining. What we actually want is to name a thing in words — any words — and have the model find it, with no new training.

The problem, stated once: recognize a category specified by its name in free text, including categories never seen during training — which means the model has to connect language to pixels, not just pixels to a fixed label.

2 · The reframe — pull each picture toward its caption

The idea: give images and text a shared space, and train so a picture and its caption land in the same place. An image encoder turns a picture into a vector; a text encoder turns a caption into a vector in the same space. Then the training does one thing: for a big batch of (image, caption) pairs, pull each image toward its own caption and push it away from everyone else's. No class labels — just which caption goes with which image. After enough pairs, the space is organized by meaning: a photo of a cat sits near the words a photo of a cat.
for each image: pull toward its caption · push away from the other captions in the batch     # contrastive alignment (InfoNCE) — the only supervision is which caption pairs with which image

Now recognition needs no labels at all. To classify a new image, write each candidate class as a little phrase (a photo of a ...), embed those phrases with the text encoder, and see which one the image sits closest to. The classifier is built out of words. That is zero-shot classification, and the run below shows it appear.

3 · Seen on a real run — zero-shot recognition emerges

We trained a small image encoder and text encoder together on (image, caption) pairs — captions are just a photo of a {class} — and never on class labels as targets. Then we classified test images purely by nearest class name. Here is the shared space it learned, and how zero-shot accuracy grows as we feed it more pairs:

4 · The honest ceiling — and where the real power hides

Be honest about the limits. A plain supervised linear probe, trained with labels on the same features, still scores higher (84% vs zero-shot 80%). On a closed set of ten classes, labels win — of course they do.

Why CLIP still wins in practice. The point of alignment is not to beat a labeled classifier on ten fixed classes — it is that its classifier is made of words, so it costs nothing to add an eleventh class, or a thousand, or a phrase no one anticipated (a plush corgi wearing sunglasses). That open-vocabulary reach only shows up with large, diverse captions — which is exactly why the real CLIP was trained on hundreds of millions of internet image-text pairs. A ten-class toy can prove the mechanism; only scale reveals the superpower.

5 · Why it matters — the engine under open-vocabulary vision

Once words and pixels share a space, you can name anything — and the whole field runs on it. Open-vocabulary detection and segmentation put a text query on every box and pixel. Vision-language models bolt a language model onto CLIP's image space. The dominant 2026 research move is lifting this whole-image alignment down to the region, pixel, and 3D-point level while keeping the name-anything reach.

6 · The surprise — a robot you can talk to

In vision, aligning words and pixels lets you label anything by name. In robotics, the same alignment lets a robot be commanded by name: paint a map with vision-language features and it will drive to the fire extinguisher you asked for; ground the words the screwdriver to pixels and it will grasp a tool it never trained on.

Same alignment, a physical outcome. Vision closes the loop at a caption; a robot closes it with a gripper — so the failure moves off the screen and into the world (grasp the wrong object, drive at the unseen obstacle). The in-the-wild page lays out both sides.

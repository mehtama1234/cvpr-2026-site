Mixture-of-LoRA · the big picture, connecting the dots

Why mix LoRAs at all

Before any mechanism, the plain question: what are all of these methods actually for? This page is the story with no jargon — the one problem the whole family is solving, why the two obvious fixes both break, the single reframe that resolves it, and the handful of real choices that follow. The three companion pages then open the machinery. A few claims here are checked against LoRAs we trained on a laptop CPU.

1 · The problem, in one sentence

We have one very capable model. We want it to be genuinely good at many different jobs — not one skill, but a whole shelf of them: answer math, write code, speak French, format JSON, follow a house style. And we want that without paying for a separate model per job, without the jobs smearing into each other, and — ideally — able to combine two skills when a request needs both.

The whole problem, stated once: how do you get one model that holds many specialist skills at the same time — cheap to store, cheap to run, with the skills kept cleanly apart, yet able to be mixed on demand?

Everything below — every method, every paper in this theme — is one answer to exactly that sentence. Keep it in mind and the field stops looking like a pile of acronyms and starts looking like a series of moves on a single board.

2 · The two obvious answers, and why both break

There are two things anyone tries first. Neither works, and understanding why is what forces the real idea.

Easy answer A

One model per skill

Make a fresh, fully fine-tuned copy of the model for each job. Each one is excellent at its own task.

Why it breaks: a copy is the entire model — hundreds of millions of numbers, every time. Fifty skills means fifty full models: you can't hold them in memory, can't serve them cheaply, and a request that needs two skills at once has nowhere to go — it lands on exactly one model.

Easy answer B

One model for everything

Fine-tune a single model on all the jobs mixed together. Now everything fits in one place.

Why it breaks: the skills interfere. Teaching it law blurs its medicine; the single set of weights becomes the average of every specialist — a generalist that is mediocre at each. And you can never cleanly add a new skill or remove an old one without retraining the whole thing.

So the tension is sharp: separate models keep skills clean but cost too much and can't combine; one merged model is cheap but muddy and rigid. The real answer has to be cheap and clean and combinable at once. That sounds impossible until you notice one thing has already been solved.

3 · The reframe — storage was already solved; the real problem is choosing

A skill does not need a whole new model. A LoRA is the proof: a new task can be carried by a tiny low-rank patch — a few megabytes — laid over a frozen base that never changes. So half the problem evaporates. Keep one big frozen model in memory, and a drawer full of tiny skill-patches. Storage: done. Storage-cleanliness too — each patch is a separate object on the shelf, so nothing bleeds while they sit there. But note the word: it is only the storage that is clean. The moment you combine two patches — the whole point of what follows — the interference from answer B comes straight back, because LoRA updates are not orthogonal and a blend of them is still a blend. Keeping skills clean through combination is the real open problem, and everything below is about winning it.

But solving that surfaces the problem this whole theme is named after. If you have a drawer full of patches, which one do you apply to a given input? And what if the input needs a blend?

Picking one is brittle — you'd have to know the task in advance, and a wrong guess applies the wrong skill. Merging them all with one fixed recipe is answer B again — a static average of the patches is back to the muddy generalist. The fix is to make the mixing weights depend on the input.
The move: make the blend depend on the input — route. Add one tiny decision-maker — a gate — that reads each input and decides, on the fly, how much of each patch to mix in. A math question leans on the math patch; a French coding request blends the code and French patches. The frozen base is shared by everyone; only the small gate and small patches carry the specialization. That is mixture-of-LoRA.
y = W·x  +  Σi  gi(x)  ·  patchi(x)     # frozen base + an input-conditioned blend of the patches

Be honest about that formula, though: with every gate weight non-zero it is still an average — just one whose weights move with the input. That is dense/soft routing, and (as the real numbers below show) a soft gate barely separates the experts. The genuine break from averaging is to make the gate sparse — keep only the top one or two weights and zero the rest — so most experts contribute nothing to a given token. Three regimes, worst to best: static average (answer B) → dense input-conditioned blend (better, still an average) → sparse top-k (a real commitment to a few experts). Keep that ladder in mind; the page keeps climbing it.

If this feels familiar, it should: it is the same shape as mixture-of-experts inside the giant models — many specialists plus a router — but done at the cheap scale of LoRA, so you can bolt it onto a model you already have instead of training a colossus from scratch. That is the dot that connects this corner to the rest of modern AI.

4 · The approaches — four questions, and every method is a set of answers

Once you accept "many small experts + a router," you have not finished — you have earned a handful of real decisions. Every method in the field, and every axis on the design-space map, is one answer to one of these four plain questions. None of them touch the frozen base; you are always just adding tiny patches and a tiny router.

  1. Where do the experts come from?

    For routed mixtures, three philosophies, and getting the category right is most of the game. Compose — take patches trained separately (a math one, a French one) and learn only a gate to mix them; modular, add or drop experts like plug-ins (X-LoRA). Grow — train the experts and gate together from scratch so they co-specialize, carving the work up between them instead of each trying to do all of it (MixLoRA). Split — make each of one LoRA's internal directions (its ranks) an expert and co-train a rank-level router, so the ranks become specialized (rank-as-expert). A caution the split is easy to get wrong on: a plain trained LoRA's ranks are not pre-labelled experts you can just route over — the decomposition isn't unique, so the specialization has to be trained in, not read off. (That the ranks specialize at all is earned, exactly as the numbers below show.)

    Two families sit outside the router, and honesty demands naming them: static merge — fold several trained adapters into one frozen blend with a good conflict-resolution rule (TIES, DARE, LoRAHub) and pay zero runtime, trading away the ability to adapt per input; and router-free mixtures that combine experts by a fixed geometric rule instead of a learned gate. So "add a tiny router" below is the choice this page follows, not a law — merging is the real alternative that buys inference cost of nothing.

  2. What does the router look at, and how sharply does it commit?

    Per whole sentence, or per individual word? Sequence-level routing is cheaper and steadier but locks in one expert mix for the whole answer — it cannot switch skills mid-generation. Token-level routing can, which is the only way to handle a compound request like "shout this, then return it as JSON," where the right expert changes partway through — at the cost of deciding on every token. On the sharpness dial: a soft blend of all experts is smooth to train; a sparse pick of the top few is cheaper to run and forces cleaner specialization; and the gate's "temperature" sets how decisively it commits versus hedges — a real knob, as the variants show (dropping τ from 1.0 to 0.3 still left the top weight near 0.36 — proof that a gate whose logits are already close cannot be sharpened into separation after the fact).

  3. Where in the model does the mixing happen?

    Every layer can host its own little committee, or only some. More mixing points means more places to specialize — and more gate parameters to train. It is the same coverage-versus-cost dial as choosing where to attach a plain LoRA, now applied to where the routing lives.

  4. What stops the experts collapsing into the same thing?

    Under sparse routing this is a real danger: an expert the router stops selecting gets exactly zero gradient that step, so it never improves, so it keeps not being selected — a rich-get-richer death spiral that ends with one expert doing everything. (Under a dense soft gate every expert always gets some gradient, so this particular death is a sparse-routing problem.) Two defences. First, a load-balance loss: the standard term penalizes the product, summed over experts, of how many tokens each expert got and how much gate probability it got — pushing both toward an even 1/N split, the way a good manager stops one person doing everything. Second, just keeping top-k with k≥2: if two experts always fire, at least two can never starve — a structural floor against collapse, which is why our top-2 builds never fully collapse and our top-1 ablation below is where the tilt actually shows.

5 · What we actually saw — the same story in real weights

These aren't just tidy claims. We built all three philosophies on a frozen Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct and watched them route three toy skills — math, shout (uppercase), json. The pattern from question 1 shows up directly in the numbers.

Compose (X-LoRA): the soft gate barely separates — and once, picks the wrong expert

Three experts trained separately, then a soft (dense) gate learned over them. Averaged across each sequence, the margins are thin — and on the JSON prompt the gate doesn't just weaken, it inverts: its top weight goes to the math expert, not the json one:

Why so faint? The gate is a small linear-then-softmax head trained only to lower the language loss — never explicitly to separate the experts — so nothing pushes its logits apart, and a softmax over near-equal logits comes out near-uniform. Worse for JSON specifically: the frozen base already emits clean JSON, so there was never a gradient teaching the gate to prefer the JSON adapter. This isn't a write-up quirk; it is the honest behaviour of a dense gate over pre-trained experts — a soft blend is, after all, still an average — and it is exactly the pressure that pushes the field toward sparse routing and jointly-trained experts, the next two philosophies.

Grow (MixLoRA): experts co-specialize when trained together

Four experts and the gate trained jointly, top-2 used per token. Now the per-task usage is visibly carved up — different tasks lean on different experts, a sharpness the dense pre-trained gate above never reached (a fair comparison caveat: that one was soft and had only three experts — the point is soft-and-separate vs sparse-and-joint, not that composition is doomed):

The dots connect in the tables. Read top to bottom, the routing gets sharper exactly as the experts move from separately-trained (compose) to jointly-trained (grow) to sharing one adapter (split). Specialization is something the training earns, not something the architecture hands you for free.

Balancing (question 4): the pressure that keeps the committee alive

Sharper routing has a failure mode hiding in it. If nothing pushes back, the router keeps calling whichever expert is momentarily ahead — that expert gets more gradient, gets better, gets called even more — and the rest starve. To stress it, we trained the same 8-expert MoE twice from the same seed with a hard top-1 router — only the single winning expert runs each token, so there is no runner-up floor to hide behind — once with the load-balance loss, once with it switched off. The pull is real, and it shows up in concentration rather than body count: the same 2 experts died in both runs — so at this scale the deaths are driven by initialization, not by the missing balance loss. What the balance loss visibly does is flatten the winners — strip it and the busiest expert's share jumps from 32% to 41% of all routing while overall evenness slips 0.88→0.82. The tilt toward a one-expert committee is unmistakable in direction; full collapse (many dead) is what the same pressure prevents once training is longer and the task is hard enough to keep gradients flowing.

Read this honestly: it is a single paired run (same seed, one condition flipped), so the 9-point gap in top-expert share is suggestive, not statistically significant — with n=1 you cannot fully separate "the loss flattened the winner" from init noise. The direction is what's defensible; the magnitude of the effect is real but small at this toy scale, which is exactly why load-balancing is framed as insurance that bites hardest at scale, not a dramatic rescue here.

Feel it yourself. The routing sandbox lets you flip these exact knobs live — experts, top-k, temperature, load-balance on/off — and watch the usage bars collapse or hold in real time. Try top-1 with balancing off, then press Train.

6 · Why it matters — what the reframe buys

This isn't cleverness for its own sake. Routing tiny patches over a frozen base changes the economics of specialization on four axes at once:

what you getwhy
Cheap to holdone base in memory serves hundreds of skills, because each skill is megabytes, not gigabytes. The thing that was answer-A's dealbreaker is gone.
Skills stay separableeach expert is its own object — add a new skill without retraining, remove one without damaging the rest. Answer B could never do this.
Skills combine on demandthe model is no longer locked to one task per request; the gate can blend two experts when the input needs both.
The specialization is legibleyou can watch which expert fires on which input — the tables above — which is impossible to read out of a single monolithic fine-tune.

That last point is why the hands-on page exists: you can literally see the math expert light up on math. And the map page exists because, once you internalise the four questions, every paper in the field is just a particular set of answers — and they cluster into a small number of sensible combinations.

One honest asterisk on "cheap." Cheap to hold is unconditional — the patches are tiny. Cheap to run is not free: keeping the adapters separable means you don't get the merge free-lunch, so you pay something at inference. A dense soft gate runs every expert; X-LoRA (our first demo) even runs the base model twice per token to compute its gate. Only sparse top-k routing keeps run-cost near the base model's — which is the deeper reason the field pushes toward sparsity, beyond just cleaner specialization.
The one idea to keep. There's a trilemma underneath all four questions — specialization (experts truly divide the labour), non-interference (skills don't bleed when combined), and efficiency (little extra compute or memory). You can comfortably buy any two; the third is what each method spends to get the other two. Static merge buys efficiency + no-runtime but gives up per-input specialization; a dense soft gate buys specialization + simplicity but pays full compute and still interferes; sparse routing chases all three and spends a load-balance loss to keep the experts from collapsing. Every row on the map is one way of picking which corner to sacrifice.

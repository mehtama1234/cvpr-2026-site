SVD, PCA & low-rank structure — why it works, mathematically

A table of numbers can be understood as a few key stretching directions, and cutting off the tiny stretches removes junk and noise while keeping the shape intact. This is how you compress a million-pixel image to a thousand numbers, or clean up a signal buried in static.

The problem, as a quantity

Imagine you have a table of numbers — maybe pixels in an image, or measurements across many sensors, or price movements of a thousand stocks. The table is often huge: millions of entries. Directly working with all of them is slow and wasteful. The vast majority of what happens in the table follows just a few patterns — the main directions along which the numbers actually change. Strip down to those patterns, and you keep the essential information while discarding the noise and the redundant stuff. But how do you find those patterns automatically, without knowing in advance what you are looking for?

The core ideas — and why they work

Any table of numbers is really a few stretch-directions

A = U·Σ·Vᵀ — rotate, stretch by amounts σ_k, rotate
Any table of numbers can be written as a rotation, followed by stretching by a set of amounts, followed by another rotation. The key insight is that the stretches come in a ranking: the first stretch is the biggest, the second smaller, and so on down to nearly zero. The big stretches are the directions where the numbers really vary; the tiny stretches are often just noise or redundant copies of information. This way of writing a table is called the singular-value decomposition, and it automatically finds the main stretching directions without you having to say what they are.

Here, the two rotation operations (one called U and the other the transpose of V, written V-transpose) are simply different coordinate systems — ways of looking at the data from different angles. The stretches in the middle (the sigma symbols) are the amounts by which the data spreads along each direction. The formula says any table A equals these three pieces multiplied together: a rotation, then a scaling that makes the data taller or narrower along each axis, then another rotation.

Keep the biggest stretches, drop the rest — the best simple fit

A ≈ Σ_{k≤r} σ_k·u_k·v_kᵀ (best rank-r approximation)
If you keep only the biggest stretches and throw away the rest, you get the best rank-r approximation — that is, the closest table you can make using only r patterns instead of the full number. 'Closest' means the difference squared between the original and the simplified version is as small as possible. Crucially, you do not have to know in advance how many patterns to keep; you can check how much error you get for r = 10, r = 100, r = 1000, and pick the point where adding more does not help much.

Why is this the best? Because the stretches are ordered by size — they rank the directions by how much spread (variance) the data has along each one. Keeping the biggest is like keeping the directions that matter most to the data. You cannot improve by swapping in a smaller direction for a bigger one: the maths proves that if you sum up the top r stretches, you get the smallest possible error. No other choice of r patterns could do better.

The leading directions are where the data spreads out most

top directions = axes of largest spread in the data (PCA)
When you apply this decomposition to data where each column is a different sensor reading or measurement, the leading directions tell you exactly where the data spreads out. If you have images of faces, the first direction might capture 'how bright', the second 'how wide', and so on — the main ways faces vary. These directions are the axes of largest spread, and they are called the principal components. Taking the data and projecting it onto these top directions is called principal component analysis, or PCA. It is the same machine as the general decomposition above, just pointed at data that is centered at zero and asking 'which directions capture the most spread?'

The small stretches are often just noise, so cutting them cleans up

drop small σ_k ⇒ compress and denoise at once
The tiny stretches in the decomposition are often nothing but noise — random jitter mixed into your measurements, or artefacts of how the data was collected. By cutting off those tiny stretches, you throw away the noise without destroying the signal. The remaining big stretches preserve all the patterns and structure in the original data. This is why the decomposition is so powerful for cleaning: you get compression (fewer patterns means fewer numbers to store or send), denoising (the noise lived in the small stretches you discarded), and dimension reduction (working in the space of top stretches is much cheaper) all in one move. You are not guessing at what counts as noise; the stretches tell you objectively which directions carry almost no energy in the data.

The approaches, as variations on the math

The same machine — find the stretching directions and rank them by size — works wherever you point it. Image compression: keep the top 50 stretches out of millions, reducing storage from a billion bytes to a million. Sensor cleanup: 100 noisy temperature sensors in a factory reduce to 5 principal directions; the noise lived in the dropped tiny stretches, leaving a clean signal. Finding structure in text: word counts in 10,000 documents live in a sparse table; the top 200 stretches capture the main topics, and the dropped tiny ones are statistical flukes. Detecting faults: abnormal patterns in a machine produce new stretches where the normal data has none, standing out clearly once you know which directions matter. The thread never changes: rank the stretching directions by energy, keep what carries signal, discard what carries noise.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

One notch deeper: each of the theme's 4 approach-families and the specific mathematical mechanism that makes its papers work — the exact knob turned on the core relations above. Under each, the specific mathematical note for every paper in that family.

1Truncated SVD for compression
Keep top k singular values, drop the rest: A_k = Σ(i=1 to k) σ_i u_i v_i^T
When you decompose a table into its singular values and vectors, most of the pattern sits in just a few terms. By keeping only the largest k singular values (the σ terms) and throwing away the small ones, you get the best rank-k approximation—no other table of rank k is closer to the original. This is why it works for image compression, denoising, and storage: you capture what matters and drop what is likely noise or redundancy.
2Variance-maximizing principal directions
Find direction v that maximizes v^T C v where C = (X - μ)^T(X - μ)/(n-1)
When you have data points scattered in space, you want to find the direction where they spread out the most. That direction is the one where projecting all the points gives the biggest variance. The spreading itself is measured by the spread table C, which tracks how much the centered data (X - μ) wiggles in each direction. The principal directions are exactly the eigenvectors of C, and SVD finds them directly from the centered data without building C explicitly—which keeps the computation stable.
3Pseudo-inverse for noisy systems
x^+ = A^+ b = U D^+ V^T b, where D^+ inverts only nonzero singular values
Real systems of equations often have no exact solution: too many measurements, conflicting constraints, or noise. Instead of failing, the pseudo-inverse A^+ gives you the least-squares solution (smallest total blame), and it picks the unique one with smallest norm among all least-squares answers. It does this by only inverting the singular values that matter (the nonzero ones) and setting aside the directions where the system collapses information. This is like giving an honest answer to a broken question.
4Best-fit affine subspaces
Fit a hyperplane through centroid μ using first d-k principal directions from SVD of X - μ
When you want to find a lower-dimensional flat model that sits closest to your data cloud—like fitting a plane through a scattered 3D point set—the answer is to center the data at its centroid μ, then take the span of its top principal directions. The principal directions come directly from SVD and capture where the data extends furthest. This model stays in the least-squares sense and automatically passes through the data's center of mass, which balances the fit.
The through-line. The core problem is taking a huge table of entangled data and finding the few patterns that really matter, throwing away the noise and redundancy without destroying what counts. Two plain ideas carry it: any table can be written as rotations and stretches combined, and if you rank those stretches by size, the big ones carry the signal and the tiny ones carry mostly noise. Everything else — compressing, denoising, finding the hidden structure, detecting when something is wrong — follows from cutting the table down to its top stretches and working there instead.

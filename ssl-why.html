Why self-supervised learning — teaching a model with no teacher

Self-supervised learning is how nearly every modern vision model is now born: it learns useful features from piles of unlabeled data before it ever sees a labelled example. This page is the plain-language story — the problem it solves, the single trick that solves it, a real run where you can watch structure appear from zero labels, the one enemy that haunts it, and the surprise that robots use the same idea to learn from their own experience.

1 · The problem — labels are the bottleneck

A deep network learns from examples, and for most of the field's history each example had to come with a human-written label: this image is a cat, that pixel is 3 metres away, this frame contains a fall. Labelling is slow, expensive, and for many things nearly impossible — nobody can hand-annotate the depth of every pixel or the content of every video frame on the internet. Meanwhile unlabelled data is effectively infinite: every photo, every video, every scan.

The problem, stated once: how do you learn genuinely useful features from data that nobody labelled — turning the endless supply of raw images into a head start on every task?

2 · The reframe — invent a task the data can grade itself on

The idea: make up a puzzle where the answer is already in the data. Two puzzles have won. Predict the hidden part — blank out patches of an image and make the model fill them back in; to do that well it must understand what it is looking at. Match two views — take two random crops or distortions of the same image and require their embeddings to agree; to do that the model must learn what is essential (the object) and ignore what is incidental (the crop). No human labels anywhere — the supervision comes from the data's own structure. The features the model builds while solving the puzzle turn out to transfer to the real tasks you actually care about.

Both puzzles are self-graded, so you can run them on unlimited unlabelled data. (A third, JEPA, predicts the hidden part's embedding rather than its pixels — cheaper and more abstract.) The model gets a free, endless teacher: the data itself.

3 · Seen on a real run — structure from zero labels

Every number below is measured. We trained a small encoder on FashionMNIST by contrastive learning — it only ever saw pairs of augmented views and was asked to pull each pair together and push other images apart. No label ever touched the encoder. Then we froze it and read its features two ways.

Structure emerges from zero labels

Left: a 2D view of the learned embeddings for held-out test images, each dot coloured by its true class — which the model never saw. The classes fall into separated clusters anyway. A simple linear classifier read off those frozen features gets 75% accuracy across 10 classes (chance is 10%). The encoder discovered the categories on its own, purely from the structure of the images.

4 · The one enemy — collapse

Every “match two views” method hides the same trap. If the only rule is “make the two views agree,” there is a trivial way to win: map every image to the exact same point. Then all views agree, the loss is zero, and the model has learned nothing. This is collapse, and it is the single deepest idea in SSL. We can watch it happen:

with negatives (push others apart)
75%
probe accuracy · embedding spread (std) = 0.053
without negatives (only pull together)
10%
probe accuracy (≈ chance) · embedding spread (std) = 0.000 — a single point

Strip the “push others apart” term and the encoder takes the cheat: it maps everything to the same point, so every pair trivially matches. The embedding spread falls to 0.000 (measured — essentially zero) and the probe drops to 10%, pure chance. That is collapse, and preventing it — with negatives, with a momentum teacher (DINO), or by predicting in latent space (JEPA) — is the entire art of self-supervised learning.

5 · Why it matters — SSL became the substrate

One honest limit of this toy. A linear probe on the raw pixels of FashionMNIST already reaches 85% — higher than our SSL features' 75%. That is expected, not a failure: FashionMNIST is nearly linearly separable, so there is little for representation learning to add. SSL's real payoff is on hard, high-dimensional data — natural images, video, 3D — where raw pixels are useless to a linear classifier. That is exactly why the field pretrains models like DINOv2/v3 on internet-scale images, not on toy sets. What this run shows cleanly are the two universal truths: structure emerges without labels, and the whole game is preventing collapse.

The consequence shows up in the numbers of the whole field: at CVPR 2026, self-supervised pretraining is no longer a research curiosity, it is infrastructure. Roughly two to three times more papers consume a frozen self-supervised backbone (DINOv2, and the brand-new DINOv3) than invent a new one — dropping those internet-pretrained features into 3D, medical, forensics, detection, and tracking, often with no training at all. The label-free head start became the ground everyone builds on.

6 · The surprise — robots learn from their own experience

Self-supervision is even more natural for a robot than for a photo library, because a robot is drowning in unlabelled sensor data it could never hope to annotate — and the world itself supplies the answer key. Point two cameras at a scene and geometry says what the depth must be (no labels needed). Let time pass and the next frame is the answer to “what happens next.” Take an action and the resulting motion labels your own dynamics. Robotics turns all of these into free supervision.

Same idea, a richer teacher. Where a vision model squeezes supervision out of a single image with crops and masks, a robot squeezes it out of the physics of its own body — multi-view geometry, the flow of time, its own actions and forces. And for the semantics on top, robots simply borrow vision's frozen SSL backbones. The in-the-wild page lays out both sides.

Where to go from here. Play with collapse yourself in the SSL sandbox — pull the views together, and watch the whole cloud implode to a point until you switch the negatives back on. Then read how the field actually uses SSL across vision and robotics, and the paper deep-reads — from frozen DINO backbones to robots learning from their own motion.

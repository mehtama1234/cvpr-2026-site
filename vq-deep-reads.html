1 · Fighting collapse: better quantizers

A VQ tokenizer turns an image into words by snapping each patch to the nearest entry in a learned codebook. That snap is what makes the tokens discrete and countable, but it is also the thing that breaks. When training starts, a few codebook words happen to sit near most patches, so they get chosen again and again while the rest are never picked and quietly go dead. This is codebook collapse: the vocabulary looks large on paper but only a handful of words do any work, which caps how much detail the tokenizer can preserve. The papers in this cluster all attack the quantizer itself rather than the model around it. Some redesign the shape of the code space so the words are spread out as evenly as possible, some make the hard snap trainable so gradients can flow through it, and some give up on pure discreteness and let the tokens stay partly continuous. The shared payoff is a vocabulary that is actually used end to end, which means sharper reconstructions from fewer tokens.

CVPR'26Spherical Leech Quantization for Visual Tokenization and Generation
Problem

Standard vector quantization scatters its codebook words unevenly through the embedding space, so patches cluster onto a few nearby words and the rest sit idle. The result is wasted vocabulary and reconstructions that lose fine detail, because there is simply no well-placed word to land on.

Approach

Instead of letting the codebook words fall wherever training pushes them, this paper arranges them using a famous mathematical pattern called the Leech lattice, which is the provably best way to pack points into a sphere in up to twenty-four dimensions. Each patch embedding is mapped by a learned transform onto this evenly-packed space and then snapped to the nearest lattice point, so no region is starved and no region is crowded. For higher dimensions than the lattice covers, the method stacks quantization in layers to keep the packing efficient. Because the words are spread out by construction, far more of them get used and each patch finds a genuinely close match.

Why it matters

The even packing lets the tokenizer reconstruct images more faithfully while using about twenty percent fewer tokens than standard VQ, directly turning better codebook utilization into higher generation quality.

CVPR'26Differentiable Vector Quantization for Rate-Distortion Optimization of Generative Image Compression
Problem

In VQ-based image compression the goal is to trade off file size against picture quality, but the hard snap to the nearest word blocks gradients: the part of the loss that cares about file size can never reach the encoder. This decouples how the tokenizer learns its representation from how the bits are actually counted, so the two are tuned in isolation and never jointly optimized.

Approach

The method, called RDVQ, replaces the hard nearest-neighbour snap with a soft, distance-aware distribution over the whole codebook, so instead of picking one winning word a patch spreads a weighted vote across nearby words. Because this soft assignment is differentiable, the file-size objective can now send gradients all the way back to the encoder, letting a single end-to-end training run shape both the latent distribution and the model that estimates its bit cost. A masked-transformer entropy model then predicts the codes autoregressively for accurate bit estimation and lets the target bitrate be dialed at test time. The whole system is trained jointly rather than as disconnected stages.

Why it matters

Joint training cuts the required bitrate by up to seventy-six percent on one perceptual metric while using far fewer parameters, showing that making the snap trainable buys both efficiency and quality.

CVPR'26MacTok: Robust Continuous Tokenization for Image Generation
Problem

Continuous tokenizers built on KL-regularized autoencoders hit their own version of collapse: when you push for very few tokens under strong regularization, the latent space degenerates and stops carrying meaning. This posterior collapse makes it hard to get both aggressive compression and high generation quality at the same time.

Approach

MacTok keeps the tokens continuous rather than discrete, sidestepping the dead-word problem, and instead fights the latent from going empty by forcing it to work harder. During training it randomly masks image patches and also masks based on DINO semantic cues, so the encoder and decoder must reconstruct pictures from deliberately incomplete inputs and cannot shortcut into a degenerate latent. It further aligns both global and local representations to keep discriminative, semantic information intact inside the compressed space. All of this lets the tokenizer run on just sixty-four or one hundred twenty-eight tokens while staying semantically rich.

Why it matters

The result is competitive generation quality at up to sixty-four times fewer tokens, showing that a continuous code space, kept alive by masking, can hit extreme compression without collapsing.

CVPR'26VQRAE: Representation Quantization Autoencoders for Multimodal Understanding, Generation and Reconstruction
Problem

Building one tokenizer that serves both understanding tasks, which want rich continuous features, and generation tasks, which want clean discrete tokens, usually forces awkward dual-encoder designs. Cramming semantic and low-level detail into a single quantized codebook without letting most of the words die is the hard part.

Approach

VQRAE builds a vector-quantized version of a representation autoencoder on top of a pretrained vision foundation model, producing continuous semantic features and discrete tokens at the same time from one architecture. It trains in two stages: first it freezes the encoder and learns a very high-dimensional semantic codebook driven by pixel reconstruction, then it unfreezes the encoder and jointly refines it with self-distillation constraints that keep the semantics aligned. Working at a large codebook dimension of 1536, the design keeps every word in play rather than letting most go dead. This removes the need for a separate second encoder while still supporting understanding, generation, and reconstruction together.

Why it matters

It reaches a full one hundred percent codebook utilization at that high dimension, meaning no wasted words, while staying competitive across understanding, generation, and reconstruction benchmarks.

2 · Tokenizers for autoregressive generators

Once a tokenizer chops an image into a string of discrete symbols, that image becomes a kind of sentence a GPT-style model can write one word at a time, predicting each next visual token from the ones before it. The field has largely agreed that this tokenizer is the make-or-break part: the generator only ever rearranges the symbols the tokenizer hands it, so whatever detail the tokenizer throws away is gone for good. The dominant recipe is not left-to-right token prediction but next-scale prediction (the VAR family): first paint a tiny coarse thumbnail of the whole image, then progressively add finer and finer layers of detail, so the model always sees the full picture in rough form before sharpening it. Because that coarse-to-fine schedule is the generation process, researchers now redesign the tokenizer and its scale ladder directly around the autoregressive generator, rather than treating tokenization as a fixed preprocessing step. Some rebuild the schedule itself — trimming the history each scale depends on, or replacing crude downsampling with a physics-honest blur-to-sharp path; others push the tokens to carry far more meaning, or patch a frozen tokenizer so it stops mangling fine structure like text. The through-line is a hard ceiling: the generator can never produce a sharper, more faithful image than its tokenizer is able to reconstruct, so improving the tokenizer and its scales is the surest way to lift the whole pipeline.
CVPR'26Markovian Scale Prediction: A New Era of Visual Autoregressive Generation
Problem
The leading visual autoregressive recipe (VAR) builds an image coarse-to-fine, predicting each new scale from every scale that came before it. That full-context dependency is stable but heavy, because every step must re-read the entire growing history of all earlier scales.
Approach
The authors ask whether a generator really needs to look at all prior scales, or just the most recent one. They introduce Markov-VAR, which makes next-scale prediction Markovian — each new scale depends mainly on the immediately preceding scale rather than the full history of scales. To keep the model from forgetting useful long-range structure when the history is trimmed, they add a history compensation mechanism that folds back the essential context a strict one-step dependency would lose. The result keeps the coarse-to-fine thumbnail-then-refine story intact while lightening what each refinement step has to condition on. They release a full series of Markov-VAR models trained on ImageNet.
Why it matters
By re-engineering the scale schedule that sits between tokenizer and generator, it shows the coarse-to-fine ladder itself is a design knob for AR quality and efficiency — not a fixed given.
CVPR'26FVAR: Next-Focus Prediction for Visual Autoregressive Modeling
Problem
To build the coarse-to-fine pyramid that VAR generators walk up, most methods just uniformly shrink the image at each scale. That crude downsampling introduces aliasing — jagged edges and moire patterns — which corrupts fine detail and makes training less stable, hurting things like readable text.
Approach
FVAR reframes the whole ladder from next-scale prediction to next-focus prediction, borrowing from how a camera pulls an image from blur into clarity. Instead of shrinking the image, it builds a progressive refocusing pyramid using real optical defocus (point-spread) kernels whose blur radius steadily decreases, giving a physics-honest blur-to-sharp path with no aliasing. During training a High-Frequency Residual Teacher network teaches the model both the clean structure and the alias residuals it should avoid, and this teacher adds zero cost at generation time. The scheme drops cleanly into existing VAR frameworks as a replacement for their downsampling step.
Why it matters
Because the generator can only reconstruct what its multi-scale representation preserved, fixing aliasing at the tokenizer/pyramid stage directly raises the fidelity ceiling — sharper details and more legible text — that the AR model can ever reach.
CVPR'26Cubic Discrete Diffusion: Discrete Visual Generation on High-Dimensional Representation Tokens
Problem
Discrete visual generators have been stuck using very thin latent tokens (roughly 8 to 32 numbers each), which throws away semantic richness. The much fatter tokens (768 to 1024 numbers) that pretrained vision encoders produce carry far more meaning, but nobody knew how to generate images in that high-dimensional discrete space.
Approach
The paper introduces Cubic Discrete Diffusion (CubiD), which discretizes those high-dimensional feature tokens and then generates them with a fine-grained masking scheme. Rather than masking whole tokens, any single dimension at any spatial position can be hidden and then predicted from the partial observations that remain. This forces the model to learn correlations both within a token and across spatial positions, and it runs in a fixed number of generation steps regardless of how many dimensions each token has. They show it scales cleanly from 900M to 3.7B parameters on ImageNet-256, and that the very same discretized tokens still support image understanding, not just generation.
Why it matters
It lifts the tokenizer's semantic ceiling: by proving high-dimensional pretrained representations can be discretized without losing their richness, it gives AR-style generators a token vocabulary that carries much more meaning per symbol.
CVPR'26Residual Decoder Adapter: ID-Preserving Tokenizer Adaption for Autoregressive Text Rendering
Problem
Autoregressive image models render text badly — blurry strokes, warped letters — because the visual tokenizer they rely on can only capture low-frequency information and loses the crisp high-frequency detail letters need. Fixing this by training a better tokenizer is obvious but brutal, since it forces expensive retraining of both the tokenizer and the whole AR model on top of it.
Approach
The Residual Decoder Adapter (RDA) patches a frozen tokenizer from the outside instead of retraining anything. A shared-ID hint codebook supplies the missing high-frequency cues while deliberately keeping the exact same token indices as the frozen codebook, so the AR model sees identical tokens and nothing downstream has to change. A small residual decoder then predicts only the pixel-level difference between the ground-truth image and the tokenizer's blurry output, fusing base and hint features and trained with reconstruction, perceptual, edge-aware Sobel, and frequency-domain losses. Because it only adapts the decoder in ID space rather than the weight space of the generator, it drops in as a plug-and-play upgrade on models like Janus-Pro, TAR, and Lumina-mGPT. On Janus-Pro 1B it lifts OCR accuracy from 24.52% to 58.26%.
Why it matters
It reframes the tokenizer as an upgradeable interface rather than a permanent bottleneck, showing you can raise an AR generator's detail ceiling — for text especially — without retraining the generator at all.

3 · Fewer tokens: video and compact tokenizers

Every image or video a model generates is first chopped into tokens, and the number of those tokens is the real bill you pay for generation. Video makes that bill brutal, because you multiply the tokens for a single frame by every frame in the clip, and cost climbs with time. So the frontier here is about spending fewer tokens without the picture falling apart. One move is to fold time into causal tokens, letting each moment reuse what came before instead of re-encoding the whole scene from scratch. Another is to spend tokens adaptively, handing more of the budget to hard, busy content and almost none to still or simple stretches. A third is flat 1D layouts that pack a whole image into a short, ordered list of tokens rather than a rigid 2D grid, so a large picture fits in a tiny budget. Fewer tokens is faster and cheaper, but cutting too deep smears fine detail into blur, so the whole fight is compactness that still holds onto the details that matter.
CVPR'26AdapTok: Learning Adaptive and Temporally Causal Video Tokenization in a 1D Latent Space
Problem
Most video tokenizers spend the same fixed number of tokens on every frame, which is wasteful because video is full of repetition and dead time. When researchers tried to make the count variable instead, the tokenizer had no global plan for where to spend its budget, so some clips got starved and others got flooded. The result was uneven quality and no clean way to stream the video as it arrives.
Approach
AdapTok lays a video out as a flat 1D list of tokens instead of a rigid grid, which frees it to move budget around without being locked to a fixed spatial shape. During training it randomly drops tokens off the tail of each clip, so the tokenizer learns to still reconstruct well when it has fewer tokens to work with. A small scorer then predicts how good the reconstruction will be at each possible token count, and because it is temporally causal each moment only looks backward in time, which is exactly what streaming and left-to-right generation need. Finally an integer-programming step (they call it IPAL) reads those predicted scores and hands out tokens across the whole batch to squeeze the most quality out of a fixed total budget. The upshot is a tokenizer that plans globally where its tokens will do the most good.
Why it matters
By allocating tokens where they actually help rather than uniformly, AdapTok pushes the quality-versus-cost frontier and hits a strong FVD of 28 on UCF-101 reconstruction while staying under a token budget. That means cheaper video without the quality collapse that usually comes with cutting tokens.
CVPR'26EVATok: Adaptive Length Video Tokenization for Efficient Visual Autoregressive Generation
Problem
Standard video tokenizers hand every block of video the same token budget, so a calm, near-static segment gets as many tokens as a fast, chaotic one. That burns tokens on easy content while shortchanging the hard, dynamic parts, hurting both efficiency and quality. Earlier attempts to vary the length leaned on crude heuristic search or small-batch optimization and left performance on the table.
Approach
EVATok builds its answer in four stages. First it trains a proxy tokenizer that can predict how good a reconstruction would be for any choice of token count, so it can judge trade-offs without re-running everything. Then it uses that proxy to label a dataset of videos with their best token assignment, scoring each option by a reward that trades quality against length. Next it trains a small, fast router that learns to look at a new clip and immediately predict its optimal token budget, turning a slow search into a quick classification. Finally it trains the real tokenizer to honor those router-chosen budgets, sharpening it further by aligning to a pretrained video encoder (V-JEPA) so the compressed tokens stay semantically faithful.
Why it matters
Spending tokens by difficulty rather than uniformly cuts 24.4% of tokens versus LARP at equal reconstruction quality, and up to 56% on WebVid, while still setting state-of-the-art class-to-video results. It is a direct win on the cost-versus-quality curve: fewer tokens, comparable pictures.
CVPR'26VibeToken: Scaling 1D Image Tokenizers and Autoregressive Models for Dynamic Resolution Generations
Problem
Autoregressive image generators pay dearly at high resolution because token count grows with image size and the compute grows even faster, roughly with the square. Worse, conventional grid-based (2D) tokenizers are trained for one resolution and shape, so they do not gracefully handle arbitrary sizes or aspect ratios. That forces expensive bolt-on super-resolution stages just to reach large images.
Approach
VibeToken is a resolution-agnostic 1D tokenizer built on a Transformer that encodes any image into a short, flat list of just 32 to 256 tokens. It uses flexible patch embeddings and positional encodings designed to stay stable as the resolution changes, so the same tokenizer works across many sizes rather than one fixed grid. Because the layout is 1D and decoupled from a spatial grid, the decoder can turn that same short token list back into whatever target resolution you ask for, giving native super-resolution for free. The generator running on top keeps a constant compute cost regardless of output size, since it always works from the same compact token budget. In effect a large image is planned in a tiny, order-friendly sequence that autoregressive models actually like.
Why it matters
VibeToken renders 1024x1024 images from only 64 tokens at a better quality score (3.94 gFID) than a diffusion baseline (5.87), while holding a flat 179 GFLOPs, 63.4x cheaper than LlamaGen. That is the compactness dream: far fewer tokens, faster generation, and no loss in fidelity.
CVPR'26LacTokGen: Latent Consistency Tokenizer for 1024-pixel Image Generation by 256-token Input
Problem
To generate a high-resolution image efficiently, you first have to squeeze it into a small set of discrete tokens, but ordinary tokenizers still produce token maps that are far too large to generate a 1024-pixel image cheaply. The more tokens the generator must produce, the slower and more memory-hungry it becomes. The open question is how far you can compress before the picture starts to degrade.
Approach
LacTokGen pushes compression to an extreme: it packs a full 1024-pixel image into only 256 tokens, roughly four times tighter than standard tokenizers. The key is consistency training, where the tokenizer is taught so that its compressed codes stay semantically and visually coherent rather than collapsing into mush at such a low count. That consistency constraint is what lets so few tokens still decode back up to a clean 1024x1024 image. Because generation now runs on a 256-token input instead of a large token map, the whole diffusion-style pipeline gets faster and lighter.
Why it matters
By reconstructing 1024 pixels from just 256 tokens, LacTokGen slashes the generation budget and its memory and latency while aiming to keep visual quality intact. The trade-off it deliberately walks up to is compression so aggressive it risks losing fine detail, which is exactly the frontier this theme is fighting over.

4 · Tokens that mean something

A token that only helps rebuild pixels is semantically empty: it tells you how to redraw a patch of color and texture, but nothing about what that patch is. The fix is to make tokens carry meaning, either by aligning them to the features of a big pretrained vision model, to language, or to whole object regions instead of a blind grid. Once a token means something, the same discrete code can serve two jobs at once: recognizing what is in a scene and generating new scenes. That is the payoff of one shared token space rather than two disconnected ones. The grander ambition goes further still: a single vocabulary that spans images, video, 3D and text, so every modality speaks the same discrete language. When that happens, understanding and generation stop being separate pipelines bolted together and become one system that can perceive and create in the same breath.
CVPR'26A More Word-like Image Tokenization for MLLMs
Problem
When a multimodal language model looks at an image, it first chops the image into tokens on a fixed spatial grid, treating every little patch as an independent unit. But a language model is built to reason over words, which are meaningful chunks, not arbitrary squares. Feeding it grid-patches is like handing a reader a page cut into equal rectangles instead of into words.
Approach
Instead of a fixed grid, this work carves the image into word-like tokens that each correspond to a meaningful region, an object, a part, a coherent concept. It uses segmentation-style grouping to find those regions, then a learned tokenizer maps each region to a discrete code, much as a text tokenizer maps a word to an index. The token vocabulary is trained to line up with how the language model already tokenizes text, so images and text share the same kind of discrete unit. The result is fewer tokens per image, each one carrying real semantic content rather than raw texture. This gives the language model something it can actually reason about, closer to reading than to staring at a pixel grid.
Why it matters
When image tokens behave like words, the language model can understand and describe a scene using the same machinery it uses for text, tightening the alignment between seeing and saying.
CVPR'26Vision Foundation Models Can Be Good Tokenizers for Latent Diffusion Models
Problem
Image generators compress a picture into a compact latent code before they diffuse and rebuild it, and the quality of that code sets a ceiling on everything downstream. The usual trick is to distill a powerful pretrained vision model into the tokenizer, but distillation quietly damages the very semantic robustness that made the vision model valuable. So you end up with a code that rebuilds pixels but has lost much of its meaning.
Approach
This work skips distillation entirely and uses a frozen vision foundation model directly as the tokenizer, so the code inherits the model's full semantic richness untouched. It reads features from shallow, middle and deep layers and projects them into the latent, then leans on a specialized decoder to solve the hard part: turning those semantically rich but spatially coarse features back into sharp pixels. The decoder splits the latent into a global summary and multi-scale spatial pieces, and rebuilds the image progressively from tiny resolution up to full size, with supervision at every scale. Because the encoder is never fine-tuned away from its original knowledge, the tokens stay aligned to what the vision model actually understands. A new robustness metric confirms the alignment barely degrades under meaning-preserving changes, where distilled tokenizers collapse.
Why it matters
Because the token space is the vision model's own semantic space, generation runs faster and cleaner, and the same tokens that draw an image also encode what it means, closing the gap between generating and understanding.
CVPR'26AToken: A Unified Tokenizer for Vision
Problem
Today's visual tokenizers are narrow specialists: one is built for images, another for video, another for 3D, and each is tuned for either reconstruction or understanding but rarely both. Language models thrived on a single tokenizer that handles all text, yet vision has no equivalent. Visual data lives in incompatible formats, so there is no shared discrete language across the modalities.
Approach
AToken puts images, video and 3D into one shared 4D latent space, where each modality simply occupies a different slice of the same coordinate system, an image as a flat plane, a video extended along time, a 3D asset spread over surface positions. A single transformer with 4D position encodings reads feature-and-coordinate pairs, so the same network processes all three without separate pipelines. The same latents feed two heads: one quantizes and reconstructs for generation, the other pools and aligns to text for understanding. It is trained without adversarial losses, using a Gram-matrix objective that avoids the instability of GANs, and it learns through a staged curriculum from images to video to 3D, each stage building on the last. One tokenizer thus delivers both faithful reconstruction and semantic recognition across every visual modality.
Why it matters
A single token space spanning images, video and 3D means the same discrete codes can rebuild any visual input and be recognized against language, so one vocabulary serves both creating and understanding across modalities that used to need separate systems.
CVPR'26OneCAT: Decoder-Only Auto-Regressive Model for Unified Understanding and Generation
Problem
Most multimodal models are assemblies of parts: a separate vision encoder for understanding, a separate diffusion model for generation, stitched around a language model. All that plumbing blocks deep cross-modal fusion and slows inference. Understanding and generation end up in different pipelines that barely share representations.
Approach
OneCAT collapses everything into a single decoder-only autoregressive model with no external encoder and no separate diffusion module, tokenizing raw images with only lightweight patch embeddings. Inside, a modality-specific mixture-of-experts routes the traffic: continuous vision tokens go to an understanding expert, discrete vision tokens to a generation expert, and text tokens to a text expert, all inside one shared network so fusion happens early rather than at the seams. A scale-aware adapter lets it generate images coarse-to-fine, from low resolution up to high, in the same autoregressive sweep it uses to read and write text. Because it is one model predicting the next token across all modalities, understanding, generation and editing live in a single unified space. That also removes the heavy diffusion machinery, giving a large speedup, especially at high resolution.
Why it matters
By doing recognition and generation as one next-token process in one model, the same tokens and the same weights serve both jobs, which is exactly the understand-and-generate-in-one-space payoff, delivered faster and without a tangle of separate components.

5 · Snapping actions to words

A robot's motor commands are smooth, continuous numbers — joint angles, wheel speeds, gripper widths that slide through an unbroken range. But a transformer or language-model-style policy would much rather do what it does best: pick the next item from a fixed menu, the way it picks the next word. So take a short chunk of motion and snap it to the nearest entry in a learned codebook of action words, and now the policy predicts codes, not raw numbers. This one move tames the awkward truth that there are many equally-right ways to reach for a cup — the same messy multimodality that trips up regression — because a menu lets you commit to one clean choice instead of blurring them together. It also lets robotics borrow the entire language-model toolkit wholesale: next-token prediction, big pretrained backbones, discrete sampling. And because each code stands for a recurring bit of behavior, you get an interpretable vocabulary of skills you can inspect and name. The honest caveat: this is a promising minority in robotics as of 2025 — continuous diffusion policies still lead the field. The punchline that ties it to the rest of this explainer: vision tokenizes something in order to render it, and robotics tokenizes something in order to execute it.
ROBOTICS'25Discrete Policy: Learning Disentangled Action Space for Multi-Task Robotic Manipulation
Problem
When you train one policy to do many manipulation tasks at once, the range of correct motions balloons and tangles together — pouring, stacking, and wiping all pull the action distribution in different directions. This multimodal, increasingly complex spread of possible actions makes it hard for a single network to generalize, and the problem gets worse as you pile on more tasks.
Approach
The idea is to stop predicting raw motion and instead predict from a menu. Vector quantization maps whole action sequences into a discrete latent codebook, so each recurring pattern of movement becomes a reusable code — an action word. The policy then learns which task-specific codes to emit given what the camera sees and what the language instruction asks for, and a decoder turns the chosen codes back into real joint commands. Because different tasks lean on different regions of the codebook, the action space gets disentangled: the many-right-ways confusion of multi-task learning is sorted into clean, separable discrete choices. It was tested on both single-arm and bimanual setups.
Why it matters
Discretizing the action space here directly buys you relief from multimodal action spread — it beats a strong Diffusion Policy baseline by 26 to 32.5%, and the lead widens as more tasks are added, exactly when the tangle would otherwise be worst.
ROBOTICS'25Action Tokenizer Matters in In-Context Imitation Learning
Problem
Once you decide to turn continuous motion into discrete action tokens, how you chop it up matters a lot. Existing tokenizers slice action trajectories into codes without any guarantee that nearby motions map to nearby codes, so small changes in intent can snap to wildly different tokens — and the robot executes jerky, unstable movement.
Approach
LipVQ-VAE fixes the tokenizer itself rather than the policy on top of it. It trains the VQ-VAE that builds the codebook under a Lipschitz continuity condition — enforced through weight normalization — which is a mathematical promise that the mapping cannot change too fast. That promise propagates the smoothness of the raw actions all the way through to the codebook entries used in in-context imitation learning, so similar motions land on similar codes and the discretization no longer introduces sudden jumps. In short, it makes the action words respect the continuity of the physical motion they stand for.
Why it matters
This shows discretizing actions does not have to mean jerking the robot: the smoothness-constrained tokenizer lifts imitation success by over 5.3% in simulation and yields visibly smoother real-robot trajectories.
ROBOTICS'25Quart-Online: Latency-Free Multimodal Large Language Model for Quadruped Robot Learning
Problem
Running a big multimodal language model as the brain of a four-legged robot is appealing but slow — the model's inference lag stalls the control loop, and a walking robot needs commands at a high, steady rate or it stumbles. The latency simply prevents real-time control at the frequency the legs demand.
Approach
The trick is to make the language model's output menu the actions themselves. Action Chunk Discretization compresses continuous motion into a compact discrete vocabulary — short chunks of movement become a small set of action words. The MLLM is then fine-tuned to encode vision, language, and these discretized actions jointly, so producing behavior becomes the same fast operation as predicting the next token. Because the model emits a handful of action codes instead of computing raw continuous trajectories, it runs in real time at 50 Hz without degrading its language-model quality.
Why it matters
Here discretization buys raw speed: folding actions into the language model's native next-token machinery is what turns a too-slow MLLM into the first latency-free vision-language-action controller fast enough to actually drive a quadruped.
ROBOTICS'25Discrete Contrastive Learning for Diffusion Policies in Autonomous Driving
Problem
To test self-driving cars you need simulated traffic that drives in believably varied human ways — timid, aggressive, hesitant, smooth. But driving style lives in a highly varied, continuous space, and it is hard to pin down and reproduce that diversity on demand.
Approach
This one keeps a continuous diffusion policy for the actual trajectories but hands it a discrete menu of styles to follow. Contrastive learning pulls apart the distinct ways humans drive, and vector quantization crystallizes them into a discrete dictionary of driving-style codes. A conditional diffusion policy is then trained to generate trajectories conditioned on a chosen style code — so you can dial up a specific human-like behavior by picking its word from the dictionary. It is a hybrid: discrete words select the flavor, continuous diffusion renders the smooth path.
Why it matters
Discretizing style yields an interpretable, controllable vocabulary of behaviors, letting the simulator explicitly disentangle and summon diverse driving personalities for safer, more human-like test traffic than plain machine-learning baselines.

6 · Motion as words

A trajectory or a body motion is a long continuous curve — a stream of positions unrolling smoothly through time — and that shape is awkward for a transformer to spit out directly, one wobbly number at a time. So the same trick from visual tokenization moves over to movement: chop the curve into discrete motion words, snapping each little chunk of movement to the nearest entry in a learned codebook, and often do it at several resolutions at once so the same motion reads as a few coarse strokes or many fine ones. Once movement is a vocabulary, a model can compose those words like sentences, and an LLM can step in as a choreographer — planning the gist in language and letting the tokens fill in the physical detail. Discretizing also makes generation controllable: you can steer, edit, or condition on a token without the whole path smearing. And it hands you a clean coarse-plan-to-fine-motion ladder, where a high-level token picks the intent and lower-level tokens render the exact limbs or wheels. Best of all, interpretable behavior codes resist the mode-averaging that blands continuous models into a single mushy compromise between two valid choices — this is the motion mirror of visual tokenization.
ROBOTICS'25COLLAGE: Collaborative Human-Agent Interaction Generation Using Hierarchical Latent Diffusion and Language Models
Problem
The goal is to generate realistic, varied sequences of collaborative motion — two people handling an object together, for instance — where two bodies and a thing all have to move in sync. This is hard because such interaction data is scarce and the motion is genuinely complex, so a model easily produces stiff or repetitive results.
Approach
COLLAGE first learns a hierarchical VQ-VAE that encodes motion into discrete tokens at several abstraction levels — coarse tokens for the overall interaction, finer tokens for the detailed limb movements. On top of these multi-resolution motion words, a language model reads the text prompt and produces high-level planning cues, effectively acting as the choreographer that decides what the interaction should look like. Those cues then steer a latent-space diffusion model, which fills in the actual token sequence for that specific prompt. The layered vocabulary lets the system plan the gist and render the detail separately, and it beats prior methods on the CORE-4D and InterHuman benchmarks.
Why it matters
Turning motion into a hierarchy of discrete words lets an LLM plan at the top and diffusion compose the physical detail below — coarse-plan-to-fine-motion in action. Because the tokens are discrete and layered, the same prompt can yield diverse, prompt-faithful interactions instead of one averaged-out result.
ROBOTICS'25Gaze-Guided 3D Hand Motion Prediction for Detecting Intent in Egocentric Grasping Tasks
Problem
From first-person video, the system must predict where a hand is about to go — its future poses and joint paths — to guess what the person intends to grasp, so an assistive robot can help. The catch is it gets no advance knowledge of which object is the target, so it has to read intent from movement and attention alone.
Approach
The method learns a VQ-VAE over hand poses, snapping each configuration of the hand to an entry in a discrete codebook so that a whole reach becomes a short string of pose tokens. An autoregressive transformer then predicts the next future motion tokens one after another, exactly the way a language model predicts the next word. Its predictions are conditioned on the person's gaze — where the eyes are pointing — along with the recent hand motion and features of the objects in the scene. Gaze acts as an early giveaway of intent, letting the model commit to a plausible future reach even when only a few input frames are available.
Why it matters
Casting hand motion as discrete tokens turns future-motion prediction into next-word prediction, so a plain autoregressive transformer can compose a full reach from a codebook. The discrete vocabulary lets gaze steer the forecast and keeps predictions crisp even from very short observations.
ROBOTICS'25CrowdSurfer: Sampling Optimization Augmented with Vector-Quantized Variational AutoEncoder for Dense Crowd Navigation
Problem
A mobile robot has to thread through a dense, constantly shifting crowd at interactive speed. Doing this the usual way means explicitly predicting how every nearby person will move, which is brittle and slow in a packed, fast-changing space.
Approach
CrowdSurfer trains a VQ-VAE to learn a discrete prior over what good expert trajectories look like, conditioned on the robot's perception input — in effect a codebook of sensible ways to move through a crowd. At runtime it samples candidate trajectories from this learned prior, giving the robot a handful of expert-flavored guesses rather than starting from scratch. Those samples then seed a sampling-based optimizer (an MPPI-style refiner) that polishes the chosen path in real time for the exact local situation. Crucially, this pairing skips any explicit prediction of obstacle motion, yet lifts success rate 40% over the DRL-VO baseline.
Why it matters
A discrete codebook of expert moves gives the real-time optimizer strong, human-like starting points, so it converges fast instead of searching blindly. Snapping to learned motion words captures the multimodal choices a crowd demands without the fragile step of forecasting every pedestrian.
ROBOTICS'25Categorical Traffic Transformer: Interpretable and Diverse Behavior Prediction with Tokenized Latent
Problem
Traffic-behavior models for self-driving cars tend to mode-collapse — averaging genuinely different choices, like turn left versus go straight, into one mushy prediction — and their internal latent spaces are opaque. That makes it hard to produce diverse multimodal futures or to plug the model into a semantic planner.
Approach
The Categorical Traffic Transformer replaces the usual opaque latent with a fully interpretable categorical latent — tokens that stand for concrete semantic choices like which lane to take or which way to go around another car (its homotopy). Because these tokens are meaningful, they can be directly supervised from ground-truth semantic labels during training, which is what pins the model away from mode collapse. The transformer emits both a continuous trajectory and its matching discrete tokens, so every predicted path comes with a readable label of the intent behind it. This tokenized behavior latent is designed to be interoperable with language models and behavior planners.
Why it matters
Making the latent a vocabulary of discrete behavior modes lets each mode be supervised on its own, so distinct choices stay distinct instead of averaging into one bland path. The tokens double as a shared language, letting an LLM or planner read and steer the car's intended behavior.
Reading it whole. Top to bottom, the themes walk the same trick outward: first fight the collapse that kills the vocabulary, then reshape the tokenizer for the generator that writes with it, then squeeze it smaller for video and tight budgets, then load the tokens with meaning and unify the modalities — and finally hand the same snap-to-a-codebook move to a robot so it can speak in actions and motions. The data differs wildly — pixels, frames, 3D, motor commands; the primitive — snap the continuous thing to the nearest word — never changes.

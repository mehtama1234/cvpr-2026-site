The big picture

One problem, eight attacks

Start from the raw fact. A camera takes a world that is three-dimensional, in motion, and full of meaning, and flattens it into a grid of brightness numbers. Nothing in that grid says this is a face, or that wall is three metres away, or the person is about to fall. All of it is implied, none of it is stated. Computer vision is the long effort to cross that gap — to go from the numbers to the meaning, and back again. Every one of the 1,626 papers here is one attempt at some stretch of that crossing.

Going from pixels to meaning happens in layers, and each layer is a theme. The first and oldest job is simply to name and locate what is in the picture — find the objects, outline them, read the text. But the flat image threw away depth, so a huge share of the field works to recover the 3D shape the picture is only a shadow of — which distances, which surfaces, what you would see from another angle. The world also moves, so time becomes its own axis: a single frame cannot tell you whether a door is opening or closing, but a video can. And meaning, in the end, lives in language, so vision has fused with words — vision-language models that don't just label an image but describe it, answer questions about it, and reason over it.

The bridge runs both directions. Once you can go from pixels to meaning, you can run it backwards: start from an idea — a sentence, a sketch — and make the pixels that match it. That is image and video generation. And for a robot or a car, none of this is the point in itself: seeing exists so the machine can act — decide and move a real body in real time, where a mistake is not a wrong label but a wrong turn.

What makes 2026 feel like one field rather than a hundred is that everyone has quietly agreed on a few building blocks. Diffusion — the trick of starting from pure noise and cleaning it up, step by step, into something that fits a description — began as a way to draw pictures and is now the engine behind images, video, 3D scenes, even robot actions. Gaussian Splatting — storing a scene as a cloud of small soft coloured blobs that is fast to draw — has become the default way to hold 3D. And vision-language models are the connective tissue that lets seeing, describing, and deciding talk to each other. Because the same few engines are reused everywhere, a good idea in one corner lifts many others at once — which is exactly why the map of this field shows a few dense, overlapping continents rather than scattered villages.

Underneath every theme sit the same unglamorous questions, and they are a theme of their own: how does a model learn without a mountain of hand-labelled examples, keep working when the world drifts away from its training data, pick up new things without forgetting the old, and shrink small enough to actually run on a phone or a car. Get these foundations right and the flashier results survive contact with reality; get them wrong and they don't.

And at the edges, something telling is happening. Because the core now genuinely works, the field has started taking on grown-up duties it could ignore before: telling a real photo from an AI-generated fake, making a model forget a face or a style on command, and carrying vision into places the ordinary camera never went — event sensors that see only change, radar that sees through weather, microscopes, brain scans, satellites. On the landscape map these show up as small islands off the coast of the main continents: the frontier.

So the through-line of the year is convergence and consequence. Convergence: the field has settled on its materials, and the themes bleed into one another because they share them. Consequence: with the basics working, the hard questions are no longer "can it work at all" but the mature ones — can it run cheaply, can we trust it, can we catch it when it lies, can it learn without forgetting, can we make it forget on purpose. The eight themes below walk the whole bridge, in order: naming what's there, recovering 3D, seeing through time, joining vision to language, making pixels, acting in the world, the foundations that hold it all up, and the frontier opening at the edge.

Recovering the 3D world from flat pictures

turning 2D images into the shape and structure of a real scene

A photograph is flat — it throws away the one thing you often need most: how far away everything is. Recovering the three-dimensional shape of a scene from flat images is one of the oldest and hardest problems in the field, because infinitely many 3D worlds could have produced the same 2D picture. The system has to use cues, priors, and multiple views to pin down the one real answer.

Theme · 743 papers

Seeing and making things that move

adding the dimension of time — understanding and generating video

A video is not just a stack of images — it is images bound together by time, and time carries most of the meaning. A single frame cannot tell you whether a door is opening or closing, whether a person is helping or fighting, where a ball will land. Understanding video means reading change and causation across frames; generating video means producing frames that are not only individually plausible but consistent and physically believable as they flow.

Theme · 596 papers

Making pixels from meaning

the reverse bridge — turning an idea or instruction into an image

Most of vision goes from pixels to meaning. Generation runs the bridge the other way: start with meaning — a sentence, a sketch, an edit instruction — and produce the pixels that match it. The difficulty is that there are countless images that could fit any description, and the result must be not only realistic but faithful to exactly what was asked, and controllable enough to change one thing without wrecking the rest.

Teaching machines to see and talk at once

joining vision and language so systems can describe, answer, and reason

Seeing and saying are different skills, and the useful systems need both. A model that recognizes a thousand object categories still cannot answer 'is it safe to cross now?' or 'which of these charts supports the claim?' Joining vision to language lets a system take an image and a question and produce an answer in words — which is how people actually want to interact with machines that see.

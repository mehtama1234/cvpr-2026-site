Why Gaussian splatting — a 3D scene as a cloud of blobs

Gaussian splatting is how the field now captures a real place from photographs and renders it from any new angle, in real time. This page is the plain story: why storing a 3D scene is hard, the surprisingly simple idea that cracked it — a cloud of little coloured blobs you splat onto the screen — a real run where you can watch a picture assemble itself from blobs, why it dethroned the neural approach, and how a robot turns the same blobs into a map it can act in.

1 · The problem — a scene you can re-view, fast

A photograph is a single frozen viewpoint. To fly a camera through a place you photographed — to see it from angles you never stood at — you need the actual 3D scene behind the pictures, and a way to draw it. Two older answers each have a catch. A mesh of triangles is fast to draw but painful to recover accurately from photos and bad at fuzzy things like foliage or hair. A neural radiance field (NeRF) is gorgeous — a small network that, for any ray you shoot into the scene, predicts the colour and density along it — but you must query that network hundreds of times per pixel, so rendering a single frame takes seconds.

The problem, stated once: find a way to store a 3D scene that is easy to recover from ordinary photos, fast to render from any new viewpoint, and simple to edit — none of which the mesh or the per-ray network gives you at once.

2 · The reframe — don't query a network; splat a cloud of blobs

The idea: build the scene out of millions of little translucent blobs. Each blob is a 3D Gaussian — a soft ellipsoid with a position, a colour, an opacity, and a shape (how stretched and which way it points). To render a view you do not query any network: you project each blob onto the screen, where it becomes a soft 2D splat, and blend the splats front-to-back. Projecting and blending millions of blobs is exactly the kind of work a GPU does in real time. The whole cloud is then trained by plain gradient descent to match the input photos, growing new blobs where detail is missing and deleting ones that earn their keep.

render(view) = blend over blobs of colourᵢ · opacityᵢ · splatᵢ(pixel)     # project each Gaussian to the screen, weight by its 2D falloff, composite

One detail carries a surprising amount of the magic: the blobs are anisotropic — they can stretch and rotate into long thin ellipsoids. A flat wall or a sharp edge is then covered by a few well-oriented blobs instead of a swarm of round dots. The run below shows exactly how much that freedom buys.

Notice it peaks around 120 blobs (36.2 dB) and then stops improving, even dipping. This simple scene only holds so much detail — piling on more blobs without directing them where they are needed is wasted, and can even hurt. That is precisely why real Gaussian splatting uses adaptive densification: it adds blobs only where the error is high, and prunes the useless ones, matching the number of Gaussians to the actual complexity of the scene.

Why the blobs must be able to stretch

round blobs — 32.1 dB
stretchable blobs — 36.2 dB
Same budget of 120 blobs. On the left the blobs are forced to be round; on the right they may stretch and rotate. The stretchable ones score 4.1 dB higher and render crisp edges — because one long thin blob cheaply covers an edge that would take a dozen round ones. That single freedom is why real 3D Gaussian splatting uses anisotropic Gaussians.

4 · Why it took over

Gaussian splatting swept 3D vision in barely two years, and by our count it is one of the largest clusters at CVPR 2026. Three properties explain it:

propertywhy it matters
Real-time renderingprojecting and blending blobs is a rasterization job GPUs love — hundreds of frames per second, where a neural radiance field needs seconds per frame. Interactive 3D became possible.
Explicit and editablethe scene is a list of blobs you can move, delete, recolour or animate — unlike the opaque weights of a network. You can grab a chair and drag it.
Photoreal from ordinary photostrained by gradient descent on the input images with adaptive densification, it captures fuzzy, view-dependent detail that meshes miss — reconstruction quality that matched or beat NeRF at a fraction of the render cost.

Those advantages spawned a whole ecosystem in one year: feed-forward models that predict a scene's blobs in a single shot with no per-scene optimization; 4D Gaussians that move and deform for video; generative splatting that conjures 3D from a text prompt; and blobs tagged with language so you can ask a scene what is in it.

5 · The surprise — the blobs become a map a robot lives in

In computer vision, the splat cloud is admired from the outside — you render pretty new views of a scene you already captured. In robotics it flips inward: the cloud becomes a map the robot is building of the place it is in, right now. A moving camera streams frames and the robot grows its Gaussian map online, using it to localize (splat-based SLAM), to plan a path, and even to grasp — and because a robot needs metric geometry, not just a pretty picture, it fuses the blobs with LiDAR or depth for true scale.

Same blobs, a working map. Where vision builds the cloud offline and looks at it, a robot builds it online from a moving sensor, in real time, accurate enough to act on — a live digital twin it can navigate and manipulate inside, and even hand to a simulator to practice in.

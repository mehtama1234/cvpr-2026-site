Optical flow — why it works, mathematically

Two frames of a video, a fraction of a second apart. For every single pixel, which way and how far did it move between them? The whole field of motion has to be squeezed out of one thin clue — that a moving speck keeps its brightness — and that clue, on its own, is never quite enough.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

Optical flow is motion written as arrows. For each point in one frame, the model estimates where that point moved in the next frame. It turns video into a field of small displacements.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology matters because motion should usually preserve connected parts. A person's arm, a road edge, or a cell boundary should move as a related piece, not as unrelated pixels. Flow can reveal when surfaces split, merge, fold, or become hidden.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

It is used in video compression, tracking, robotics, sports analysis, fluid motion, medical imaging, and self-driving. Any field with moving measurements needs a way to connect now to next.

Why Optical flow is worth learning

It matters because change is often the evidence. One still frame can hide whether something is approaching, leaving, opening, or closing.

The problem, as a quantity

You want the motion of every point between two video frames: a little arrow at each pixel saying where that bit of the world went. The trouble is that pixels do not come labeled with arrows — all you have are two grids of brightness, and you must infer the motion that carried one into the other.

To get any grip at all you need an assumption, and the natural one is that a bit of the world keeps its brightness over a tiny slice of time. But that single assumption yields only one clue per point, and one clue turns out to be fundamentally not enough to fix a two-part motion. So the problem is twofold: turn 'brightness stays the same' into something you can solve, and then find extra information — from neighbors, or from an assumption that motion is smooth — to make up for the fact that one clue per point leaves the answer half-unknown.

The core ideas — and why they work

The one clue: a moving point keeps its brightness

dI/dt = I_x·u + I_y·v + I_t = 0, where (u,v) = (dx/dt, dy/dt)
To track a point from one frame to the next you must assume something, since the pixels carry no arrows. The natural assumption: a bit of the world does not change its brightness over a tiny slice of time — the same speck of a red shirt is still that same red an instant later, just in a slightly different place.

Written out, that says the brightness change you would see at a fixed spot must be exactly cancelled by the point moving to where the brightness is a little different. This gives one equation at each point, linking how the image brightness varies across space to how fast things are moving. It is the single clue everything else is squeezed out of — and, as the next idea shows, one clue per point is genuinely not enough to recover a motion that has two parts, a sideways and an up-down.

One clue can only see motion across an edge, not along it

u_n = -(I_t / ‖∇I‖), direction: n̂ = ∇I / ‖∇I‖ — motion along the edge vanishes from I_t
Here is the catch built into a single clue per point. Watch a long straight edge — the side of a pole — slide past through a small peephole. You can tell it is moving toward you or away, across the edge, but you cannot tell whether it is also sliding along its own length, because sliding a uniform edge lengthwise changes nothing you can see through the peephole.

So from one point's brightness clue you can recover only the part of the motion that crosses the edge; the part running along the edge is invisible. This is a real, unavoidable gap, not a weakness of any method — a straw's-eye view of a moving edge simply cannot reveal the full motion. Every technique that follows exists to bring in more information and fill this gap.

Fill the gap by assuming a small patch moves together

M·[u,v]ᵀ = -b, M = Σ_patch [I_x², I_xI_y; I_xI_y, I_y²], b = [ΣI_xI_t, ΣI_yI_t]ᵀ
One way to recover the full motion: assume a little patch of pixels all move as one. A single pixel gives one clue with the along-the-edge gap. But a small patch around it — provided it holds some texture or a corner, where brightness varies in more than one direction — gives several clues pointing different ways, and together they pin down both parts of the motion at once.

So you pool the clues from the whole patch and solve for the single motion that best fits them all. This works beautifully where there is texture, and fails exactly where you would expect it to: on a big blank region or a straight featureless edge, all the patch's clues agree with each other and still leave the along-edge motion unknown. Neighbors supply what a single point cannot — but only if those neighbors actually vary.

Or assume nearby motion is smooth and let answers spread

E(u,v) = ∬(I_xu+I_yv+I_t)² dx dy + λ∬(|∇u|²+|∇v|²) dx dy → minimize
A different way to fill the gap: assume the motion field is mostly smooth — neighboring points tend to move alike, because real surfaces move as wholes.

Then set up a tug-of-war over the whole image at once: honor each point's brightness clue where it is informative, while also pulling neighboring motions toward agreement. Where a point has no usable clue — a blank wall, or along an edge — the smoothness pull lets a confident answer from a nearby textured spot flow in and fill the void. So motion computed at corners and textures spreads across the featureless regions between them, giving a complete motion field where the pointwise clues alone left holes. A single dial trades how strictly you trust the raw clues against how smooth you insist the final motion be.

Handling big jumps: look coarse first, or test many shifts

û_{l-1} = 2·upsample(û_l) + Δu_l, cost(d) = ⟨F_1(x), F_2(x+d)⟩ for d ∈ search window D
The brightness clue quietly assumes the motion is tiny — but things in real video can leap across many pixels between frames, breaking that assumption. There are two standard fixes.

One: shrink both frames far down, where even a large jump becomes a small shift you can measure, then step back up toward full resolution, refining the estimate at each scale — a coarse guess made progressively precise. Two: for each point, directly try many candidate shifts and score how well the two frames match under each — how similar the point looks here versus there-plus-a-shift — then keep the best-matching shift. Both escape the tiny-motion assumption: one by rescaling until the motion is small, the other by openly searching over large motions. Modern methods lean heavily on the search-and-match version.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Optical-flow methods turn a few dials on the same clue.

How the edge-only gap is filled. Pool clues over a textured patch, or assume smooth motion and let confident answers spread into blank regions — most methods blend both. How big motions are handled. Shrink the frames until the motion is small, or search directly over many candidate shifts and keep the best match. How much smoothness is imposed. A dial trading trust in the raw clues against insistence on a smooth field, eased near real motion boundaries so different objects can move differently. What the flow feeds. Increasingly it is scaffolding under 3D and video understanding, or a robot judging its own motion. The thread never changes: brightness-stays-the-same turns motion into a solvable clue, and because one clue is never enough, you borrow from neighbors — by patch or by smoothness — to recover the rest.

A worked numeric example

If a pixel moves from (20,10) to (23,8), optical flow is (u,v)=(3,-2). Brightness constancy says the same surface patch should keep its intensity, so the image equation constrains motion. One pixel alone is underdetermined; neighboring smoothness supplies the missing constraint.

1Estimating the motion field
ρ(x,y)·u + ρ(x,y)·v + ρ(t) = 0 ; ρ := correlation volume in (u,v) cost-space
Search all possible shifts by computing how well a patch from frame 1 matches patches at every shifted position in frame 2, building a table of match scores ρ across all possible motions. Motion estimation picks the shift that maximizes the match. Rather than solving an equation directly, you can learn a smooth path from random noise to the solution, sidestepping the optimization entirely. This generalizes matching to every dot on the image, learning velocity fields that satisfy brightness constancy implicitly. The method treats the problem as finding a curve through shifting space rather than solving an equation in velocity space.
These four papers all tackle the core problem: building a cost table that scores every possible motion and picking the best one. They differ in how smartly they sample the table, whether they learn the velocity as a continuous path versus discrete jumps, and how they handle noise.

2Follow one point through the whole clip
W_t(p,t) := argmin_w ‖I(w(p,t),t) - I(p,0)‖² ; track via warp not correlation
Instead of building a match table and picking the best score, point tracking warps the reference patch toward the current frame by minimizing how different the pixels are. This is equivalent to solving brightness constancy locally over time: the warp that makes brightness match between frames is the trajectory. Recurrent tracking learns how confident each prediction should be alongside motion, encoding the ambiguity at edges (where motion along the edge is invisible) and passing that uncertainty through the entire sequence.
These four papers track a single dot across many frames by warping instead of lookup. They close the gap between synthetic data and real videos, extend to multiple viewpoints, and use self-teaching where the model labels its own data.

3Match the same point across wide views
f_i ~ f_j via Σ_k w_k · d_k(i,j) ; d_k := semantic descriptor, w_k := learned mixing
Large camera separations violate the assumption of small motion, so simple brightness matching fails. Instead, use learned descriptions (mathematical vectors built from images) that capture meaning to match scene understanding instead of raw pixels. The key is descriptor blending: weighted sums of different semantic costs (visual, language, geometry) resolve wide ambiguity by adding prior knowledge about what is truly the same dot across cameras or frames.
These four papers solve matching when the camera has moved far apart, where brightness alone fails. They use learned descriptions and language guidance to match across 2D and 3D.

4Motion in 3D, and where methods die
C(x₁,x₂,d) := ‖I₁(x₁) - I₂(x₂ + d·baseline/Z)‖² ; solve for (d, Z) jointly
2D motion assumes a flat surface or known distance; 3D scene motion must solve brightness constancy AND geometric constraints at the same time. Probabilistic scene motion treats this as Bayesian inference (updating probability over distance and motion using the evidence) instead of a single hard point answer. Multiple types of observations (brightness + event stream, stereo + depth) tighten the ill-posed ambiguity at edges by adding temporal and geometric cues that 2D brightness alone cannot resolve, especially in featureless regions where lines and textures align in frequency space.
These four papers extend to full 3D motion and depth, combining multiple sensors and handling tough cases where brightness and textures fail. They fuse event streams, depth, and multiple cameras to resolve ambiguity.

6Correspondence a robot acts on
p̂_next = p_t + (u,v)_t + Σ(p_t,t) ; Σ := predicted covariance in metric units
Robots need more than predictions — they need honest uncertainty in real-world distances. The key is probabilistic correspondence: every matched dot carries a learned uncertainty table encoding how far the prediction can drift. Multi-sensor fusion joins optical flow with inertial and leg odometry by treating each as a noisy observation of the same motion, weighting by the flow's per-dot confidence. This extends the smoothness idea (smooth global motion) to probabilistic fusion where flow uncertainty gates which measurements to trust.

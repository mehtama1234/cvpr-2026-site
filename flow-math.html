Optical flow — why it works, mathematically

One intensity constraint per pixel, two velocity unknowns per pixel — the aperture problem is not a bug to patch; it is a hard algebraic fact about what intensity can and cannot tell you.

The problem, as a quantity

A pixel at position (x,y) at time t has intensity I(x,y,t). If that pixel belongs to a surface patch moving with velocity (u,v), brightness constancy says the same patch carries the same intensity to its new position one frame later: I(x+u, y+v, t+1) = I(x,y,t). Linearize with a first-order Taylor expansion (valid when motion is small relative to the spatial scale of intensity change): the brightness-constancy constraint becomes one linear equation in two unknowns, I_x·u + I_y·v + I_t = 0, where I_x, I_y are the local intensity gradients and I_t is the frame-difference signal. One equation cannot pin two unknowns. This is not a measurement-noise problem — it is a rank deficiency: every pixel contributes a constraint that slices the velocity plane with one line, leaving a whole line of valid (u,v) pairs. The real problem is therefore: given that each pixel delivers one scalar measurement of a 2D quantity, what additional structure — local, global, or learned — can close the gap?

The core ideas — and why they work

Brightness constancy: one constraint from the chain rule

dI/dt = I_x·u + I_y·v + I_t = 0, where (u,v) = (dx/dt, dy/dt)
Differentiate I(x(t), y(t), t) with respect to time by the chain rule: dI/dt = I_x·(dx/dt) + I_y·(dy/dt) + ∂I/∂t = 0. Setting u = dx/dt and v = dy/dt gives the optical-flow constraint equation. The partial derivative I_t is literally what you measure — the pixel's change between frames — and I_x, I_y are the spatial gradients you estimate from a single frame. What the equation says geometrically: the constraint is a line in (u,v) space passing through the point (-I_t/I_x, 0) with slope -I_x/I_y. Every pixel hands you one such line. Only where two linearly independent lines meet does a unique velocity emerge. The equation is exact only under pure brightness constancy (rigid surface, Lambertian, no changing illumination) — real scenes violate it at occlusions, specularities, and shadows, and the whole framework inherits those limits honestly. Same primitive elsewhere: this is the Underdetermination primitive — one observation cannot pin a multi-dimensional answer — the same algebraic bind that makes monocular depth estimation require a prior (one intensity image, infinite 3D scenes project onto it) and makes SAM's segmentation masks ambiguous without a disambiguation click.

The aperture problem: only normal flow is recoverable

u_n = -(I_t / ‖∇I‖), direction: n̂ = ∇I / ‖∇I‖ — motion along the edge vanishes from I_t
At a straight edge oriented along direction ê, the intensity varies only in the perpendicular direction n̂ = ∇I/‖∇I‖. The constraint I_x·u + I_y·v + I_t = 0 then becomes ‖∇I‖·(u·n̂) = -I_t, which fixes only the scalar projection of velocity onto the gradient direction: the normal flow u_n = -I_t/‖∇I‖. The component along the edge, u·ê, cancels out of every pixel's constraint because intensity does not change as you slide along the edge. You can slide the aperture (the measurement window) along the edge forever without seeing any intensity signature of that motion. This is not a failure of algorithm design — it is a consequence of the algebra of partial derivatives. Every optical-flow algorithm must resolve this ambiguity through means outside the brightness-constancy equation itself: either by finding a location where gradients are 2D (a corner), or by coupling the pixel to its neighbors (a spatial prior). The enemy it defeats: recognizing that only normal flow is locally measurable rules out an entire family of naive local estimators and explains exactly where they will fail — any single-edge pixel will produce a velocity biased toward the gradient direction, regardless of what the surface is actually doing.

Lucas-Kanade: a window of constraints and the structure tensor

M·[u,v]ᵀ = -b, M = Σ_patch [I_x², I_xI_y; I_xI_y, I_y²], b = [ΣI_xI_t, ΣI_yI_t]ᵀ
Stack the brightness-constancy equations from all N pixels in a small spatial window into an overdetermined system: A·[u,v]ᵀ = -c where each row of A is [I_x, I_y] for one pixel. The least-squares normal equations reduce this to M·[u,v]ᵀ = -b where M is the structure tensor, the sum of outer products ∇I·∇Iᵀ over the patch. M is a 2×2 symmetric positive-semidefinite matrix, and its eigenvalues tell the whole story: if both eigenvalues are large, gradients point in two independent directions — a corner — and M is invertible, giving a unique solution. If one eigenvalue is near zero, all gradients in the patch are nearly parallel — an edge — and M is rank-deficient, recovering only normal flow. If both eigenvalues are near zero — flat region — you have nothing. The structure tensor is also exactly the object behind Harris/Shi-Tomasi corner detection: a corner is just a pixel where M is well-conditioned. Local flow and corner detection are the same linear algebra, asked in two directions. Same primitive elsewhere: gathering many weak linear constraints to form a well-conditioned normal-equations system is the Matching primitive in another guise — DETR's bipartite matching and RAFT's cost-volume correlation both ask which patch best corresponds to which, and the structure tensor is the local version of the same game.

Horn-Schunck: a smoothness prior to propagate information globally

E(u,v) = ∬(I_xu+I_yv+I_t)² dx dy + λ∬(|∇u|²+|∇v|²) dx dy → minimize
A single corner gives a unique local velocity, but most of the image is not corners. To assign velocities everywhere — including flat regions and single-edge pixels — you need to couple pixels together. Horn and Schunck (1981) write a global energy over the whole image: the first integral penalizes brightness-constraint violations, the second penalizes rapid spatial variation in u and v. Setting the functional derivatives to zero gives a pair of PDEs: at each pixel, λ∇²u = I_x(I_xu+I_yv+I_t) (and symmetrically for v), where ∇² is the Laplacian. The Laplacian averages a pixel's velocity with its neighbors — it is literally diffusion of velocity information across the image. Information from well-determined pixels (corners) diffuses outward through the smoothness prior into underdetermined regions (edges, flat areas), resolving what local brightness alone cannot. The tradeoff parameter λ controls how strongly the prior overrides the data: large λ makes the field smooth everywhere, sacrificing accuracy at genuine motion boundaries; small λ trusts data more but leaves flat regions without a valid estimate. This is the bias-variance dial of every regularized inverse problem. Same primitive elsewhere: the smoothness prior is the Propagation as smoothing primitive — iterative GNN message passing spreads information from labeled to unlabeled nodes by exactly the same Laplacian diffusion, and helpful in moderation, destructive in excess (it smears sharp motion boundaries).

Coarse-to-fine and cost-volume matching: closing the large-displacement gap

û_{l-1} = 2·upsample(û_l) + Δu_l, cost(d) = ⟨F_1(x), F_2(x+d)⟩ for d ∈ search window D
The Taylor linearization that produced I_xu+I_yv+I_t=0 is only valid when (u,v) is small relative to the spatial scale of intensity changes — roughly, the displacement must be less than the patch size. Real motion can span tens or hundreds of pixels. Coarse-to-fine pyramids handle this by halving the image resolution until the motion (measured in pyramid-level pixels) is small enough for the linear approximation to hold, estimating flow, upsampling, then refining at each finer level: û_{l-1} = 2·upsample(û_l) + Δu_l. The residual Δu_l at each level is small by construction. An alternative that is robust to the linearization failure entirely is direct correspondence search: build a cost volume by computing the inner product (correlation) cost(x,d) = ⟨F_1(x), F_2(x+d)⟩ between a feature at pixel x in frame 1 and features at displaced positions x+d in frame 2, for all displacements d in a search window. The peak of cost(x,·) gives the best-matching displacement without any linearization. RAFT builds a 4D cost volume over all pairs and then iteratively refines the flow estimate with a recurrent network reading from it — still fundamentally asking the same question as a window-based template match, but with learned features and learned aggregation. Same primitive elsewhere: the cost volume is the Matching primitive used verbatim in stereo depth estimation (search along the epipolar line for the best disparity), in DETR (match predicted boxes to ground-truth boxes), and in CLIP (match image features to text features) — the same inner-product similarity, pointed at different correspondence problems.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Lucas-Kanade (window-based): solve the 2×2 structure-tensor system per pixel over a small patch. Fast, edge-preserving, naturally gives a confidence measure (the smaller eigenvalue of M says how reliable the estimate is). Fails on flat regions and uniform-texture regions — correctly, because there is genuinely nothing to match there. Horn-Schunck (global variational): minimize a quadratic energy coupling all pixels through a smoothness prior. Fills in estimates everywhere but blurs motion discontinuities. The iterative Laplacian updates are cheap per iteration but convergence is slow. Coarse-to-fine: build an image pyramid, solve at the coarsest level (small displacements in coarse pixels), upsample and refine. Standard component in every classical and learned method; handles large real-world displacements that violate linearization. Learning-based (FlowNet, PWCNet, RAFT): train end-to-end on synthetic data (FlyingChairs, FlyingThings3D) with a supervision signal on ground-truth flow. Networks learn features that are more discriminative than raw intensity gradients, build cost volumes explicitly, and iterate refinement steps. RAFT uses a 4D correlation volume and a recurrent update operator — architecturally, it is a learned version of coarse-to-fine + cost-volume search, with a differentiable matching step replacing template correlation. The core tension — one constraint per pixel, two unknowns — is resolved the same way in every approach: either structural redundancy (corners), spatial coupling (smoothness), or exhaustive search (cost volume).

A worked numeric example

If a pixel moves from (20,10) to (23,8), optical flow is (u,v)=(3,-2). Brightness constancy says the same surface patch should keep its intensity, so the image equation constrains motion. One pixel alone is underdetermined; neighboring smoothness supplies the missing constraint.

1Estimating the motion field
ρ(x,y)·u + ρ(x,y)·v + ρ(t) = 0 ; ρ := correlation volume in (u,v) cost-space
All-pairs correlation samples the matching cost ρ at discrete (u,v) displacements, and flow estimation inverts this: the velocity (u,v) that minimizes the cost recovers the brightness-constancy residual. Flow matching reframes this as a learned density path from noise → solution, bypassing hard optimization by learning smooth velocity fields that satisfy brightness constancy implicitly. This generalizes Lucas-Kanade's corner-cost idea to the full dense case.

2Follow one point through the whole clip
W_t(p,t) := argmin_w ‖I(w(p,t),t) - I(p,0)‖² ; track via warp not correlation
Instead of building a cost volume and picking the best match, point tracking warps the reference patch toward the current frame by minimizing pixel difference. This is equivalent to solving brightness constancy locally over time: the warp w that makes I match forward is the trajectory. Recurrent tracking learns confidence alongside flow, encoding the aperture-problem uncertainty (ambiguous regions get low confidence) and propagating it through the sequence.

3Match the same point across wide views

Wide baselines violate Lucas-Kanade's small-motion assumption, so simple correlation fails. Semantic descriptors (DINO, language-guided embeddings, state-space features) bypass raw-pixel brightness constancy and match scene concepts instead. The lever is descriptor blending: weighted sums of different semantic costs (visual, text, geometry) resolve wide ambiguity by adding prior knowledge about what is really the same point across cameras or frames.

4Motion in 3D, and where methods die
C(x₁,x₂,d) := ‖I₁(x₁) - I₂(x₂ + d·baseline/Z)‖² ; solve for (d, Z) jointly
2D optical flow assumes planar motion or known depth; 3D scene flow must solve brightness constancy AND geometric constraints simultaneously. Probabilistic scene flow treats this as Bayesian inference over depth and motion, not a single hard answer. Multi-modal observations (RGB+events, stereo+depth) tighten the ill-posed aperture problem by adding temporal and geometric cues that 2D brightness constancy alone cannot resolve, especially in featureless regions where lines and textures align in frequency space.

6Correspondence a robot acts on

Robots need more than predictions—they need honest uncertainty in real-world distances. The lever is probabilistic correspondence: every matched point carries a learned covariance matrix encoding how far the prediction can drift. This extends Horn-Schunck's regularization idea (smooth global motion) to probabilistic fusion where flow uncertainty gates which measurements to trust.

The through-line. Optical flow is a single algebraic fact dressed up in many algorithms. Differentiating the brightness-constancy assumption by the chain rule hands you one linear equation per pixel in two unknowns — a line in velocity space, never a point. That line pins only the velocity component in the gradient's direction; the component along an edge is permanently invisible to intensity alone. Every optical-flow algorithm is an answer to the same question: what second source of information do you invoke to pick a point on that line? Lucas-Kanade says collect lines from neighboring pixels until the system is overdetermined — which works wherever the scene provides two independent gradient directions (corners). Horn-Schunck says couple all pixels by a smoothness prior and let information diffuse from corners to edges — which fills in everywhere but sacrifices sharp motion boundaries. Cost-volume matching says skip the linear approximation entirely and just search for the best-matching patch — which handles large displacements but costs quadratic search. Learning replaces each hand-designed piece with a trained one while keeping the same structure. The deepest connection: the underdetermination at the heart of flow — one measurement, two degrees of freedom — is exactly the same trap that makes monocular depth ambiguous, makes SAM's segmentation non-unique without a prompt, and makes any single-sensor inversion require a prior. The aperture problem is not optical flow's peculiarity; it is the general face of underdetermined inverse problems in vision, seen clearly because intensity gradients make the geometry so explicit.

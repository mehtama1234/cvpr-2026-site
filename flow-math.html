Optical flow — why it works, mathematically

Motion from intensity change — when one constraint meets two unknowns

The problem, as a quantity

A moving point keeps its brightness: I(x,y,t) = I(x+u, y+v, t+1). If you linearize with a first-order Taylor expansion, you get I_x·u + I_y·v + I_t = 0 — one equation, two unknowns (u,v). This is the aperture problem: you can only recover motion along the image gradient (perpendicular to edges); motion parallel to an edge is invisible. One gradient direction gives one constraint; motion has two degrees of freedom; you need a second independent gradient or spatial information to pin it down.

The core ideas — and why they work

Brightness constancy

foundation

A pixel's intensity doesn't change as it moves — only its position does. Differentiate I(x(t), y(t), t) with respect to time: dI/dt = I_x·(dx/dt) + I_y·(dy/dt) + I_t = 0, where (dx/dt, dy/dt) = (u,v) is the velocity. This is exact under pure translation and Lambertian surfaces. It's one linear constraint per pixel, but motion is 2D — you always need more.

Aperture problem

core limitation

At a horizontal edge (only I_y ≠ 0), the equation I_x·u + I_y·v + I_t = 0 becomes I_y·v = -I_t. You can solve for v but u is unconstrained — you cannot tell if the edge is moving left, right, or standing still along its own direction. The gradient vector points normal to edges; only motion normal to the edge leaves a brightness signature. Edges hide along-edge motion from local intensity alone.

Lucas-Kanade: corners unlock flow

local resolution

At a corner, gradients point in two independent directions (I_x and I_y both nonzero and uncorrelated). Within a small window over a corner, you have multiple independent linear equations in (u,v) — enough to solve least-squares. The corner acts as a natural anchor for 2D motion. Smooth regions and single-orientation edges still fail, but corners everywhere (textured scenes) anchor the whole field.

Horn-Schunck: smoothness prior

global resolution

Instead of requiring corners, assume the motion field is smooth: neighboring pixels move similarly. Minimize a total energy = Σ(I_x·u + I_y·v + I_t)² + λ·(‖∇u‖² + ‖∇v‖²). The first term enforces brightness constancy; the second term penalizes spatial gradients in u and v. Smooth prior propagates information from constrained regions (corners, edges) to underconstrained regions (flat areas). Trade-off λ controls smoothness strength.

Variational methods: dense, regularized flow

modern framework

Generalize Horn-Schunck to learned regularizers, anisotropic smoothness, or learned priors trained on real motion statistics. Deep networks learn to balance data (brightness) and prior (smoothness) adaptively. The core remains: one pixel = one brightness constraint, two unknowns → you need regularization or local multi-directional gradients. Learned regularizers replace hand-tuned λ.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Window-based (Lucas-Kanade): gather all brightness equations in a small patch; solve 2×2 least-squares per pixel. Fast, preserves edges, fails on flat textureless regions. Variational (Horn-Schunck, FlowNet, RAFT): minimize global energy over the whole frame with a smoothness prior or learned regularizer. Handles textureless regions, spreads information spatially, slower. Matching-based: search for the best correspondence patch between frames (template matching, cost volumes). Coarse, robust to appearance change, requires descriptors. Coarse-to-fine: estimate flow at low resolution, upsample, refine at high resolution. Handles large displacements without huge search windows. Learning-based (FlowNet, PWCNet, RAFT): train a CNN or recurrent architecture end-to-end on synthetic data (FlyingChairs, FlyingThings3D). Learn features, cost volumes, and iterative refinement implicitly.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

One notch deeper: each of the theme's 6 approach-families and the specific mathematical mechanism that makes its papers work — the exact knob turned on the core relations above. Each links to those papers in the deep-reads.

1Estimating the motion field
ρ(x,y)·u + ρ(x,y)·v + ρ(t) = 0 ; ρ := correlation volume in (u,v) cost-space
All-pairs correlation samples the matching cost ρ at discrete (u,v) displacements, and flow estimation inverts this: the velocity (u,v) that minimizes the cost recovers the brightness-constancy residual. Flow matching reframes this as a learned density path from noise → solution, bypassing hard optimization by learning smooth velocity fields that satisfy brightness constancy implicitly. This generalizes Lucas-Kanade's corner-cost idea to the full dense case.
2Follow one point through the whole clip
W_t(p,t) := argmin_w ‖I(w(p,t),t) - I(p,0)‖² ; track via warp not correlation
Instead of building a cost volume and picking the best match, point tracking warps the reference patch toward the current frame by minimizing pixel difference. This is equivalent to solving brightness constancy locally over time: the warp w that makes I match forward is the trajectory. Recurrent tracking learns confidence alongside flow, encoding the aperture-problem uncertainty (ambiguous regions get low confidence) and propagating it through the sequence.
3Match the same point across wide views
f_i ~ f_j via Σ_k w_k · d_k(i,j) ; d_k := semantic descriptor, w_k := learned mixing
Wide baselines violate Lucas-Kanade's small-motion assumption, so simple correlation fails. Semantic descriptors (DINO, language-guided embeddings, state-space features) bypass raw-pixel brightness constancy and match scene concepts instead. The lever is descriptor blending: weighted sums of different semantic costs (visual, text, geometry) resolve wide ambiguity by adding prior knowledge about what is really the same point across cameras or frames.
4Motion in 3D, and where methods die
C(x₁,x₂,d) := ‖I₁(x₁) - I₂(x₂ + d·baseline/Z)‖² ; solve for (d, Z) jointly
2D optical flow assumes planar motion or known depth; 3D scene flow must solve brightness constancy AND geometric constraints simultaneously. Probabilistic scene flow treats this as Bayesian inference over depth and motion, not a single hard answer. Multi-modal observations (RGB+events, stereo+depth) tighten the ill-posed aperture problem by adding temporal and geometric cues that 2D brightness constancy alone cannot resolve, especially in featureless regions where lines and textures align in frequency space.
5Correspondence as scaffolding
L_geo = ‖T_flow(I_t, (u,v)) - I_{t+1}‖² ; supervise 4D VAE with 2D flow predictions
Instead of hand-labeling 3D geometry and camera pose, plain 2D optical flow provides dense temporal correspondence supervision. The lever is flow as a proxy: a video diffusion model can learn dense geometry by matching frames via predicted motion, treating flow not as the end goal but as the constraint that scaffolds 4D reconstruction. This inverts the usual pipeline—flow becomes a teaching signal for geometry, not a byproduct of it.
6Correspondence a robot acts on
p̂_next = p_t + (u,v)_t + Σ(p_t,t) ; Σ := predicted covariance in metric units
Robots need more than predictions—they need honest uncertainty in real-world distances. The lever is probabilistic correspondence: every matched point carries a learned covariance matrix encoding how far the prediction can drift. Tightly-coupled SLAM fuses visual flow with inertial and leg odometry by treating each as a noisy observation of the same motion, weighting by the flow's per-pixel confidence. This extends Horn-Schunck's regularization idea (smooth global motion) to probabilistic fusion where flow uncertainty gates which measurements to trust.
The through-line.

One pixel sees only one gradient; two degrees of freedom are underdetermined. Corners (two independent gradients) or spatial smoothness priors resolve the aperture problem. Motion is observable only along the gradient; along-edge motion is invisible without additional structure.

Expectation–Maximization — why it works, mathematically

You have data with hidden causes buried inside it, and you need to learn both what the hidden causes are and the rules that generate them. Neither can be learned without the other — it's a circular dependency.

The problem, as a quantity

Imagine you hear a voice on the phone but can't see the speaker. You're trying to figure out two things at once: who is actually speaking (the hidden cause), and what the person's true voice patterns are (the settings that make that voice). Here's the trap: if you already knew who was speaking, you could learn their voice patterns by listening carefully. But to figure out who is speaking, you'd need to already know what each person's voice sounds like. You can't measure one without knowing the other, and you can't know the other without measuring the one.

This circularity appears everywhere data has invisible causes. A medical test comes back with a number, but the true state you care about (disease or no disease) is hidden. To learn what the test number *means*, you'd need to know the true state. But you don't know it. Or a customer's purchases tell you something about which product category they love most — but you never see their true preference, only their buying behavior. To learn what drives purchases, you need to know the hidden preferences. But the only way to infer preferences is to already understand what drives purchases.

The core ideas — and why they work

Fitting with a hidden cause is a chicken-and-egg problem

unknown hidden labels z and unknown settings θ, each needs the other
The circularity is real, but it has a way out: make a guess. Start with a rough guess about what the hidden causes might be — don't overthink it. The guess doesn't need to be right; it just needs to be something to build on. Once you have a guess, the circular dependency breaks: you can now talk about what settings would explain the data *if your guess were true*. This is the insight that unlocks the whole mechanism. You're not solving both problems at once anymore. You're solving a sequence of simpler problems: "Given that the hidden causes are probably this, what settings fit best?" repeated over and over, each time refining your guess.

Guess the hidden part from the current settings (the E-step)

q(z) = p(z | x, θ_old) — soft guess of the hidden cause
In this step, you ask: given my current settings, what are the hidden causes probably doing? You compute a soft guess — not a hard bet on one answer, but a likelihood: the hidden cause is *probably* this with high confidence, *probably* that with lower confidence. This is called conditioning: you're updating what you believe about the hidden part based on the observed data and the settings you already believe in. You're asking the data, through the lens of your current settings, to tell you a story about the hidden world. The formula reverses the direction: instead of "if I knew the hidden causes, what would I expect to see?" you ask "given what I saw, what were the hidden causes probably doing?" This is Bayes' rule doing its job: taking new information (the observed data) and updating old beliefs (your guess about what the hidden causes are) into new beliefs (the soft guess).

Then re-fit the settings as if the guess were true (the M-step)

θ_new = argmax_θ E_q[ log p(x, z; θ) ]
Now that you have a soft guess about the hidden causes, treat it as true. This sounds crazy — you know it's just a guess — but here's why it works. You're not claiming the guess is definitely right. You're saying: "Let me play-act as if this guess were true, and see what settings would be most sensible in that world." You pick the settings that would make the most sense if the guess were true. This is optimization: find the settings that make the observed data look as expected as possible, *weighted by how likely each guess about the hidden cause is*. You're not re-running the data through the network a billion times. You're taking the soft guess, using it as weights on different outcomes, and finding the one choice of settings that best explains what you observed under those weights.

Alternating never makes it worse — it climbs to a local best

each round: the fit log p(x; θ) does not decrease
Here's the surprising part: even though you're alternating between guesses and settings, and every intermediate step is based on a guess that's probably wrong, the fit never gets worse. Each cycle: you guess the hidden causes (step one), then you re-fit the settings (step two). On the next cycle, your guess improves because the settings are better. The fit — measured by how expected your data is under your current guess and settings — never decreases. It climbs. It might not reach the absolute best possible answer (there might be higher peaks), but it will find and stick to a local high point. This is because each step is designed not to waste what you learned in the previous step. The guess improves the settings, the settings improve the guess, and the overall score keeps rising.

The approaches, as variations on the math

The core loop stays the same — guess the hidden causes, re-fit the settings, repeat — but you control several dials that change how the algorithm behaves. How soft is the guess? You can make your guesses sharper (nearly all the probability on one answer) or softer (spread across many possibilities). How aggressive is the re-fitting? You can take one big step toward the new settings, or many small cautious steps. How many times do you loop? More loops mean a better answer, but more cost. What's your starting guess? A lucky start can find a better answer faster. The thread never changes: all methods alternate between softly updating your belief about the hidden world and then optimizing the settings that explain what you see.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

One notch deeper: each of the theme's 4 approach-families and the specific mathematical mechanism that makes its papers work — the exact knob turned on the core relations above. Under each, the specific mathematical note for every paper in that family.

1Hard versus soft cluster membership
r_ik ∈ {0,1} (K-means) vs γ_ik ∈ [0,1] (GMM); γ_ik = π_k N(x_i | μ_k, Σ_k) / Σ_j π_j N(x_i | μ_j, Σ_j)
K-means forces each point into exactly one cluster by making r_ik a 0-or-1 flag, then updates cluster centers as means of assigned points. Gaussian Mixture Models replace this with soft responsibilities γ_ik, a number between 0 and 1 that says how much each point belongs to each cluster. The E-step computes these responsibilities from the current model using Bayes-style normalizing: take how likely the point looks under cluster k, multiply by the mixture weight π_k, and divide by the total likelihood under all clusters. Points on cluster boundaries now carry partial blame for multiple centers, so centers update via weighted averages rather than hard counts, capturing overlapping populations and measurement uncertainty.
2Flexible component shapes
Fixed spherical covariance Σ = σ²I (K-means) → per-component Σ_k (GMM); Σ_k = (1/N_k) Σ_i γ_ik (x_i - μ_k)(x_i - μ_k)ᵀ
K-means assumes all clusters are round balls of the same size because every point-to-center distance is weighted equally. A GMM learns a separate shape table Σ_k for each cluster, allowing some to be elongated ellipses, others tight spheres, and some to be oriented at different angles. The M-step updates these covariance matrices using responsibility-weighted averages: each point contributes its deviation from the cluster mean, scaled by how much that cluster claims it. This flexibility lets the model match real population geometries—such as disk stars versus halo stars with genuinely different patterns in feature space—rather than forcing all clusters into the same mold.
3Latent-probability estimation in the E-step
γ_ik = π_k N(x_i | μ_k, Σ_k) / Σ_j π_j N(x_i | μ_j, Σ_j)
The E-step is the core innovation of EM: given the current best guess at cluster centers, covariances, and mixture weights, compute the probability that each cluster generated each point. For K-means, this simplifies to nearest-center assignment. For GMM, it uses Bayes' rule: the numerator is how likely the point would be under cluster k if that cluster's assumptions are true, times π_k (the cluster's overall frequency). The denominator normalizes across all clusters so the responsibilities sum to 1. This inversion—going from 'what do the cluster parameters say about the points?' to 'what do the points say about which cluster they came from?'—breaks the circular dependency: you can guess the hidden assignments now and fit better parameters next.
4Monotonic improvement with model complexity trade-offs
Each EM iteration increases or maintains log-likelihood L; BIC = -2ln(L̂) + P·ln(N); AIC = -2ln(L̂) + 2P
EM's key guarantee is that each iteration climbs: the log-likelihood L never goes down, only up or stays flat at convergence. However, EM typically finds a local peak, not a global one, so different starting guesses can lead to different local optima. To choose between models (different K, different covariance structures), you cannot simply compare likelihoods because more clusters always fit better. BIC and AIC penalize likelihood by complexity: BIC adds P·ln(N), where P is the number of free parameters and N is sample size, making it more conservative as data grows. AIC adds 2P, a fixed penalty that cares more about predictive accuracy. By comparing -2ln(L̂) plus penalty across candidate models, you balance fit against overfitting, letting the data tell you how many clusters are truly present.

What breaks without this

What goes wrong. Without EM, hidden labels can leave training in a loop: you need labels to fit the model, but you need the model to guess the labels.

Why it goes wrong. Many data points come from causes you cannot see. A point may partly belong to one cause and partly to another.

What this idea gives you. EM alternates between guessing the hidden causes and refitting the model. Each step makes the current story fit the data no worse than before.

The through-line. The core problem is learning both an invisible cause and the rules that generate data from it, when each one requires knowing the other first. Most learning algorithms assume you already have the full picture; this one handles missing pieces by treating them as a guessing game, played between two steps. Two plain ideas carry it: (1) break the circularity by guessing first, then re-fitting the settings as if the guess were true instead of trying to solve both at once, and (2) trust that alternating between these guesses and fits always improves the fit — so the climb never reverses, even though every intermediate guess is probably wrong.

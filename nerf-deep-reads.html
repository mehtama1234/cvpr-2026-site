1 · The original job, and the cost fight

The original job a NeRF was invented to do is simple to state: take a handful of ordinary photos of a scene and let you fly a camera anywhere, seeing views nobody ever shot. Nobody re-proposes plain vanilla NeRF anymore, though. The frontier work here does one of two things instead: it hardens the idea against real-world nuisances like shadows and passing people, or it makes the rendering step fast enough to compete with Gaussian splatting. The catch that drives all of this is cost. To decide the colour of a single pixel, a NeRF sends an imaginary ray out through that pixel and asks its network what is there at hundreds of points along the way, then blends the answers — that is volume rendering, and doing it hundreds of times per pixel is painfully slow. That slowness is exactly why Gaussian splatting overtook NeRF for everyday use, so the papers that still render the honest volumetric way spend most of their effort attacking that query cost, very often by baking the scene into a mesh the graphics card already knows how to draw fast.
CVPR'26Radiance Meshes for Volumetric Reconstruction
Problem
There is a frustrating trade-off at the heart of scene reconstruction: the shapes a computer can draw fast (like meshes of triangles) are hard to fit to photos, while the representations that fit photos easily (like neural fields) are slow to draw. Gaussian splatting tries to have both but pays for it with popping flicker, limited camera support, and rendering that is still slower than plain mesh drawing.
Approach
This paper carves the space of the scene into a mesh of tetrahedra — solid four-cornered cells, packed like a 3D jigsaw using Delaunay tetrahedralization. Each cell is given one constant fog density and a smoothly-varying colour, so the whole scene becomes a mesh the graphics card already knows how to rasterize at full speed. The clever part is that drawing this mesh computes the exact volume-rendering equation rather than an approximation, so there is no cheating and no popping. A Zip-NeRF-style backbone keeps the optimization stable even as the mesh has to change which cells touch which. The result is honest volume rendering that runs faster than splatting instead of slower.
Why it matters
This is a direct answer to the query-cost problem that let splatting win: by meshing the field, it turns hundreds of slow network lookups per ray into one fast pass over triangles, coming out 32% quicker than Gaussian splatting at 1440p while keeping higher quality and no flicker.

CVPR'26DiffSoup: Direct Differentiable Rasterization of Triangle Soup for Extreme Radiance Field Simplification
Problem
Splatting looks great but leans on millions of tiny primitives, which is far too heavy to ship to a phone, a headset, or a laptop. The goal here is extreme slimming: capture the same scene in a few thousand plain textured triangles that any standard graphics engine can draw without special tricks.
Approach
DiffSoup distills a radiance field down to a loose pile of textured triangles — a triangle soup — and trains those triangles directly against the photos. The hard part is that a solid, opaque triangle has a sharp edge, and sharp edges normally block the gradient signal that training needs; earlier methods blurred the edges to get around this. Instead DiffSoup uses stochastic opacity masking, randomly flipping each triangle fully on or off, which lets training feel the effect of moving an opaque edge without softening it. Compact neural textures then paint the fine high-frequency detail back onto the sparse triangles. The whole scene ends up as ordinary opaque geometry that slots straight into a normal rendering pipeline.
Why it matters
This attacks cost from the deployment side: fewer than 20,000 triangles replace millions of primitives and run at 3,500 frames per second on a smartphone, turning an expensive radiance field into something a pocket device can render effortlessly.

CVPR'26MU-GeNeRF: Multi-view Uncertainty-guided Generalizable Neural Radiance Fields for Distractor-aware Scene
Problem
A generalizable NeRF learns to reconstruct new scenes from just a few photos without retraining, but real photos are messy: shadows shift, people walk through, objects move. These transient distractors disagree from view to view, poison the training signal, and drag the reconstruction down — and worse, existing methods often mistake genuine solid structure for a distractor and erase it.
Approach
The key idea is to split the notion of doubt into two separate kinds of uncertainty. Source-view uncertainty looks across the input photos and flags places where they structurally disagree with each other, the fingerprint of occlusion or movement. Target-view uncertainty looks at the specific view being reconstructed and flags anomalies that only appear there, the fingerprint of a passing distractor. These two signals feed a heteroscedastic loss that automatically turns down the training weight on pixels it distrusts, so distractors get quietly ignored while real geometry is preserved. Because the doubt is estimated on the fly from the views themselves, it works across scenes with no per-scene optimization.
Why it matters
Here the fight is about robustness rather than raw speed: by reasoning carefully about which pixels to trust, it matches slow scene-specific distractor-free NeRFs while keeping the fast, no-retraining generalizable setting intact.

CVPR'26RenderFlow: Single-Step Neural Rendering via Flow Matching
Problem
Turning a scene representation into a finished photorealistic image the traditional way means either an expensive rendering simulation or a slow chain of iterative refinement steps, and both are too heavy for real-time use. The question is whether the whole slow loop can be collapsed into a single shot.
Approach
RenderFlow borrows an idea from modern generative models called flow matching, which learns a smooth path that carries one thing into another. Here it learns the path that carries a raw scene representation straight to its finished rendered image. By training this flow across many diverse scenes, the model absorbs a general sense of how scenes turn into pictures, rather than memorizing one scene. At inference a single forward pass — one step, not an iterative loop — produces the photorealistic output. It also folds in knowledge from path tracing so the single step stays physically grounded and robust.
Why it matters
This confronts the cost problem head-on by deleting the loop entirely: what used to take many rendering iterations becomes one step, matching the quality of iterative methods at a fraction of the time.

2 · Moving scenes and clean surfaces

A static field learns one frozen snapshot, so it simply cannot represent a scene where things move. The fix is to add a time axis: one line warps a single canonical field per frame with a deformation field, while another carries a latent scene-state that evolves continuously over time, so you can query moments the camera never actually saw. This is the niche where continuous-time neural fields edge out frame-by-frame splatting, which stitches together discrete per-frame reconstructions. Separately, and pointing at a different weakness, instead of asking the network for colour and density another line makes it output a signed distance to the nearest surface. The zero level-set of that distance — the exact place where inside flips to outside — is the surface, which yields clean watertight geometry that a fuzzy cloud of density blobs can never give you. Both tricks lean on the same quiet advantage: the field is a smooth continuous coordinate-function, so a time axis or a sharp zero-crossing slot in naturally where discrete blobs would be awkward.
CVPR'26Node-RF: Learning Generalized Continuous Space-Time Scene Dynamics with Neural ODE-based NeRFs
Problem
Existing dynamic NeRFs only capture motion inside the window they were trained on, and fall apart the moment you ask them about a time before or after the recorded clip. They also cannot generalize across different motion sequences, so each new trajectory means starting over. What is missing is a way to reason about unseen moments in time.
Approach
Node-RF stops treating each frame as a separate thing and instead holds the whole scene as a single latent state — a compact vector describing where everything is right now. A learned Neural ODE then says how that state changes from one instant to the next, so the state flows smoothly and continuously through time rather than jumping frame to frame. To draw a picture at any moment you want, the state at that timestamp is handed to a NeRF renderer, which turns it into geometry and appearance. Because the motion is captured as a continuous law rather than memorised frames, you can integrate the ODE past the end of the clip to extrapolate far into the future, and even start it from an initial condition it never saw to predict a brand-new trajectory. All of this is trained only on photometric loss over multi-view image sequences, with no optical-flow or depth supervision.
Why it matters
Modelling the scene as a continuously evolving latent state, rather than a stack of independent frames, is exactly what lets it answer questions about moments no camera ever captured.

CVPR'26RF4D:Neural Radar Fields for Novel View Synthesis in Outdoor Dynamic Scenes
Problem
Neural fields for novel-view synthesis lean on cameras and LiDAR, both of which degrade badly in rain, snow, and fog — exactly the outdoor driving conditions where you most need reliable perception. Radar sees through that weather, but nobody had built a neural field that takes radar as its primary input. The scenes are also dynamic, with the world moving while the sensor does.
Approach
RF4D rebuilds the neural-field idea around radar instead of pixels, modelling radar reflectivity and flow directly in a 4D space-time field. Radar returns are sparse, so rather than expecting dense supervision it uses occupancy-aware rendering: the handful of radar points it does get are used to pin down where surfaces are and constrain the geometry. To cope with a moving world it decomposes the field along the time axis, separating what is static from what is changing frame to frame. The result is a field you can query from viewpoints the radar never physically occupied, synthesising novel views even when the weather has blinded ordinary cameras.
Why it matters
Putting the whole thing in a continuous 4D field lets a weather-robust but sparse sensor still generate coherent novel views, which frame-by-frame camera methods cannot do once the air is full of rain or fog.

CVPR'26ManifoldNeuS: Manifold-aware View Optimizability for Pose-Free Neural Surface Reconstruction
Problem
NeuS-style neural surface reconstruction normally demands accurate camera poses to start, and in the real world precise poses are expensive or simply unavailable. Trying to go pose-free tangles two problems together: you cannot pick informative views without knowing poses, and you cannot recover poses without good views. That chicken-and-egg deadlock is what stalls existing pose-free methods.
Approach
ManifoldNeuS keeps the core idea of representing the shape as an implicit signed distance function, whose zero-crossing is the surface, and asks a different question: which views are actually easy to optimize, judged by the intrinsic geometry of the problem rather than by pose. It studies the optimization manifold — the gradient flow and curvature of the reconstruction landscape — to score how optimizable each view is without ever needing a pose. Views are then selected and ordered by these manifold properties, so the reconstruction climbs a well-behaved part of the landscape first and converges more reliably. Camera parameters are refined jointly with the surface through differentiable rendering, so poses and geometry improve together instead of one being frozen. The upshot is high-quality SDF surfaces recovered with no pose initialization at all.
Why it matters
Because the geometry lives as a signed-distance field, its zero level-set gives a clean, consistent surface, and reasoning about that field’s optimization landscape is what breaks the pose-versus-view deadlock.

CVPR'26Opti-NeuS: Neural Reconstruction for Dual-Layered Transparent and Opaque Objects
Problem
Reconstructing transparent objects is hard because light bends as it passes through, and the network struggles to tell a genuine refraction apart from the background seen behind the glass. Real objects often make this worse by being dual-layered — a transparent shell wrapped around an opaque core, like a bottle holding a solid inside. Standard neural reconstruction has no way to keep those two materials straight.
Approach
Opti-NeuS extends the NeuS-style implicit-surface renderer to handle both layers at once, treating the transparent shell and the opaque contents as different materials with different light behaviour. It uses material-aware volume rendering so that light is accounted for differently depending on whether a ray is passing through glass or hitting a solid. Crucially it adds refraction-aware ray tracing: instead of assuming light travels straight, it models how rays bend at the transparent boundary, which is what lets refractions be explained rather than mistaken for background. Surface normals and boundary detection supply geometric constraints that keep the two nested surfaces sharp and separated. The whole thing still resolves to clean signed-distance surfaces, now one for the transparent outer layer and one for the opaque inner object.
Why it matters
Because the surface is defined by an SDF zero-crossing rather than a fuzzy blob cloud, it can carve out two crisp nested watertight shells, and folding refraction into the rendering is what makes that possible for see-through objects.

3 · Where there are no blobs to splat

This is where neural fields genuinely thrive, precisely because there is nothing to splat: the scene is not a surface studded with colored points but a continuous physical quantity spread through space, like X-ray attenuation in a body, plasma emissivity glowing around a black hole, radio power filling a room, or the height field a microscope traces over a sample. For all of these, a coordinate network paired with a physics-based volume-rendering forward model is the natural choice: you feed in a location, the network answers with the physical value there, and a known physical law turns those values back into the measurements you actually recorded. Because the answer is a smooth function of position rather than a bag of discrete blobs, there is simply no set of Gaussians to place, and the continuous field is the honest representation of the thing itself. The same coordinate-in, value-out network, an implicit neural representation, is also studied on its own terms, as a general tool for fitting arbitrary signals. There the central question is spectral bias: plain coordinate networks lean toward smooth outputs and struggle with sharp edges, so a line of work asks how to fix that so a single network can capture both crisp detail and gentle background. Wherever the signal is continuous, non-RGB, and bound by real physics, the neural field is genuinely the right model and not a leftover habit from NeRF.
CVPR'26Dynamic Black-hole Emission Tomography with Physics-informed Neural Fields
Problem
Astronomers want to reconstruct the glowing, swirling plasma around a black hole, both how bright it is at each point and how fast it is moving, from the sparse and noisy signals the Event Horizon Telescope collects. Each observation gives only a handful of measurements, so the problem is badly under-determined: many different plasma configurations could explain the same faint data.
Approach
The method fits two coordinate networks at once, one that outputs the emissivity (how much light) at any point in space and time, and one that outputs the plasma velocity there. To tie these fields back to what the telescope actually saw, it traces light along the bent, relativistic paths that rays truly follow near a black hole, implemented as a physics-based propagation loss that pushes the rendered image to match the telescope measurements. A velocity prior derived from an accretion model keeps the recovered motion physically plausible, and a decay term encourages the emissivity to stay compact rather than smearing everywhere. Nothing here is a splat: the plasma is a continuous glowing quantity, and the geodesic ray-tracing is the correct forward model. The result is a dynamic reconstruction whose emissivity and velocity stay consistent over time and honor the physics better than static baselines.
Why it matters
The unknown is a continuous physical field, plasma light and motion in space, so a coordinate network with a real relativistic volume-rendering model fits far more naturally than any collection of discrete blobs. The physics is not decoration but the forward model itself, which is what makes such sparse, noisy data solvable at all.

CVPR'26Generalizable Radio-Frequency Radiance Fields for Spatial Spectrum Synthesis
Problem
Predicting how radio signals spread through a room, including reflections, shadows, and multipath, is central to wireless planning, but current neural methods must be retrained from scratch for every new environment and need dense on-site measurements. Earlier NeRF-style approaches for radio overfit to a single scene and simply fail when moved to an unseen room.
Approach
The paper builds a generalizable radio-wave field, grounded in a proven result that the spectrum at a new transmitter location can be interpolated from spectra measured at nearby transmitters, with error tied to how curved the environment is. A geometry-aware Transformer encoder reads the spectra and positions of neighboring transmitters and distills them into a latent radio radiance field, capturing path loss, shadowing, and multipath without ever training on the target scene. A neural ray-tracing step then shoots directional rays from the receiver, samples points along each path, and, using that latent field, models the complex-valued signal and attenuation at every point, aggregating them with proper free-space loss and phase shifts to produce the full spatial spectrum. Again there is nothing to splat: radio power is a continuous quantity filling space, and ray-based propagation is its honest forward model. Because the field is conditioned on neighbors rather than baked into one scene, it transfers to unseen rooms with no retraining.
Why it matters
Radio power is a continuous physical field governed by propagation physics, so a coordinate-and-ray formulation is the natural model rather than a legacy of visual NeRF. Conditioning the field on nearby observations is what lets a single network generalize across rooms instead of memorizing one.

CVPR'26SASNet: Spatially-Adaptive Sinusoidal Networks for INRs
Problem
A popular kind of coordinate network uses sine activations to fit signals with fine detail, but it suffers from frequency leakage: because each sine neuron acts across the whole signal at once, a single chosen frequency either blurs smooth regions or sprinkles high-frequency noise into them. Real signals mix sharp edges with smooth backgrounds, so one global frequency setting can never be right everywhere.
Approach
SASNet keeps the frequency structure of the network fixed and instead learns where each frequency is allowed to act. It freezes the frequency-embedding layer, whose spectrum is fully determined by the input frequencies, and multiplies each sine layer by a spatially varying mask, a value between zero and one at every location, produced by a lightweight hash-grid network. This makes high-frequency neurons switch on only in the parts of the signal that actually contain sharp detail, and stay quiet over smooth regions. Neurons are grouped by frequency magnitude to keep the number of masks small, and a sparsity penalty discourages turning on frequencies that are not needed. The upshot is a network that localizes its detail spatially, curing frequency leakage without adding meaningful parameters. It sets the best numbers across image fitting, volumetric smoke reconstruction, and signed-distance shape fitting, and reaches strong baselines many times faster.
Why it matters
This studies the coordinate-in, value-out network purely as a signal-fitting tool, addressing its core weakness, spectral bias, so it can hold both crisp edges and gentle backgrounds at once. Fixing where each frequency lives is exactly the kind of methodology a neural field needs to represent sharp, physically varying signals faithfully.

CVPR'26GH-NAF: Grid-Adaptive Hash-Level-Attended Neural Attenuation Fields for Discrepancy-Aware CBCT
Problem
Cone-beam CT reconstructs a body from a small number of X-ray projections, but real projections are corrupted by scatter, noise, and beam-hardening that ideal models ignore. When a neural attenuation field fuses its multi-resolution features uniformly, these discrepancies tangle coarse and fine detail together, producing spurious textures and blurred material boundaries.
Approach
GH-NAF represents the scene as a neural attenuation field, a coordinate network whose output at each point is how strongly tissue there absorbs X-rays, rendered back into projections by the physics of X-ray transmission. Its key idea is to stop fusing the hash-grid resolution levels blindly and instead weight them with a grid-adaptive, hash-level attention mechanism. That attention is guided by learned uncertainty and by how fast the attenuation is changing in space, so smooth low-frequency regions and sharp high-frequency boundaries are handled by the levels best suited to each. This disentangles frequency content that would otherwise contaminate one another, all without needing any extra supervision that labels the discrepancies. There are no blobs here either: attenuation is a continuous physical field through the body, and X-ray transmission is its forward model. The result is better contrast within materials and cleaner reconstructions than prior NeRF- and attenuation-field methods on both synthetic and real CBCT data.
Why it matters
X-ray attenuation is a continuous physical quantity, so a coordinate network with a transmission-based forward model is the natural fit rather than a splatted approximation. Making the network aware of frequency and uncertainty is what lets it stay stable under the messy, non-ideal physics of real scans.

4 · Where it lost ground

This is the clearest place neural fields lost ground. The DreamFusion-era idea was to optimize a neural field until its rendered images matched what a 2D image diffusion model expected, which conjured 3D shapes out of nothing but a text prompt, and distilling CLIP features into a neural feature field gave you 3D you could edit by typing words. But at CVPR 2026 almost all text-to-3D now spits out meshes or Gaussians directly, either through models trained natively on 3D or through diffusion run in a compressed latent space, and language-embedded 3D overwhelmingly carries its meaning per-Gaussian rather than in a continuous field. The field-native survivors are the minority, and they survive for a specific reason: either an implicit distance function is genuinely the thing being generated, or a continuous feature function over a surface is simply the cleaner mathematical model of the problem. We read these survivors honestly here, rather than pretend the migration to splatting and mesh diffusion did not happen.
CVPR'26LoG3D: Ultra-High-Resolution 3D Shape Modeling via Local-to-Global Partitioning
Problem
Generating detailed 3D shapes is hard when the shapes have awkward geometry, like thin sheets, open edges, or surfaces that do not cleanly enclose a solid volume. The usual trick of storing a shape as a signed distance field needs expensive cleanup to make every model watertight first, and it breaks on open or self-touching geometry. Point clouds dodge that but come out speckled and full of gaps, and existing compressors cannot hold on to fine detail as the resolution climbs.
Approach
LoG3D trains a 3D variational autoencoder, a network that squeezes a shape into a compact code and then rebuilds it, but the thing it generates is an unsigned distance field. Unsigned means it only records how far you are from the surface, never which side you are on, so it can describe open sheets and non-manifold shapes that signed fields choke on. To reach very high resolution without blurring detail, it chops the field into a grid of small uniform blocks and processes them locally with 3D convolutions, then stitches the blocks back into a coherent whole using a sparse transformer that lets distant blocks talk to each other. A Pad-Average step smooths the seams so block boundaries do not leave visible cracks. This lets the model scale all the way to a 2048-cubed grid, a level of detail that VAE-based methods had not reached before.
Why it matters
This is a field-native survivor because the distance field is not a rendering stand-in here, it is the actual thing being generated, and the unsigned variant unlocks topologies that mesh and splat pipelines handle awkwardly. Generation moved to meshes and Gaussians almost everywhere, but when open and non-manifold geometry is the point, an implicit field is still the honest representation to synthesize.

CVPR'26Semantic Foam: Unifying Spatial and Semantic Scene Decomposition
Problem
You can reconstruct a scene beautifully for novel views with Gaussian splatting or a NeRF, but cutting it into clean, editable objects is still messy. Semantic labels bleed across occlusion boundaries, supervision from 2D masks is inconsistent between views, and point-based methods have no idea what to do with the primitives buried inside an object where no camera ever saw them.
Approach
Semantic Foam builds on Radiant Foam, a representation that already carves space into a Voronoi mesh, meaning space is tiled into cells where every point belongs to its nearest seed. To each cell it attaches an explicit learned semantic feature, so meaning lives directly in the volumetric decomposition rather than being painted on afterward. It then adds a smoothness penalty defined over which cells touch which, using the Voronoi adjacency graph, so neighboring cells are encouraged to agree on their labels and features stay consistent through the interior. Because the labels are anchored to actual volume cells, you can extract, delete, or insert whole objects cleanly without leaving floating fragments behind. It beats Gaussian-based segmentation methods like Gaussian Grouping and SAGA by roughly 2 to 6 percent mIoU on three real datasets.
Why it matters
This is a field-native survivor because a continuous, volume-filling feature field is exactly what fixes the interior-and-occlusion problem that per-Gaussian labeling stumbles on. The mainstream now attaches semantics per-Gaussian, but Semantic Foam shows the field-style answer is still the cleaner one when you need editable, interior-aware decomposition rather than a surface sprinkle of labels.

CVPR'26Learning Convex Decomposition via Feature Fields
Problem
Breaking a shape into a handful of convex pieces is a workhorse operation for physics simulation, collision checking, and animation, but doing it well is genuinely hard. The classic exact methods are NP-hard and rely on slow branch-and-bound search, while earlier learned methods only worked on narrow, curated object categories rather than the messy open-world assets that generative pipelines now produce.
Approach
The authors learn a continuous feature field defined along a shape's surface, a function that assigns every surface point a vector such that points on the same convex part end up with similar vectors. The training signal comes straight from the textbook definition of convexity: for a convex region, the straight line between any two of its points stays inside the shape, so a self-supervised contrastive loss pulls together point pairs whose connecting segment is contained and pushes apart pairs whose segment pokes outside. Once trained, simply clustering the learned features hands you the convex parts, and you can dial the clustering to get coarser or finer decompositions. A feedforward network makes this fast and scalable across large open-world datasets, and it generalizes across input types, working on meshes, CAD models, and even Gaussian splats.
Why it matters
This is a field-native survivor because a continuous function over the surface is simply the cleaner model of a smoothly varying part assignment than any discrete primitive would be. CVPR'26InstructMix2Mix: Consistent Sparse-View Editing Through Multi-View Model Personalization
Problem
Editing a 3D scene by typing an instruction, like make the chair red, is easy to describe and hard to do consistently. A 2D image editor such as InstructPix2Pix will happily edit each viewpoint on its own, but the separate edits disagree with each other and the 3D result comes out incoherent. It is worst in the sparse-view setting, where only a few reference images exist to pin things down.
Approach
InstructMix2Mix keeps the score distillation skeleton, the DreamFusion-style loop where a 2D diffusion model teaches a 3D student by scoring its renders, but makes one pointed swap: the student is no longer the 3D scene itself, it is SEVA, a multi-view diffusion model that already reasons across viewpoints. The 2D teacher, InstructPix2Pix, supplies the edit instruction, and its guidance is backpropagated into SEVA's weights instead of into a neural field or splat cloud. The clever piece is Random Cross-View Attention: at each step one frame is picked at random as a key frame and every other frame in the teacher's pass attends to that frame's tokens instead of its own, which softly forces all the views to agree without ever retraining the teacher. It reaches a state-of-the-art CLIP Directional Consistency of 0.342, and rival methods like I-N2N collapse entirely in the sparse-view regime.
Why it matters
This is the most honest survivor of all, because it survives by abandoning the field as the optimization target: the neural scene is no longer the student, a multi-view diffusion model is. It keeps the DreamFusion mechanism alive precisely by moving the burden off the neural field, which is the migration this theme is about, seen from the inside.

5 · A map you build as you move, and geometry to grasp

A robot exploring a room has to build the map while it drives, from a camera or LiDAR that never stops moving — it cannot stroll around collecting every photo first the way a vision researcher does. So instead of fitting one neural field to a finished pile of images, it grows the field incrementally, folding in one keyframe at a time, and at the same instant it has to figure out where it is, fighting drift, moving people, and the sheer cost of shooting a ray through the field for every pixel. That is the SLAM half of this theme: map and pose, learned live, together. The other half is grabbing things. To pick up an object a robot needs its full 3D shape, but the hardest objects — glass, shiny metal, a mug half-buried in clutter — are exactly the ones a depth camera fails to see, returning noise or nothing. A neural field can fill in that missing, continuous geometry from just a few views, and a grasp is then simply read off the reconstructed surface. The very same trick — a continuous function you can query anywhere for is this point solid or empty, and how far to the nearest surface — even lets the robot model its own moving arm, so it can reach into a tight space without clipping itself. The through-line is that in robotics the answer you want out of the field is never colour or a prettier picture: it is geometry and free space — where the surface is, and where it is safe to move.
ROBOTICS'25DVN-SLAM: Dynamic Visual Neural Slam Based on Local-Global Encoding
Problem
Neural implicit SLAM builds its map as a single learned field, but that field has limited capacity to hold a whole scene, it is uncertain about what it is rendering, and it quietly breaks when objects in the room actually move. A map that assumes the world stands still falls apart the moment a person walks through it.
Approach
DVN-SLAM keeps two views of the scene at once and fuses them: a global encoding that captures the room-scale structure and a local encoding that pins down fine detail, so one field can hold both the big shape and the sharp edges. It adds an information concentration loss that pushes the scene information onto the actual object surfaces rather than smearing it through empty space, which cuts the rendering uncertainty. Crucially it copes with moving objects without leaning on semantic segmentation or optical-flow priors — no separate network telling it what is a person or which pixels are drifting. That makes the dynamic-scene handling self-contained inside the SLAM field itself. The result is a NeRF-style map that stays consistent even when the world in front of the camera does not.
Why it matters
This is the map-as-you-move problem in its purest form: the sensor is moving, the scene is moving too, and the field still has to converge into a stable map the robot can trust. Robustness to dynamic objects is what separates a real-world robot map from a tidy vision-lab reconstruction.

ROBOTICS'25HS-SLAM: Hybrid Representation with Structural Supervision for Improved Dense SLAM
Problem

It is worst exactly where robots live: when the camera swings hard, or when the robot comes back to a place it saw earlier and the two versions of the map no longer line up.
Approach
HS-SLAM stops relying on a single kind of field and instead uses a hybrid encoding that stacks three complementary representations — a hash grid, a tri-plane, and a one-blob encoding — so the strengths of each cover the others' weak spots. It adds structural supervision by sampling rays in patches of nearby, non-local pixels rather than one isolated ray at a time, which teaches the field about local structure and keeps surfaces coherent instead of speckled. To fight the drift that accumulates as the robot travels, it runs active global bundle adjustment, re-optimising camera poses and map together so the whole trajectory stays globally consistent. That is what makes revisiting an old region snap back into alignment instead of ghosting. Together these give reconstructions that are more complete, smoother, and better tracked.
Why it matters
Building a map online means every small pose error compounds into drift, so the field is only useful if it can be pulled back into global agreement as the robot roams and loops back. ROBOTICS'25Implicit Articulated Robot Morphology Modeling with Configuration Space Neural Signed Distance Functions
Problem

Approach

6 · Is this spot free, and a place to train in

Once a robot has a scene, planning a safe path comes down to asking the same few questions over and over: is this spot free, how far is the nearest obstacle, and which way does that distance grow. A signed-distance field answers all three in a single query — its value is the distance to the closest surface, its sign tells you inside from outside, and its gradient points the way out. Because that field is smooth and differentiable, you can pour those queries straight into a trajectory optimizer as collision constraints, so the solver feels the obstacles rather than guessing at them with crude boxes and spheres. Separately, robot learning is hungry for photorealistic training scenes, and ordinary simulators look fake enough that a policy trained inside them falls apart on the real robot. So a NeRF captures a real place once and then renders it from any new viewpoint — a photorealistic, controllable digital twin you can fly a virtual drone through or mint fresh training views from. The point is not pretty pictures but transfer: the rendered world is bent toward sim-to-real so what the policy learns holds up on hardware. Both halves are the robotics answer to what neural fields are really for — not a picture, but a substrate to plan in and learn from.
ROBOTICS'25RMMI: Reactive Mobile Manipulation using an Implicit Neural Map
Problem
A mobile manipulator — an arm on a moving base — reaching into a cluttered space has to dodge obstacles continuously as it moves, not once at planning time. Doing that well needs a representation of the surroundings that is smooth and gives useful gradients, but most controllers fall back on crude stand-in shapes like boxes and spheres that miss the real geometry.
Approach
RMMI builds the map as a neural signed-distance field: an implicit function that, for any point in space, returns how far it is to the nearest surface and — because it is differentiable — which direction that distance grows. The robot samples many points across its own body and base and queries this field at each, turning the answers into distance-and-gradient information about how close it is to hitting something. Those queries become inequality constraints inside a quadratic program. That single solver then jointly optimizes the base motion, the arm motion, and manipulability at the same time, rather than treating them as separate problems. The result is one unified formulation where free-space awareness flows directly into the control law.
Why it matters
This is the differentiable free-space query put to work: because the SDF hands the optimizer both distance and direction in one shot, the controller can push away from obstacles smoothly, lifting the success rate on cluttered reaching tasks by 25% over methods that lean on geometric primitives.

ROBOTICS'25EASEIR: Efficient and Adaptive Safe-set Estimation via Implicit Representation for High-dimensional Motion Planning
Problem
For a high-degree-of-freedom arm, planners want to know the whole set of joint configurations that are collision-free in a given environment — a safe set. Computing that set analytically is accurate but painfully slow, too slow for practical online use or for quickly recomputing when the scene changes.
Approach
EASEIR learns an implicit neural representation that maps discretized workspace coordinates directly to collision sets in the robot's configuration space. A Latent Key Generator and Latent Key Decoder turn a point in the real Cartesian workspace into the corresponding chunk of collision-free joint configurations. A Full Set Compositor then stitches those pieces together with set operations to assemble the complete safe set. Because the heavy geometry is baked into the network, the safe set can be computed offline and reused, and adapted rapidly when the environment is reconfigured. The whole pipeline trades slow exact reasoning for fast learned lookups.
Why it matters
It is the same free-space idea moved up a level — instead of one point at a time, the network answers which whole regions are safe to be in, generating 6-DOF collision-free sets 43x faster than analytical methods while keeping the collision rate around 0.2%.

ROBOTICS'25FalconGym: A Photorealistic Simulation Framework for Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Vision-Based Quadrotor Navigation
Problem

Approach

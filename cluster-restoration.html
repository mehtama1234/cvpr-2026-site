The principles underneath this cluster

Restoration is not simply making images prettier. It is an inverse problem: a clean scene passed through blur, noise, darkness, weather, compression, or missing pixels, and the method tries to infer the clean cause. The difficulty is that many clean images could explain the same corrupted input.

Corruption destroys information unevenly

Everyday evidence. Blur removes sharp edges, noise hides weak signals, darkness reduces photon counts, and compression discards high-frequency detail.

Fundamental math principle. The principle is measurement loss. The observed image is a degraded measurement of a hidden clean image. Some information is weakened, some is mixed, and some may be gone.

How it shows up in the papers. Deblurring, denoising, low-light, deraining, dehazing, super-resolution, and compression-restoration papers all model what the corruption changed.

A prior fills what the measurement cannot decide

Everyday evidence. When an image is very noisy or missing pixels, the corrupted input alone cannot determine the exact clean image.

Fundamental math principle. The principle is prior-guided inverse inference. A learned image prior supplies likely structure while the measurement keeps the result tied to the observed input.

How it shows up in the papers. Diffusion restoration, generative priors, masked reconstruction, burst recovery, and low-light enhancement papers all balance measured evidence with learned natural-image structure.

Frequency explains blur and sharpness

Everyday evidence. Edges and fine textures live in faster changes; smooth lighting lives in slower changes. Many corruptions damage these parts differently.

Fundamental math principle. The principle is frequency separation. Fourier-style thinking separates slow and fast components so the method can restore edges, suppress noise, or avoid amplifying artifacts.

How it shows up in the papers. Deblurring, super-resolution, denoising, JPEG cleanup, and texture restoration papers often differ in how they recover or protect high-frequency information.

Restoration must not invent unchecked detail

Everyday evidence. A restored image can look sharp but add a texture, face detail, lesion boundary, or license-plate stroke that was not supported by evidence.

Fundamental math principle. The principle is uncertainty-aware reconstruction. The method should distinguish likely detail from proven detail, especially in medical, forensic, or scientific images.

How it shows up in the papers. Faithful restoration, uncertainty maps, reference-guided restoration, medical enhancement, and evaluation papers all ask whether the clean-looking output is actually justified.

The problem — from first principles

Every camera, every sensor, corrupts the world it captures. Dust on a lens, darkness, motion during exposure, rain between observer and scene — these are not bugs; they are fundamental physics. A pixel isn't a clean sample of light; it's a measurement through a degraded channel. Computer vision wants to turn pixels into meaning: to understand what's in a scene. But meaning is hidden behind noise, blur, haze, shadow, underexposure. Restoration asks a hard question: given only what we can measure — the corrupted image — can we infer what was actually there?

The challenge is that restoration is ill-posed. A noisy image could come from infinite different sharp originals; a blurred frame from countless motion trajectories. Without additional structure — priors about what images look like, what scenes are plausible — there's no unique answer. For decades, restoration relied on hand-crafted priors (sparsity, smoothness, low-rank structure). These work when their assumptions match reality, and fail when they don't.

Recent work has shifted the foundation: instead of hand-crafted priors, use learned generative priors — diffusion models trained on vast natural images, or flow-matching models, or other generative machinery. These priors embed implicit knowledge of what real images look like. The question becomes: how do we guide a generative model to produce a clean image that matches the degraded observation?

The families — how the field tackles it

Diffusion guidance for inverse problems

The simplest framing: run diffusion backward (from noise to clean image), but nudge it toward images that, when corrupted, match what we see. Papers like PnP-CM (plug-and-play consistency models), Outlier-Robust Diffusion Solvers, Dual Ascent Diffusion, and CARD (Correlation Aware Restoration) formalize this. They solve inverse problems — blur, noise, rain — by treating diffusion's iterative denoising as a solver for the constraint that the degraded version of the restored image must match the observation. GSNR explicitly learns a null-space prior (solutions that corrupt to the same degraded image). Residual Diffusion Bridge unifies diffusion bridges through residual modulation, enabling spatially adaptive restoration. These methods don't require retraining for each degradation type; they leverage pre-trained diffusion as a generic prior.

Degradation-aware conditioning

Instead of solving a constraint, predict the degradation first, then use that prediction to guide restoration. HiDRA explicitly estimates degradation representations and adapts a diffusion model's fine-tuning. Self-supervised Dynamic Heterogeneous Degradation Modeling unifies diverse degradations (blur, noise, rain, haze) into a minimal, physically coherent parameterization. UniLDiff and FAPE-IR combine degradation-aware and detail-aware mechanisms to adapt implicitly to compound degradations without predefined labels. Beyond Ground-Truth integrates image-quality-aware priors at training stages. The insight: degradation is structured (it's not random noise; it's blur, or rain, or underexposure). Explicitly modeling degradation type helps the model restore more accurately.

Event camera and multi-modal fusion

Event cameras see the world differently: they measure brightness changes, pixel by pixel, at microsecond timescale. They're sensitive in darkness. They don't see static content — only change. For restoration, this is powerful. Event-based Motion Deblurring and Event-based Motion Deblurring with Unpaired Data use events to guide deblurring without paired sharp/blurry training data. Event-Illumination Collaborative Low-light Image Enhancement, Learning to See through Illumination Extremes with Event Streaming, and From Events to Clarity (event-guided dehazing) all use events as a high-frequency, high-temporal-resolution signal to correct RGB images. NEC-Diff (Noise-Robust Event–RAW Complementary) explicitly exploits the complementary noise properties of events and raw sensors for extreme low-light. High-Quality and Efficient Turbulence Mitigation with Events discovers polarity alternation and event tube properties to reduce data overhead by 77–90% while restoring dynamic scenes through turbulence.

Where it stands

What works now: diffusion-based restoration with degradation-aware guidance outperforms hand-crafted priors on most benchmarks. Event cameras unlock extreme-condition sensing (turbulence, low-light, darkness) that RGB alone cannot handle. Physics-grounded models (turbulence, thermal, fluid dynamics) stabilize restoration when domain knowledge is available. Multi-modal fusion (RGB, event, RAW, thermal) and one-step distillation enable near real-time deployment on mobile. All-in-one models can handle diverse compound degradations, though still with quality trade-offs versus specialized models.

What's still open: first, generalization — models trained on synthetic degradations often fail on real-world complexity; test-time adaptation and domain-shift correction are active. Second, speed — even distilled diffusion is slower than classical methods; flow matching and normalizing flows are emerging alternatives but not yet dominant. Third, extreme conditions (photon-limited imaging, severe multi-modal misalignment, unknown degradations) still require research into new sensing modalities or physics integration. Finally, the frontier is joint restoration and perception: not just recovering pixels, but restoring images in ways that improve downstream tasks (detection, segmentation, pose estimation), which may require different trade-offs than perceptual quality alone.

Why it matters: This matters because a model cannot reliably undo damage it has not named. Degradation-aware restoration turns cleanup from a generic beautification step into a targeted inverse measurement problem.

Light and darkness

The everyday problem: Low-light images are not merely darker versions of daylight images. Photon noise, motion blur, exposure time, active illumination, RAW sensor behavior, and missing structure all change what evidence survives.

The hidden mathematical idea: The mathematical idea is signal recovery under scarce photons. Separate illumination from reflectance, use RAW or event evidence when possible, and model how exposure, noise, blur, and geometry interact in the dark.

Why it matters: This matters because nighttime vision is a measurement limit, not a style problem. Good low-light restoration recovers usable structure without inventing details that the sensor never supported.

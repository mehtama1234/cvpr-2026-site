Natural gradient & Fisher information — why it works, mathematically

A network is steered by a billion tiny dials, but turning each dial moves the output by a wildly different amount. Plain training steps ignore this imbalance and waste effort, often getting stuck or zigzagging. This is the fix: measure how far you actually went by watching the output change, not the dial positions.

The key turn — the principle at work

The stuck point. A step that is small in parameter numbers can be large in model behavior, and a large parameter step can barely change behavior.

The mathematical move. The principle is geometry of distributions. The Fisher information measures how much the output distribution changes when parameters move, and the natural gradient rescales steps by that geometry.

What changes. Optimization stops measuring distance only in settings. It measures distance in what the model predicts, making updates more consistent across curved parameter spaces.

The problem, as a quantity

A network is controlled by a billion small adjustable numbers—let's call them dials. To make the network learn, you nudge each dial in the direction that would shrink the error a little. But here is the trap: when you turn one dial by a tiny amount, the network's answer might shift noticeably. Turn a different dial by the same tiny amount, and the answer barely moves. In other words, some dials have enormous leverage over the output, and some have almost none.

A normal training step treats all dials the same: it applies the same step size everywhere. This is like trying to steer a car by turning the wheel by a fixed angle, without noticing that sometimes that angle barely changes direction and sometimes it spins you around. You end up wasting motion on the low-leverage dials and sometimes overshooting on the high-leverage ones, leading to slow learning or bouncy, unreliable training.

The core ideas — and why they work

The plain slope treats every direction as equal — but they aren't

plain step ignores how much the output actually bends per direction
When you nudge a dial, the output moves by an amount that depends on that dial's sensitivity at the current setting. Some dials steer the output a lot per nudge; others steer it almost not at all. The plain slope (the blame for what went wrong) points downhill at the same rate everywhere, but the output actually bends and curves through the space of dial settings—some directions are steep, others are shallow. A step of size η along the slope works well only if the output bends the same way everywhere. In reality, you need a larger step in a shallow direction and a smaller step in a steep direction to make the same useful progress toward lower error.

Measure distance by how much the output changes, not the settings

distance ≈ change in the output spread (the Fisher metric)
What matters for learning is not how much you changed the dials, but how much the output changed as a result. Two different dial movements could change the output by the same amount, but one might be a tiny dial nudge and the other a huge one—the first is much more efficient. The Fisher information table measures this: it captures how sensitive the output spread is to each dial and each pair of dials. The distance between two dial settings should be measured by how different the output would be, not by how different the dial numbers are. This flips the sense of 'how far you went'—you care about the space of what the network says, not the space of what the dials are.

Re-scale the step by that geometry for a straighter path

θ ← θ − η · F⁻¹ · ∇L (the natural gradient)
The natural gradient step re-scales your direction of motion to account for the output's curvature. Instead of stepping along the plain slope by η, you step by η times the inverse of the Fisher table times the slope. This inverse table acts like a stretcher: it shrinks your step in steep directions and stretches it in shallow directions, so that every step moves you the same useful distance in the output space. The result is that you take fewer big strides to reach lower error, and the path stays straighter because you are no longer fighting the geometry of the problem.

The full geometry is huge, so it is cheaply approximated

F is enormous → use diagonal or block approximations
The Fisher table has billions of rows and columns—one for each dial and each pair of dials—so storing it and computing its inverse is impossible on a real computer. Instead, people use cheap approximations. The simplest is to use only the diagonal: ignore how dials interact with each other and re-scale each dial independently. This throws away some geometry information but is so fast that you can train. A better middle ground is to use blocks: group nearby dials and keep the geometry within each block while ignoring interactions between blocks. Some methods even skip computing the Fisher explicitly and use tricks like computing the Hessian (the curvature of the loss itself, not the output spread) or using moment estimates from past steps. All of these are different knobs on the same machine: the true goal is always to step in a direction that accounts for where the output is actually sensitive.

The approaches, as variations on the math

The natural gradient is not a single algorithm but a principle: rescale your step by the inverse of how the output bends per dial direction. Different implementations turn three dials: Which table captures the geometry? (Fisher, Hessian, the spread of past slopes), How much of that table do you keep? (full, diagonal, block-diagonal, rank-reduced), and How do you compute or approximate its inverse? (explicit inversion, implicit via conjugate-gradient, online updates, or moment estimation). The thread never changes: always re-scale your step by how much the output would actually change per dial nudge, rather than stepping blindly in the slope direction.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

1Fisher Matrix Approximation
F ≈ diag(∇log π ⊙ ∇log π) or F̂ = (1/N) ∑ᵢ gᵢgᵢᵀ
The full Fisher table is huge and slow to invert. Instead, we approximate it using only the diagonal (ignoring interactions between parameters) or by averaging outer products of slope-vectors from sampled actions. This keeps computation cheap while still capturing the main geometry of the problem.
2Natural Gradient via Trust Region
max_θ E[r] s.t. D_KL(π_old || π_new) ≤ δ
Instead of rescaling by the inverse Fisher (expensive), we directly limit how much the output probabilities can shift per step using a KL divergence constraint. This keeps updates steady and prevents wild parameter swings that would break off-policy data. The natural geometry is built into the constraint itself rather than explicit table math.
3Curvature-Aware Step Scaling
Δθ_nat = F⁻¹ * ∇J(θ) vs Δθ_plain = α * ∇J(θ)
When a parameter change produces large shifts in output (high curvature), we shrink the step; when it produces small shifts (flat direction), we can step further. This is like following a contour map where steep slopes get shorter steps. The rescaling by F inverse trades computation cost for far fewer total steps needed to reach a good policy.
4Reparameterization & Empirical Fisher
F̂_emp = E[(∇log π_θ(a|s))²] via on-policy samples only
We only compute Fisher information from actions the current policy actually takes, not all possible actions. This empirical version is cheaper, works with gradient samples we already have, and avoids expensive policy-independent computations. The trade-off is that we ignore geometry in regions the policy never explores.

What breaks without this

What goes wrong. Without natural gradient, two steps in the settings that look equal can change the model by very different amounts.

Why it goes wrong. The settings are not the real object. The real object is what the model predicts. In some places, a tiny settings move changes the prediction a lot; in other places, a large move barely matters.

What this idea gives you. Natural gradient measures distance by change in the model's behavior. It makes steps in the space of predictions, not just in the space of settings.

The through-line. The core problem is that the slope points downhill, but the ground is bumpy—some directions are steep and some are shallow, so the same step size does not make the same progress everywhere. A normal training step walks downhill at a fixed pace, which means slow progress in shallow places and wasted motion in steep places. The natural gradient fixes this by measuring distance in the output space: instead of asking 'how much did I move the dials,' it asks 'how much did the answer change.' Then it rescales the step so that every stride moves you the same distance in the space of what the network says, not the space of what the dials are. Two plain ideas carry it: measuring distance by output-spread change (the Fisher metric) rather than dial change, and rescaling the step direction by the inverse of that metric to flatten out the bumpy terrain and find a straighter path to lower error.

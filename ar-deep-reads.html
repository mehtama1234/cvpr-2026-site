1 · Coarse thumbnail, then finer

The classic way a GPT writes an image is to predict thousands of little image tokens one at a time, marching left-to-right and top-to-bottom like reading text. This is painfully slow, and worse, the model commits to a corner of the picture before it has any sense of the whole — there is no global blueprint. The VAR recipe throws out next-token prediction and replaces it with next-scale prediction: first paint the entire image as a tiny blurry thumbnail, then predict the next higher-resolution version as a correction layered on top of every coarser version so far, working coarse to fine. Now each step fills in a whole scale of the picture rather than a single pixel, so the model locks in overall structure first and adds detail later, and the number of steps shrinks from thousands to a handful. This turned autoregressive image generation into the dominant efficient recipe. With the core idea settled, a whole cluster of work now just improves the schedule itself — which coarser scales each step should look at, how the blurry-to-sharp ladder is built, and how to carry the trick into 3D shapes and motion.
CVPR'26Markovian Scale Prediction: A New Era of Visual Autoregressive Generation
Problem
The VAR recipe generates an image coarse to fine, but at every new scale it re-reads all the coarser scales it has produced so far. That full-context habit is expensive, and it is not obvious that a step really needs to consult every ancestor — the most useful information tends to sit in the version just below it.
Approach
This paper makes the process Markovian: instead of conditioning each new scale on the entire history of coarser scales, it conditions mainly on the immediately coarser scale — the one step below. To make sure nothing important gets dropped when you stop looking all the way back, it adds a history compensation mechanism that folds a compact summary of the older scales back into the prediction. The result, Markov-VAR, keeps the coarse-to-fine ladder but slims the dependency between rungs from all-previous to essentially one-previous-plus-summary. The team releases a full series of Markov-VAR models trained on ImageNet.
Why it matters
Shortening the reach of each step cuts the cost of the recipe while keeping the global-first structure intact — the model still paints the whole picture coarsely before refining, but it no longer pays to re-read its entire past at every scale.
CVPR'26FVAR: Next-Focus Prediction for Visual Autoregressive Modeling
Problem
The coarse-to-fine ladder in VAR is normally built by plain downsampling — shrinking the image to make each smaller scale. But naive shrinking introduces aliasing: jagged edges and moire patterns that get baked into the pyramid and then destabilize training and blur fine detail like readable text.
Approach
FVAR reframes the ladder as next-focus prediction instead of next-scale, borrowing from how a camera racks focus from blur to sharp. Rather than downsampling, it builds a progressive refocusing pyramid using physics-based defocus blur kernels whose radius shrinks scale by scale, so each rung is a cleanly out-of-focus version of the next rather than an alias-ridden shrunk one. A High-Frequency Residual Teacher network is used during training to teach the model both the clean structure and the alias residuals, so the sharp details are learned rather than corrupted. Because this only changes how the pyramid is made, it drops into existing VAR frameworks with no extra cost at generation time.
Why it matters
Swapping downsampling for a defocus-to-focus pyramid gives an alias-free coarse-to-fine ladder, so the same one-step-per-scale generation now preserves fine detail instead of destabilizing on artifacts it created itself.
CVPR'26PointNSP: Autoregressive 3D Point Cloud Generation with Next-Scale Level-of-Detail Prediction
Problem
A 3D point cloud is an unordered set of points, so forcing a GPT to emit it point-by-point means inventing an artificial ordering. That ordering pushes the model toward short-range, local continuity and makes it bad at global properties like symmetry and overall shape consistency — which is why autoregressive point clouds had been losing to diffusion.
Approach
PointNSP carries the coarse-to-fine idea into 3D as next-scale level-of-detail (LoD) prediction. Instead of predicting the next point, each step predicts the entire shape at a higher level of detail, so every step outputs a complete 3D object — a rough blob first, then progressively denser and finer versions. Because a step commits to a whole shape rather than a sequence of individual points, there is no need to flatten the cloud into a 1D order, which keeps the generation permutation-invariant and preserves global structure. It scales up to dense clouds of 8,192 points.
Why it matters
Predicting a full shape per level of detail rather than one point at a time lets an autoregressive model finally lock in global geometry early — matching diffusion quality while keeping the parameter and sampling-speed advantages of the AR recipe.
CVPR'26Next-Scale Autoregressive Models for Text-to-Motion Generation
Problem
Generating human motion from a text prompt means producing a long, coherent sequence of poses over time. Doing it token-by-token, one pose-token after another, is slow, and long sequences drift because the model never fixes the overall arc of the motion up front.
Approach
This work ports the VAR next-scale recipe from images to motion over time. A hierarchical motion tokenizer encodes a movement at multiple time scales, from a coarse low-resolution motion skeleton down to fine detail. Generation then goes coarse to fine: it first lays down the rough shape of the whole motion, then refines it at finer time scales, with the text prompt steering the prediction at every scale. Because each step fills in a whole scale of the sequence rather than the next single token, it can produce long, coherent motions efficiently.
Why it matters
Sketching the entire motion coarsely before refining gives the sequence a global blueprint from the start, so long clips stay coherent and are generated in a handful of scale-steps instead of token-by-token.

2 · Escaping the one-token-at-a-time bottleneck

The core weakness of an autoregressive image generator is strict sequentiality: if a picture is a sequence of N tokens, the model needs N separate forward passes, one token at a time, and there is no way to hurry that up without changing the recipe. So one line of work breaks the strict ordering by predicting many tokens in a single pass, using masked or parallel decoding to get diffusion-like speed while still keeping a token-by-token generator underneath. A separate problem lives on the quality side: a standard AR model first quantizes the image into a fixed menu of discrete VQ codebook tokens, so that generating a picture becomes a classification problem of picking the next token from that menu. But squashing every patch into the nearest entry on a small menu throws away fine detail, and that lossy rounding puts a hard ceiling on how good the output can ever look. So a second line keeps the AR backbone but predicts continuous tokens instead, attaching a small diffusion or flow head that draws each token from a smooth space, or using normalizing flows to model the raw pixels directly with exact likelihoods. Both lines chase the same prize from an AR starting point: the fidelity and the speed people expect from diffusion.

CVPR'26Parallel Jacobi Decoding for Fast Autoregressive Image Generation
Problem

Autoregressive image models are painfully slow because they insist on predicting one token at a time, so a single picture can cost thousands of forward passes. An earlier trick called Jacobi decoding speeds up the first few iterations by guessing several tokens at once and refining them, but it treats the image as one long line of tokens, so it quickly stalls as guessing errors pile up and spread.

Approach

The paper's idea is that an image is not a line but a grid, and nearby pixels are strongly correlated, so guessing can happen in two dimensions instead of one. Its method, Parallel Jacobi Decoding, is training-free: once the current row has enough context filled in, it seeds draft tokens for the next row and lets several rows be refined in parallel each iteration, exploiting the strong local spatial structure the model's own attention already shows. It also adjusts the attention masks so that early wrong guesses do not snowball, which keeps the parallel refinement stable and converging. The result is that acceleration actually grows with image resolution, because a bigger grid offers more neighboring tokens to refine together. On Lumina-mGPT and LlamaGen it cuts the required autoregressive steps from 2357 down to a few hundred, giving roughly a 4.8x to 6.4x speedup with no drop in quality.

Why it matters

This attacks the sequential bottleneck head-on: instead of waiting for token N before starting token N+1, it uses the picture's spatial layout to refine whole regions at once.

CVPR'26Masked Auto-Regressive Variational Acceleration: Fast Inference Makes Practical Reinforcement Learning
Problem

Masked auto-regressive (MAR) models generate excellent images but need many iterative denoising steps, so inference is slow and hard to deploy. That slowness also makes it impractical to fine-tune them with reinforcement learning toward human preferences, because every RL step has to wait on the sluggish generator.

Approach

MARVAL is, to the paper's knowledge, the first distillation framework built specifically for masked auto-regressive models, compressing a many-step generator down to a handful of steps. The trouble is that off-the-shelf diffusion distillation does not transfer, because MAR works with a masked-prediction objective rather than continuous score matching, so the authors derive a Score Implicit Matching objective that treats the slow multi-step MAR sampler as an implicit score to match with a fast student. They extend it to a Guided version that folds classifier-free guidance directly into the distillation loss, preserving conditional quality even at very low step counts. Once the student is fast, it doubles as an RL policy: rewards from PickScore and CLIP are backpropagated straight through its differentiable sampling chain to align the model to human taste. The distilled MAR-H generates in 8 to 16 steps instead of 64 to 256, reaching FID 2.00 on ImageNet-256 with a 20x to 32x speedup, and the RL stage lifts preference scores without hurting FID.

Why it matters

By collapsing the long chain of masked-prediction steps into a few, it removes the sequential bottleneck that made both fast sampling and RL alignment impractical for MAR models.

CVPR'26FARMER: Flow AutoRegressive Transformer over Pixels
Problem

Modeling images autoregressively straight from raw pixels means facing extremely long sequences in a very high-dimensional space, which makes direct pixel-level likelihood modeling nearly intractable. Most systems dodge this by first quantizing into discrete tokens, but that rounding discards detail and caps quality.

Approach

FARMER keeps the AR backbone but unifies it with normalizing flows so it can predict continuous values and model the raw pixels directly with exact likelihoods, sidestepping the lossy VQ codebook entirely. An invertible autoregressive flow first transforms an image into a latent sequence whose distribution is modeled implicitly, giving a tractable, reversible bridge between pixels and latents. To tame the dimensionality, a self-supervised step splits the flow's latent channels into informative and redundant groups, so the AR transformer only has to do the heavy modeling on the channels that carry real signal. A one-step distillation scheme then accelerates the otherwise slow inference, and a resampling-based classifier-free guidance sharpens sample quality. The payoff is competitive pixel-level generation that, unlike quantized approaches, still hands back exact likelihoods and trains end-to-end from raw pixels.

Why it matters

This targets the quantization ceiling: by predicting continuous tokens through a normalizing flow instead of picking from a discrete codebook, no fine detail is thrown away before generation even begins.

CVPR'26STARFlow-V: End-to-End Video Generative Modeling with Autoregressive Normalizing Flows
Problem

Normalizing flows can compute exact likelihoods and invert cleanly, but they have trailed diffusion on video because the bijective architectures they need become prohibitively expensive on high-dimensional video data. On top of that, generating video autoregressively frame by frame is slow, since each frame waits on the ones before it.

Approach

STARFlow-V is presented as the first normalizing-flow text-to-video model at competitive quality, and it works by splitting the flow into a global and a local piece. The global component is an autoregressive flow that models how frames depend on each other across time, while the local component handles the structure inside each individual frame. A 3D causal VAE first squeezes video clips into a compact latent space so the flow is not fighting raw pixel dimensionality, and the whole system is a 7-billion-parameter model trained on 70M text-video and 400M image pairs. Its key training trick, flow-score matching, ties the flow's learned density to a causal denoiser on noisy latents, giving a stable signal without ever evaluating an explicit likelihood during training. At inference, block-wise Jacobi iteration decodes the flow in parallel across spatial blocks for a 15x speedup over sequential decoding, and the model reaches VBench scores of 78.67, or 79.70 with a prompt rewriter.

Why it matters

It fights both costs at once: normalizing flows dodge the quantization ceiling by modeling continuous latents with exact-likelihood machinery, and block-wise Jacobi decoding breaks the frame-by-frame sequential bottleneck for a large parallel speedup.

3 · Where drift is deadliest

Video and 3D are where autoregression feels most natural: for video you simply predict the next frame, and for a shape you grow the mesh one face at a time, so the sequential story of prediction lines up with how the content is actually built. But these are also the settings where error accumulation hurts the most, because a tiny mistake early on gets fed back in as if it were true, and over hundreds of frames or thousands of mesh tokens that small drift compounds into visible failure. For video the hard part is streaming very long or even endless sequences without the picture slowly decaying or, worse, collapsing back onto a few reference frames. For 3D and mesh the hard part is sheer sequence length and keeping the geometry coherent, so that faces grown token by token still meet cleanly and stay symmetric. Underneath both is the same enemy: a sequential rollout tends to drift further the longer it runs, wandering away from where it started. The papers here each attack that drift head-on, whether by generating coarse first and refining, diagnosing why the collapse happens, or shrinking the sequence so there is simply less room for error to pile up.
CVPR'26TempoMaster: Efficient Long Video Generation via Next-Frame-Rate Prediction
Problem
Making a long video is a tug of war between two things that both get harder the longer you go: keeping the story visually consistent from start to finish, and paying the enormous compute bill of generating so many frames. Ordinary approaches that just keep predicting the next frame tend to lose coherence over an extended clip, as small errors early on quietly steer the later frames off course.
Approach
TempoMaster reframes long video generation as predicting the next frame rate rather than the next frame. It first generates a low-frame-rate coarse blueprint, say around 6fps, that locks in the global motion, semantics, and overall arc of the clip. Then it iteratively refines by filling in intermediate frames at higher and higher rates, 12fps then 24fps, so detail and smoothness are added on top of a structure that is already committed. Within each frame-rate level it uses bidirectional attention so frames can look both forward and backward, while it stays autoregressive across frame rates to carry coherence between refinement stages. Because the refinement at each level can be produced in parallel, the whole process stays efficient instead of crawling frame by frame.
Why it matters
By deciding the big picture first at low frame rate, TempoMaster stops early mistakes from compounding across hundreds of frames, since the coarse blueprint anchors everything that follows instead of letting drift build up one frame at a time.
CVPR'26LoL: Longer than Longer, Scaling Video Generation to Hour
Problem
Autoregressive video models that generate hour-long clips suffer badly from error accumulation and slowly lose long-term coherence. To stabilize themselves they lean on attention sink frames, but this backfires into a failure the authors call sink-collapse: the generated content keeps snapping back to those sink frames, producing abrupt scene resets and cyclic, repeating patterns instead of a fresh continuing video.
Approach
LoL is the first framework to push streaming video generation to indefinite length, and it does so by first diagnosing why sink-collapse happens. It traces the problem to a conflict between the periodic structure of Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) and the multi-head attention mechanism, which drives different heads to concentrate their attention on the same sink frames. Its fix is training-free: a multi-head RoPE jitter that shifts the base frequencies of different attention heads, breaking the homogenization across heads so they stop all piling onto the sink. Around this it adds streaming RoPE generation, adaptive noise sampling, and a 3D causal VAE decoder to keep the stream flowing continuously. The result is real-time infinite streaming, demonstrated as a full 12-hour continuous video with little quality decay from only a 1.3B model plus a KV cache.
Why it matters
Sink-collapse is drift in its most extreme form: over an hours-long rollout the model wanders so far that it can only recover by resetting to a reference frame, so fixing the head-level cause is what lets quality survive indefinitely instead of decaying or looping.
CVPR'26MeshMosaic: Scaling Artist Mesh Generation via Local-to-Global Assembly
Problem
Autoregressive mesh generators choke when asked to produce high-detail, artist-quality shapes, because the token sequence for a dense mesh becomes so long that the model hits a bottleneck and quantization runs out of resolution. In practice they top out around 8K triangles, while real game and film assets need 100K or more faces, so fine geometric detail simply cannot be reproduced faithfully.
Approach
MeshMosaic switches from generating one giant sequence to a local-to-global assembly. It first segments the shape into semantically meaningful patches, then generates each patch autoregressively as its own manageable sequence, like laying down tiles of a mosaic. To keep the tiles from clashing, neighboring patches share boundary conditions, which enforces coherence, symmetry, and seamless connectivity where patches meet. It also quantizes each patch locally, so detail and scalability improve together instead of trading off. With this design a compact 0.5B model stably generates meshes of 100K+ triangles, outperforming methods like BPT and DeepMesh.
Why it matters
Breaking one enormous mesh sequence into short shared-boundary patches directly limits how far drift can travel, since each patch is a fresh short rollout kept in line by its neighbors rather than a single chain that accumulates error across thousands of tokens.
CVPR'26FACE: A Face-based Autoregressive Representation for High-Fidelity and Efficient Mesh Generation
Problem
Autoregressive mesh models flatten a shape into a long sequence of raw vertex coordinates, which is punishing because Transformer self-attention costs grow with the square of the sequence length (O(N^2)). That quadratic cost makes generating high-fidelity geometry computationally prohibitive, and earlier fixes based on traversal tricks or block tokenization just traded the problem for algorithmic complexity or exploding vocabulary sizes.
Approach
FACE attacks the length itself by working at the level of whole faces instead of individual coordinates. Its central idea is one-face-one-token: a triangle is 3 vertices, or 9 numbers, and FACE packs each entire face into a single token, cutting the sequence length by a factor of nine. It uses an Autoregressive Autoencoder in which a shape encoder compresses the input point cloud into a compact latent, and an autoregressive face decoder then emits faces one at a time, using causal self-attention to capture local mesh structure and cross-attention to inject the global geometry. Faces are ordered in a fixed canonical way, sorted by their minimum-coordinate vertex, so the generation order is consistent. This yields a 0.11 compression ratio, roughly halving the previous best of 0.22, for an 81x theoretical and 9x practical cut in compute while reconstruction quality holds or improves on Objaverse, Toys4K, and Famous.
Why it matters
Shortening the sequence ninefold both tames autoregression's quadratic cost and shrinks the runway over which drift can accumulate, so there are simply far fewer steps in which a small error can compound into a topological mistake.

4 · Read and write in one model, and stop the drift

The most strategic thread in autoregressive vision is one decoder-only transformer that both reads images and writes them, the same way a language model reads and writes text with a single next-token engine. Autoregression is the natural substrate for this, because reading and writing become the same act of predicting the next token in one shared sequence, so a single model can understand a picture and then draw one. This is also where AR most clearly beats diffusion: a diffusion model denoises pixels but has no native way to understand what it is looking at, whereas an AR decoder answers questions and generates images with the same weights. But AR carries a built-in flaw called exposure bias — it is trained on perfect ground-truth history yet at generation time it must consume its own imperfect outputs, so an early mistake gets fed forward and small errors compound into global drift. A self-correction line fixes this by giving the strictly-causal generator what it lacks: a foresight or global plan injected at training time, or interleaved textual reasoning that inspects and repairs the picture as it is being drawn. Together these two moves make the sentence-writing image generator both smarter (it can read as well as write) and steadier (it stops drifting from its own early errors).
CVPR'26OneCAT: Decoder-Only Auto-Regressive Model for Unified Understanding and Generation
Problem
Most multimodal systems bolt together separate pipelines — one stack to understand images and a different stack to generate them — which blocks deep cross-modal fusion and slows everything down. Many still lean on heavy external parts like a vision encoder to look and a diffusion model to draw, so the model is never really one thing.
Approach
OneCAT is a single encoder-free decoder-only transformer: it throws away the external vision encoder and tokenizer, turning raw image patches straight into tokens with a lightweight embedding. A modality-specific mixture-of-experts routes each token to the right specialist — continuous vision tokens go to an understanding expert, discrete vision tokens go to a generation expert, and text goes to a text expert — all inside one shared decoder so fusion happens early. A scale-aware adapter lets it generate images coarse-to-fine, autoregressively from low resolution up to high, rather than in one shot. The whole thing is one pure autoregressive objective, warmed up by pretraining the experts and distilling from a stronger multimodal teacher. The result is that reading, writing, and editing images all run through the same next-token decoder.
Why it matters
This is read-and-write in one model taken to its logical end — no borrowed encoder, no borrowed diffusion, just one AR decoder that understands, generates, and edits — and it runs markedly faster than diffusion baselines, especially at high resolution.
CVPR'26TUNA: Taming Unified Visual Representations for Native Unified Multimodal Models
Problem
Unified models face a painful trade-off: keep separate representations for understanding and generation and you pay in format mismatches and extra parameters, or share one representation and it usually performs worse than the split version. The deeper cause is that different vision encoders emit incompatible spatial, temporal, and channel shapes, so their features fight each other when you try to merge them.
Approach
TUNA builds one unified continuous visual space by cascading a VAE encoder (for reconstructable detail) with a representation encoder like SigLIP (for semantic meaning), so a single feature serves both looking and drawing. These unified visual features are fused with text tokens and fed to one LLM decoder. That decoder does two jobs at once in the same continuous space: ordinary autoregressive text generation for understanding, and flow-matching visual generation for producing images and video. A three-stage training schedule jointly optimizes understanding and generation so neither task starves the other. Crucially, the unified representation ends up beating the decoupled alternative across every task, reversing the usual penalty for sharing.
Why it matters
It shows one decoder can genuinely read and write images and video from a single shared continuous representation, hitting 61.2 on MMStar understanding and 0.90 on GenEval generation — proof that unifying does not have to mean compromising.
CVPR'26Thinking-while-Generating: Interleaving Textual Reasoning throughout Visual Generation
Problem
Reasoning-augmented image generators today either plan in text before they draw or critique after they finish, but neither talks to the picture while it is being made. Pre-planning locks the plan in before a single pixel exists so it cannot course-correct, and post-refinement costs an extra pass without giving timely, fine-grained guidance at each step.
Approach
Thinking-while-Generating (TWIG) runs on a unified understanding-generation model (Janus-Pro-7B) and weaves textual thoughts into the autoregressive image trajectory itself. It works in three moves: when to think schedules a few checkpoints across the image (empirically three — upper background, central content, lower background); what to say generates a textual thought at each checkpoint conditioned on the prompt, all prior thoughts, and the image drawn so far, then draws the next region under that guidance; and how to refine has the model score the region it just made and, if it falls short, re-draw only that local region with a revised caption. All of this stays inside one autoregressive sequence — no image-to-image editing needed — by keeping generated pixels at the tail and prepending the new thought to the context. It is trained zero-shot, then with supervised data (TWIG-50K), then with reinforcement learning that rewards the whole think-generate-reflect loop.
Why it matters
This is self-correction applied step-by-step during generation: by reasoning and reflecting mid-draw, the model catches its own drift before it compounds, lifting shape binding by +15.41 with no training at all and pushing color, shape, and texture scores far past the base model.
CVPR'26Mirai: Autoregressive Visual Generation Needs Foresight
Problem
A strictly causal AR image model only ever sees the past — its supervision is purely next-token, local guidance — so it has no holistic sense of where the whole picture is going and comes out globally incoherent. Unlike words, image tokens genuinely depend on two-dimensional, long-range, bidirectional context, so pure left-to-right training starves the model of the global plan it needs.
Approach
Mirai gives the causal generator foresight at training time only, changing nothing about the architecture or the test-time causal inference. Mirai-E supplies explicit foresight from the exponential moving average of a unidirectional model, while Mirai-I supplies implicit foresight distilled from a frozen bidirectional encoder that has already seen the whole image. Both inject that future information deep inside the network and, critically, align it with the model's internal 2D image grid rather than a 1D scan order, which turns out to matter a lot. Because the foresight lives only in training, inference stays exactly as cheap and causal as before. In effect the model learns a global plan while keeping its streaming next-token behavior.
Why it matters
This directly attacks exposure bias and drift: by teaching a causal AR model to anticipate the global structure it can never see at inference, Mirai speeds LlamaGen-B convergence up to 10x and cuts FID from 5.34 to 4.34 on ImageNet — a steadier generator at zero extra test-time cost.

5 · Write the action like the next word

A vision-language-action model would like to drive a robot the way GPT writes an essay: feed it a camera image and a plain-language instruction, and have it emit motor commands one token at a time. To make that work you first turn continuous motion into a small vocabulary of discrete action tokens, and then let an ordinary transformer predict them autoregressively, each token conditioned on everything that came before. The payoff is that the robot inherits the entire large-language-model toolkit at once: in-context learning, clean scaling with data and size, and one unified model that ties vision, language, and control together. The most literal transplant of this idea is in-context imitation: you stuff a handful of demonstration trajectories into the prompt and let the model autoregressively predict the next action, with no gradient steps at test time. A supporting but decisive finding is that how you tokenize the actions matters enormously; keeping the token stream smooth is what separates steady real-robot execution from jittery failure. The catch is that generating tokens one by one is slow, which is fatal for a legged robot that needs commands many times a second, so a real-time design shrinks the action vocabulary until even a big model can still hit the required control rate. Together these papers show the next-word recipe pointed squarely at control: write the action like the next word, and borrow everything language models already learned to say.
ROBOTICS'25ICRT: In-Context Imitation Learning via Next-Token Prediction
Problem
A useful robot has to pick up brand-new tasks at test time without being retrained, working only from a few example demonstrations you hand it on the spot. The hard part is generalizing to tasks the policy has never seen, using that tiny bit of context as the only guidance.
Approach
ICRT (In-Context Robot Transformer) is a plain causal transformer that does exactly what a language model does, only over robot experience instead of words. It performs autoregressive next-token prediction over sensorimotor trajectories, the stream of camera images, proprioception (the robot sensing its own joints), and actions. At test time you simply prompt it with a few demonstration trajectories and it infers the task and rolls out the next actions, all without any additional training. This is the literal transplant of the next-word recipe onto control: condition on the prompt, predict the next token, repeat. Because the task is read from context rather than baked in by fine-tuning, one model can be repurposed on the fly.
Why it matters
This is in-context learning borrowed wholesale from the LLM playbook: the robot learns a new skill from examples in its prompt, with no gradient steps, exactly the way a language model follows a few-shot example. It shows the entire trick that made LLMs flexible can carry straight over to motor control.
ROBOTICS'25Action Tokenizer Matters in In-Context Imitation Learning
Problem
To predict actions like words, you first have to chop continuous motion into discrete tokens, and existing tokenizers do this carelessly. They ignore the fact that real motion is smooth over time, so the resulting token stream jumps around and makes the robot execute unstable, jerky trajectories.
Approach
The paper introduces LipVQ-VAE, an action tokenizer built to keep the token stream smooth. It enforces a Lipschitz-continuity condition, roughly a cap on how fast the output can change, inside the quantized latent space by applying weight normalization while training the VQ-VAE. That constraint propagates the smoothness of the raw motion all the way through to the discrete codebook entries that the in-context policy actually reads and predicts. The result is a vocabulary of action tokens where nearby motions map to nearby tokens, so autoregressive prediction produces continuous, executable behavior rather than jitter. It is a small change to the tokenizer with an outsized effect on stability.
Why it matters
It is the quiet but load-bearing lesson of the whole theme: the action tokenizer makes or breaks execution. A Lipschitz-smooth vocabulary lifts in-context success by over 5.3% and yields visibly smoother real-robot trajectories, proving that how you turn motion into tokens matters as much as the transformer that predicts them.
ROBOTICS'25Quart-Online: Latency-Free Multimodal Large Language Model for Quadruped Robot Learning
Problem
You can plug a full multimodal large language model into a quadruped as its vision-language-action brain, but generating tokens one at a time is slow. That inference delay means the model cannot keep up with the fast control loop a walking robot needs, so real-time deployment simply stalls.
Approach
Quart-Online fixes this with Action Chunk Discretization (ACD), which compresses continuous motion into a compact discrete vocabulary of action tokens, deliberately keeping the token set small so each prediction step is cheap. The multimodal LLM is then fine-tuned to jointly encode vision, language, and these discretized actions in one unified model. By shrinking the action vocabulary rather than shrinking the model, it preserves the language model quality while cutting the per-step cost enough to run in real time. The result is a big VLA that still emits control tokens fast enough for the loop, hitting 50 Hz inference for legged control.
Why it matters
This is the real-time answer to the catch in autoregressive control: token-by-token generation is slow, so shrink the vocabulary until a large model can still meet the robot control rate. It is the first latency-free VLA for quadrupeds, showing the next-word recipe can run fast enough for legs on the ground.
ROBOTICS'25Distributional Decision Transformer: Risk-Sensitive Offline RL via Quantile-Based Critics and Stochastic Return
Problem
Return-conditioned transformers learn to act from logged data by predicting actions the way a language model predicts words, but they cannot tell deliberate behavioral variety apart from random noise. Treating both the same way makes the policy collapse toward mediocre, averaged-out behavior.
Approach
The Distributional Decision Transformer (DDT) keeps the autoregressive action generation of a decision transformer but pairs it with a full picture of possible outcomes rather than a single expected return. A Gaussian stochastic-return mechanism proposes a diverse set of candidate actions, and an Implicit Quantile Network critic, embedded right in the decision loop, scores each candidate across the whole distribution of returns instead of just the mean. That lets the model reason about risk, favoring or avoiding the spread of outcomes on purpose rather than being fooled by noise. It unifies probabilistic return-distribution modeling with the next-token style of predicting actions in one transformer.
Why it matters
It shows the autoregressive action-generation recipe can absorb genuinely new machinery, here a distributional, risk-sensitive critic, and still keep the transformer emitting actions token by token. That marriage delivers state-of-the-art results on standard offline RL locomotion and sparse-reward benchmarks.

6 · Roll out the plan, but fast

Here the action being generated is a whole trajectory — a future path, a pose sequence, or a driving plan — produced one step at a time, with each step conditioned on the steps already generated, exactly like extending a sentence one word at a time. This is why the idea shows up so heavily in autonomous driving and multi-agent motion, where the natural output is a rollout of where everything goes next. But the one bottleneck a robot cannot ignore is that generating actions one token at a time is murderous for a manipulator that has to move now — every extra token is latency the controller has to pay for, and the arm cannot pause between words. So a distinctly robotics cluster attacks the sequential decode itself, rather than the model. Some reformulate autoregressive action generation so that many tokens can be produced in parallel instead of strictly one after another; others compress the action vocabulary so that far fewer tokens are needed for the same motion, recovering real-time control without retraining the underlying model. This parallel-decoding branch barely exists in ordinary text generation, because a chatbot can happily wait a fraction of a second, and a robot cannot.
ROBOTICS'25Generalizing Motion Planners with Mixture of Experts for Autonomous Driving
Problem
Learning-based motion planners for self-driving cars tend to work in the situations they were trained on but fall apart in new ones. Two training failures are blamed: the model collapses onto one dominant way of driving and ignores the others, and the reward signal is lopsided so rare-but-important cases get drowned out.
Approach
STR2 is a decoder-only planner built like a language model: a vision transformer turns the scene into tokens, and a causal transformer backbone then rolls out the ego vehicle's plan one step at a time, each step conditioned on what came before. The transformer backbone is a mixture of experts, where a router hands different situations to different specialist sub-networks, which is what fixes the collapse-onto-one-behavior and the lopsided-reward problems. Because it is just next-token prediction over driving, it can be scaled up like a foundation model — trained on billions of urban driving scenarios. The payoff is that accuracy keeps improving as you add more data and more model, rather than plateauing. It beats prior planners both on held-out test sets and in closed-loop simulation where the plan actually has to drive.
Why it matters
This is the purest example of the theme: the action being generated is a driving plan, emitted step by step by a causal transformer exactly the way a sentence is extended. It shows that treating a trajectory as a sequence to roll out lets a planner scale with data and generalize instead of memorizing.
ROBOTICS'25On Learning Closed-Loop Probabilistic Multi-Agent Simulator
Problem
To test a self-driving car you need a simulator full of other vehicles that react to each other believably. Existing tools struggle to produce genuinely varied, realistic traffic, and they give you little control over whether a given car drives cautiously or aggressively.
Approach
NIVA learns a model that predicts what every vehicle does next from what it currently observes, and it runs closed-loop: each predicted move feeds back in and shapes the next one, so the whole scene rolls forward autoregressively. Driving styles like cautious or aggressive are baked in as a mixture of probability distributions, so you can dial an agent's intent up or down. The elegant part is that it unifies sequence-to-sequence trajectory prediction and next-token prediction under one Bayesian framework — the same machinery that predicts a future path also generates a live, interacting simulation. That means the two things that used to be separate tools, forecasting where cars will go and simulating how they interact, become one model. The result is diverse, controllable, believable multi-agent traffic.
Why it matters
It shows the trajectory-as-sequence idea scaled up to many agents at once, each rolling out its own path while conditioned on everyone else's. And it makes explicit that predicting a trajectory and generating tokens are the same operation wearing two hats.
ROBOTICS'25PD-VLA: Accelerating Vision-Language-Action Model Integrated with Action Chunking via Parallel Decoding
Problem
Vision-language-action models predict robot movements in chunks of several steps at a time, but the bigger the chunk, the more the cost grows in lockstep, because the steps are decoded one after another. That makes them too slow to drive a robot arm in real time.
Approach
Instead of accepting that the action tokens have to come out one by one, PD-VLA recasts the entire chunk of autoregressive decoding as a single fixed-point system — a set of equations whose answer is the same sequence the slow method would have produced. It then solves that system with parallel fixed-point iterations, computing many tokens at once rather than strictly in order. Crucially this is training-free: the same model gets faster with no retraining, and it stacks on top of other speedup tricks. Because the parallel solution provably converges to the same answer, there are mathematical guarantees that accuracy is not sacrificed for speed. The result is a 2.52x jump in execution frequency.
Why it matters
This is the theme's other half made concrete: the latency of decoding actions one token at a time is exactly what a manipulator cannot wait through, so PD-VLA attacks the sequential decode itself and produces the tokens in parallel. It recovers real-time control without touching the model's weights.
ROBOTICS'25Decision Transformer-Based Drone Trajectory Planning with Dynamic Safety-Efficiency Trade-Offs
Problem
Drone path planners usually make you hand-tune a handful of knobs to balance flying safely against flying fast, and that tuning takes an expert. Worse, once those settings are fixed they do not adapt when the environment changes.
Approach
This planner is a Decision Transformer, a sequence model that generates the drone's control one step at a time, each step conditioned on the plan so far, just like extending a sentence. The trick is that it is return-conditioned: it is trained so that a single number — the return-to-go, meaning how much reward you expect from here on — steers the whole trajectory. At runtime you turn that one knob to slide continuously between cautious and fast, and the behavior shifts without retraining. That one dial cleanly spans the entire safety-versus-efficiency frontier, replacing the old multi-parameter hand-tuning. So instead of an expert re-tuning several settings, an operator moves a single scalar.
Why it matters
It is the trajectory-as-sequence idea applied to a fast-moving drone: the flight plan is rolled out token by token by a causal transformer. And it shows how much control you get from that framing — one return-to-go number reshapes the entire generated path.
Reading it whole. Top to bottom, the themes walk the same generator outward: first collapse the per-pixel sequence into per-scale steps, then escape the one-token bottleneck, then survive the drift over long video and 3D, then read-and-write in one model — and finally point the same next-token recipe at control so a robot writes its own actions. The outputs differ wildly — patches, frames, mesh faces, motor commands; the move — predict the next piece from all before it — never changes.

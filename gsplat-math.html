Gaussian splatting — why it works, mathematically

A 3D scene is a cloud of blob shapes — each one oval or flattened or needle-like. Project every blob onto the image once, blend them in depth order by multiplying how much light gets through each one, and let pixel errors drive changes to blob position and shape. No neural network ever sees a pixel directly.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

Gaussian splatting represents a 3D scene as many small soft blobs. Each blob has a position, size, color, and opacity. Rendering means projecting those blobs into the camera and blending them.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology asks how a scene is connected. Splats are excellent for appearance but can be weak at explicit structure: a surface may look right while its connectedness is unclear. Topology-aware splatting tries to keep blobs arranged as coherent surfaces, rooms, roads, or objects.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

It is used in 3D capture, virtual production, robotics maps, telepresence, architecture, and augmented reality.

Why Gaussian splatting is worth learning

It matters because it made 3D scenes fast to train and fast to view. That speed changed what researchers could try.

The problem, as a quantity

Reconstructing a 3D scene from ordinary photos is genuinely hard: you have many 2D images and must infer a single consistent 3D structure that could have produced all of them. An earlier method (Neural Radiance Fields, or NeRF) solved quality but introduced a bottleneck: to colour one pixel you must march a ray through the scene and ask a small neural network hundreds of times — the cost grows with number of ray steps × network depth per pixel, and a 1080p frame has over a million pixels, so real-time is impossible. Mesh-based rendering is fast but meshes are painful to recover accurately from photos and cannot represent fuzzy geometry (foliage, hair, partial transparency). The actual mathematical obstacle is a trilemma: the scene representation must be (1) continuously differentiable so gradient descent (nudging parameters to lower error) can fit it to photos; (2) rasterizable in O(K) where K is the number of scene elements — not the number of ray-steps — so speed scales with scene complexity, not pixel count; and (3) expressive enough to model both hard surfaces and soft, view-dependent appearance. No classical representation satisfies all three at once.

The core ideas — and why they work

Scenes as anisotropic Gaussian blobs

G_i(x) = exp(−½·(x−μ_i)ᵀ·Σ_i⁻¹·(x−μ_i)), Σ_i = R_i·S_i·S_iᵀ·R_iᵀ (rotation × scale²)
A Gaussian blob is defined entirely by its mean μ_i (position in 3D), covariance Σ_i (shape and tilt), opacity α_i (how solid), and colour c_i (optionally how colour changes with viewing angle). Factoring the covariance as Σ_i = R_i·S_i·S_iᵀ·R_iᵀ — a rotation matrix (the tilt) times a diagonal scale matrix (the stretches) — guarantees that Σ_i is always valid regardless of how gradients perturb it, and makes each degree of freedom interpretable: you are learning how much to stretch in each axis and how to tilt separately. An isotropic blob (equal stretches) is a sphere and can only model amorphous volume; a full anisotropic blob can flatten into a thin disc to model a wall, or elongate into a needle to model a wire. The scene is simply a list of a few hundred thousand such blobs — explicit, geometric, editable objects rather than weights buried inside a neural network. Because every parameter enters the rendering equations smoothly (the Gaussian function is infinitely differentiable in μ and Σ), gradients (the direction to nudge parameters to lower error) flow cleanly from pixel error back to blob position and shape.

Alpha-compositing: the transmittance product

C = Σ_i T_i · α_i · c_i, T_i = ∏_{j<i}(1 − α_j), T_1 = 1
Once every blob is projected onto the 2D image plane (a linear transformation: μ_i^{2D} = K·[R|t]·μ_i / z_i where K is the camera matrix, containing focal length and principal point), you blend them in depth order using the alpha-compositing formula. The transmittance T_i = ∏_{j<i}(1−α_j) is the fraction of light that survives past all blobs in front of blob i: if every blob before it is opaque (opacity near 1), T_i → 0 and the blob is occluded; if the scene is empty in front, T_i = 1 and the blob contributes fully. The rendered colour is then the depth-sorted weighted sum C = Σ_i T_i·α_i·c_i. This is not an approximation of volume rendering — it is the exact discrete volume-rendering formula under piecewise-constant density (assuming each blob has constant density). Every factor is differentiable: ∂C/∂α_i, ∂C/∂c_i, and (through the 2D Gaussian footprint) ∂C/∂μ_i and ∂C/∂Σ_i are all computable in closed form. This is the differentiable rendering principle — the same move as NeRF and depth estimation, where you put the physics of image formation inside the loss so that pixel supervision drives 3D structure. NeRF writes the same transmittance product as a continuous integral along a ray; 3DGS writes it as a discrete sum over sorted blobs. The math is identical; only the density parameterization changes.

Rasterization is O(#projected blobs), not O(ray-depth × MLP)

cost(render) = O(K·P / W·H) vs NeRF O(W·H·S·L_MLP), K≈10⁵, S≈200, L_MLP≈8
The key performance win is architectural, not quantitative: NeRF and 3DGS have inverted cost structures. NeRF fires one ray per pixel, samples it ~200 times, and runs a neural network at each sample — cost grows with image resolution × ray samples × network depth, and there is no shortcut because you cannot know which parts of the network matter before you query it. 3DGS instead processes each blob once: project to 2D, compute the 2D Gaussian footprint (the circular or oval shadow the blob casts), then add the blob to every pixel it overlaps — cost grows with the number of blobs times their average screen area. A scene of 200,000 Gaussians with average footprint of 10 pixels costs roughly 2 million pixel-operations, which is what a GPU dispatches in a fraction of a millisecond. Crucially, projection and blending are exactly the operations a GPU is designed for — they are the core of classical triangle rasterization (drawing polygons), so GPU hardware and drivers are already maximally optimized for this path. No neural-network query, no per-ray memory allocation, no iterative refinement: one forward pass over the blob list renders the frame. The obstacle it circumvents is underdetermination — you cannot know which network queries matter until you make them all, so NeRF cannot prune them. 3DGS sidesteps this by making the scene representation explicit: you can skip blobs that project outside the viewing frustum before touching a single pixel.

Photometric loss drives gradient descent on blob geometry

L = ||C_render(μ,Σ,α,c) − C_photo||² → ∂L/∂μ_i, ∂L/∂Σ_i, ∂L/∂α_i, ∂L/∂c_i via chain rule
Fitting the cloud to multi-view photos is pure gradient descent (nudging parameters downhill to lower error) on a pixel-space loss. You render an image, compare it to the ground-truth photo, backpropagate (trace the error backward through the equations) through the compositing formula and the 2D-projection geometry, and update every blob parameter. Because the Gaussian and the transmittance product are smooth, the gradient (the direction to nudge) is well-defined everywhere: no discrete choices, no sampling variance, no tricks. The gradient of the rendered colour with respect to blob position μ_i encodes 'move the blob toward pixels where you were too dark and away from pixels where you were too bright.' Gradients flow to covariance too, so a blob that should be a thin disc will be squeezed flat over training. Two practical strategies augment the gradient signal: adaptive densification (detect when an area should have a blob but the current blob cannot fill it, so split or add another blob) and pruning (remove blobs whose opacity drifts below a threshold — they stopped contributing). The result is a self-organising population of blobs that migrates and reshapes to explain the photos. Training a scene takes minutes on one GPU; inference is a single GPU pass at 100+ FPS. The weighted blend here is structurally the same as a mixture gate that blends outputs as Σ_i g_i·output_i, except here the weights are transmittance-opacity products and the outputs are Gaussian colour signals. In both cases a continuous, differentiable weighting replaces a hard selection, enabling gradient flow.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Every variant in the literature turns one of three knobs. Knob 1 — representation richness: replace the per-Gaussian constant colour c_i with a basis that models how colour changes with viewing angle to capture shiny surfaces; add a per-Gaussian compact number vector so the cloud is simultaneously a geometry model and a feature field for segmentation or language grounding. Knob 2 — scene dynamics: add time: parameterize μ_i(t) and Σ_i(t) as functions of a time coordinate (or learn deformation fields), keeping the rasterizer unchanged — you get 4D Gaussian splatting for video or dynamic robotics maps at essentially no extra rendering cost. Knob 3 — feed-forward prediction: instead of per-scene optimization (minutes), train a large encoder that takes sparse 3D observations (depth scans, stereo) and predicts the full Gaussian cloud in one forward pass (milliseconds). This trades per-scene quality for generalization. The rasterizer stays the same in all variants — it is the stable core around which representation and inference strategy rotate.

A worked numeric example

A ray sees two Gaussian blobs with opacity α=[0.3,0.5] and colors [1.0,0.2]. Alpha compositing gives 0.3·1.0 + (1-0.3)·0.5·0.2 = 0.37. Because this formula is differentiable, image error can move blob color, opacity, and position.

The through-line. The whole of 3D Gaussian splatting is a single insight: make the scene representation explicitly differentiable and rasterizable in one pass. A cloud of anisotropic Gaussian blobs is continuously differentiable in all its parameters, so photos can supervise blob geometry directly through the rendering equation without any neural network between the blob and the pixel. The transmittance product T_i = ∏_{j<i}(1−α_j) is the same physics that NeRF integrates along a ray — both methods implement the same volume-rendering law — but splatting discretizes it over explicit sorted blobs rather than implicit network samples, which is exactly the move that inverts the cost structure: from O(pixels × ray-samples × network) to O(blobs × footprint). The two moves that do everything — factorize geometry into differentiable primitives, put the physics in the loss — are the same moves appearing in differentiable mesh rendering and depth estimation. 3D Gaussian splatting makes those moves in the most hardware-friendly form yet, which is why it displaced a generation of neural methods not by being more expressive but by being honestly shaped for the problem: explicit, geometric, parallelizable, and fully differentiable.

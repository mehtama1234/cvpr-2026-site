Gaussian splatting — why it works, mathematically

Rebuild a 3D scene you can fly through from a handful of photos, and draw it fast. Instead of asking a slow function for the color at points along every sightline, fill the scene with thousands of little colored fuzzy blobs and paint a view by stamping them onto the screen.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

Gaussian splatting represents a 3D scene as many small soft blobs. Each blob has a position, size, color, and opacity. Rendering means projecting those blobs into the camera and blending them.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology asks how a scene is connected. Splats are excellent for appearance but can be weak at explicit structure: a surface may look right while its connectedness is unclear. Topology-aware splatting tries to keep blobs arranged as coherent surfaces, rooms, roads, or objects.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

It is used in 3D capture, virtual production, robotics maps, telepresence, architecture, and augmented reality.

Why Gaussian splatting is worth learning

It matters because it made 3D scenes fast to train and fast to view. That speed changed what researchers could try.

The key turn — the principle at work

The stuck point. A 3D scene must be stored in a form that can render quickly from new views. A dense volume is expensive, and a mesh can be hard to recover from photos.

The mathematical move. The principle is differentiable scene particles. Gaussian splatting represents the scene as many soft colored blobs in 3D. Rendering projects and blends those blobs, and the image error can train their positions, shapes, colors, and opacity.

What changes. 3D reconstruction stops needing a slow ray march through every point in space. The scene becomes a set of editable blobs that draw fast, but quality depends on whether the blobs cover the true surfaces cleanly.

The problem, as a quantity

You want the full 3D of a scene back from a few photos, and you want to be able to move a virtual camera through it smoothly, in real time. One approach represents the scene as a function you query along every pixel's sightline — accurate, but so slow that a single frame takes many heavy function-calls per pixel, and flying through it crawls.

The problem is to find a scene representation that (a) can be drawn extremely fast, (b) still blends into smooth, continuous surfaces rather than looking like a pile of dots, and (c) can be shaped automatically from photos — nobody hand-places anything. The answer is to build the scene out of soft blobs that can be stamped onto the screen cheaply, and to let the photos themselves arrange those blobs.

The core ideas — and why they work

A scene is a cloud of little colored fuzzy blobs

G_i(x) = exp(−½·(x−μ_i)ᵀ·Σ_i⁻¹·(x−μ_i)), Σ_i = R_i·S_i·S_iᵀ·R_iᵀ (rotation × scale²)
Represent the whole 3D scene as thousands of little soft blobs floating in space. Each blob carries a center — where it sits — a color, a see-through-ness, and, crucially, a shape: it can be squished into an oval, long and thin in one direction and flat in another, and turned to face any angle.

Why ovals rather than round dots? Because the real world is full of flat, stretched surfaces — a tabletop, a wall, a leaf — and one stretched blob can hug a flat surface that would take many round dots to cover. Each blob is densest at its center and fades softly outward, so neighboring blobs overlap and blend into continuous surfaces instead of showing up as separate specks. The scene is just this cloud: its geometry, its color, and its solidness all live in the blobs' centers, shapes, colors, and see-through-nesses.

Painting a view: stack the blobs front to back

C = Σ_i T_i · α_i · c_i, T_i = ∏_{j<i}(1 − α_j), T_1 = 1
To render the scene from a viewpoint, sort the blobs by distance and lay them down front to back. Each blob adds its color in proportion to two things: how solid it is — its see-through-ness — and how much light still reaches it, which shrinks the more solid blobs sit in front of it.

So a pixel's final color is the frontmost blobs shown at nearly full strength, the ones behind them showing through only where the front blobs are thin, and so on into the distance. A solid near blob hides what is behind it; a wispy one lets the background peek through. This front-to-back blending is exactly what makes near things correctly cover far things, and what lets soft edges and semi-transparent surfaces — smoke, glass, hair — come out right instead of as hard cutouts.

Why it draws so much faster than asking a function along every ray

cost(render) = O(K·P / W·H) vs NeRF O(W·H·S·L_MLP), K≈10⁵, S≈200, L_MLP≈8
The earlier neural-field approach draws a frame by marching along every pixel's sightline and asking a heavy function for color and solidness at dozens of points per pixel — an enormous amount of work for every single frame.

Splatting turns the effort around. Instead of every pixel hunting through space, each blob simply works out where it lands on the screen and stamps its soft oval there; the screen then adds up the overlapping stamps. The cost now scales with how many blobs there are, not with heavy function-calls per pixel — and stamping ovals onto a screen is precisely the operation graphics hardware is built to do at blistering speed. That is why a splatted scene can be flown through smoothly in real time, while the ray-marching approach, doing far more work per pixel, can only crawl.

The blobs arrange themselves to match the photos

L = ||C_render(μ,Σ,α,c) − C_photo||² → ∂L/∂μ_i, ∂L/∂Σ_i, ∂L/∂α_i, ∂L/∂c_i via chain rule
Nobody places the blobs by hand. Start with a rough scattering, draw the scene from a camera angle you have a real photo for, and measure how far the drawn picture is from that photo.

Then nudge every blob — its position, its color, its shape, its see-through-ness — in whatever direction shrinks that gap, and repeat across all the photos from all the angles. Because the whole stamp-and-blend drawing is built from smooth steps, this error can be traced back to each individual blob, so the cloud gradually rearranges itself: blobs slide to where surfaces really are, stretch to lie flat against them, and take on the right colors. Where detail is missing, extra blobs are spawned; where blobs contribute nothing, they are removed. The photos alone, through this tracing-back, teach the cloud its entire shape.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Splatting methods turn a few dials on the same idea.

How many blobs, and where. Spawn more blobs where a region is still blurry and prune ones that earn nothing, so detail concentrates where the photos demand it. What a blob carries. Beyond color and shape, blobs can hold a direction-dependent shine, or a tag of what object they belong to, so the same cloud can be queried by words or split into parts. Still scenes versus moving ones. Give blobs a motion over time, or a separate set per instant, to capture a scene that changes. Vision versus robotics. As a prettier reconstruction to look at, or as a live map a robot grows from its own camera and then acts inside. The thread never changes: build the scene from soft, stampable blobs, and let the photos rearrange them by tracing each picture's error back to each blob.

A worked numeric example

A ray sees two Gaussian blobs with opacity α=[0.3,0.5] and colors [1.0,0.2]. Alpha compositing gives 0.3·1.0 + (1-0.3)·0.5·0.2 = 0.37. Because this formula is differentiable, image error can move blob color, opacity, and position.

The through-line. The core idea is to represent a 3D scene as a cloud of little colored fuzzy ovals and to paint any view by stamping them front to back onto the screen. Letting the ovals stretch lets a few of them hug flat surfaces; front-to-back blending makes near things correctly cover far things and handles soft, see-through surfaces; stamping instead of marching along every ray is what makes it real-time fast; and tracing each photo's mismatch back to the blobs is how the cloud learns its shape from pictures alone. Two plain ideas carry it: build the scene from soft overlapping blobs you can stamp cheaply, and let the photos rearrange those blobs by tracing each picture's error back to them.

Information bottleneck — why it works, mathematically

Generalization means learning the pattern that repeats, not memorizing accidents. A good representation is just wide enough to carry the pattern but too narrow to carry every accident of measurement.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

The information bottleneck asks a model to keep what predicts the answer and drop the rest. A good representation should be narrow enough to ignore accidents but wide enough to keep the cause.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology often separates deep structure from surface detail. A donut stays a donut under many visual changes. A bottleneck can force the model to keep that stable structure instead of memorizing texture, lighting, or noise.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

It appears in compression, privacy, biology, neuroscience, representation learning, distillation, and domain adaptation.

Why Information bottleneck is worth learning

It matters because generalization depends on forgetting the right things. A model that keeps every detail may keep the wrong details.

The problem, as a quantity

An input X contains two kinds of information: the part that predicts the target Y, and the part that only identifies this particular sample. A big model can store both, and that is the danger. If the representation Z keeps every scratch mark, texture, watermark, and dataset habit in X, training loss can fall while the learned pattern is mostly a memory of accidents. What you really want is selective information: preserve what predicts Y, discard what only describes X itself.

The core ideas — and why they work

Keep the label, forget the accident

min encoder I(Z;X) − β·I(Z;Y)
The bottleneck is a trade: punish a representation for remembering too much raw input, then reward it for keeping what still predicts the label. Information is not one substance. Some bits of X are shared with Y; other bits are private noise. Compression hits the private noise first when it is not needed downstream. That is why the bottleneck does not make the model ignorant. It forces a question: after I throw away detail, what remains that still changes the answer?

A representation is a pipe with limits, not a storage box

X → Z → Y, p(y|x,z)=p(y|z)
The chain says the prediction must go through Z. Once Z is known, the model cannot sneak back to the raw input. That makes Z a pipe with finite capacity, not a drawer. A limited pipe must spend its bits where they change the prediction. If background color and label are correlated in training but not in the real world, compression can drop the background because it is expensive to carry and unstable as evidence. This is why learning a representation is not just pulling out features. It is choosing what to forget.

Noise makes compression smooth and trainable

z = μ(x) + σ(x)·ε, ε ~ N(0,I), capacity ≈ KL(q(z|x) || p(z))
A hard rule that erases bits is awkward to optimize. A noisy channel is smooth. If the encoder sends x through z = μ + σε (a mean plus random noise), then small details survive only by spending variance, moving the learned distribution away from the base distribution. The KL term (a score for how far one distribution is from another) is the price of that move. Details that do not help the label become too expensive to keep. This Gaussian-channel accounting is the same tool used by autoencoders and models that measure uncertainty: noise is not decoration, it is the ruler that counts how many distinct messages the representation can carry.

Too much squeeze erases the answer

β ↑ ⇒ I(Z;X) ↓, but if I(Z;Y) ↓ too, accuracy ↓
Compression is only helpful until it erases the evidence. The bottleneck has a threshold: weak compression stores accidental details; moderate compression keeps the pattern that predicts the label; excessive compression makes different answers look the same. This matters because simplicity by itself is not magic. A representation generalizes when its remaining bits match the true causal degrees of freedom of the task, not when it is merely small.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Methods differ mainly in how they estimate the mutual information terms, which are difficult to compute exactly. Variational bottlenecks use a noisy encoder and a KL penalty to limit channel capacity. Invariant methods keep the part of two views that agrees while dropping view-specific accidents. Minimal sufficient representation methods search for the smallest Z that still predicts Y. In images, this shows up whenever masking, distillation, or removing redundant tokens asks the same question: which information survives a squeeze and still supports the answer?

A worked numeric example

In the toy run at analysis/ib/toy_ib.json, the representation has one label bit and one accident direction. With β=0, the information about the input is high, I(Z;X)=2.07, but accuracy is lower because the accident survives. At β=0.3, accident kept drops to 0.40 and accuracy peaks at 0.938. At larger β, the label information also falls, so compression stops helping. The finding is the bottleneck in numbers: keep less of X, but not less of the part of X that predicts Y.

1Variational bottlenecks
L = E[-log p(y|z)] + β·KL(q(z|x) || p(z))
The KL term charges the encoder for each new message it sends about the input. Prediction loss pays back only if the bits help predict the target. The trade scales up and down with β: high β makes the encoder compress hard; low β lets it keep more.

2Invariant views
keep information shared by x₁ and x₂, drop information private to one view
Take two different versions of the same input (e.g., two crops of the image, or two time steps of the same scene). Both share the object but not every detail: not every crop angle, color shift, or noise pattern. Agreement between the two becomes a filter for stable information. What changes between views is usually accident; what stays the same across views is usually the pattern.

3Token pruning
choose S with |S|≪n while preserving p(y|tokens_S)
A finite token budget forces the model to keep the visual bits that change the answer and throw away patches that repeat or don't matter. Each token costs computation and memory, so the model must spend them where they buy predictive power. This is discrete compression: either keep the token or drop it entirely.

The through-line. The information bottleneck is the clearest statement of why learning is possible at all: the world repeats structure, but accidents never do. A representation should be just wide enough to carry the structure that predicts the target and too narrow to carry every accident of the measurement. That is the parent idea behind compression, invariance, distillation, token removal, and the hope that a model learns the rule instead of the dataset.

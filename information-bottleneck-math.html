Information bottleneck — why it works, mathematically

To recognize a friend's face you don't memorize every pixel — the exact lighting, the background, a stray hair. You keep what identifies them and let the accidental rest fall away. A good internal summary is a narrow pipe that passes the signal and blocks the noise.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

The information bottleneck asks a model to keep what predicts the answer and drop the rest. A good representation should be narrow enough to ignore accidents but wide enough to keep the cause.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology often separates deep structure from surface detail. A donut stays a donut under many visual changes. A bottleneck can force the model to keep that stable structure instead of memorizing texture, lighting, or noise.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

It appears in compression, privacy, biology, neuroscience, representation learning, distillation, and domain adaptation.

Why Information bottleneck is worth learning

It matters because generalization depends on forgetting the right things. A model that keeps every detail may keep the wrong details.

The problem, as a quantity

When a model learns to recognize something, most of what it is shown is accidental — the exact lighting on a face, the background behind it, a stray hair — and only a little of it actually identifies the thing. If the model keeps everything, it drags all that accidental clutter along and gets confused by it on new examples; if it keeps too little, it throws away the very details it needs.

So a good internal summary has to strike a balance: hold on to the part that predicts the answer, and deliberately forget the part that is just accident. The problem is how to make a model forget on purpose — to build a summary that is narrow enough to shed the noise yet wide enough to keep the signal — and how to find the point between squeezing too little and squeezing too much.

The core ideas — and why they work

Keep what predicts the answer, forget the rest

min encoder I(Z;X) − β·I(Z;Y)
When you learn to recognize something, most of what is in front of you is accidental — the exact lighting on a face, the background, a stray hair — and only a little of it identifies the thing. A good internal summary should hold onto the identifying part and deliberately forget the accidental part.

Written as a goal, that is two pulls in tension: keep as little of the raw input as you can, forgetting aggressively, while keeping as much as possible of the part that predicts the answer, so you do not forget what matters. A single dial sets how hard you push to forget. The whole idea is that understanding is as much about throwing the right things away as about keeping things — a summary that remembers everything has understood nothing.

A summary is a pipe, not a storage box

X → Z → Y, p(y|x,z)=p(y|z)
Think of the summary as a narrow pipe that everything must pass through on its way from the raw input to the answer. Whatever does not make it through the pipe is simply gone — the answer has to be rebuilt from the summary alone, never from the original input again.

This is why the width of the pipe matters so much. Make it too wide and it carries the accidental junk along with the signal; make it too narrow and it strangles the signal too. Seeing the summary as a limited pipe rather than a roomy storage box is the key shift in view — it forces the question 'what is actually worth letting through?', because a pipe, unlike a box, cannot carry everything, and the model must choose.

Adding a little noise turns forgetting into something you can tune

z = μ(x) + σ(x)·ε, ε ~ N(0,I), capacity ≈ KL(q(z|x) || p(z))
How do you actually make a network forget on purpose? A clean trick: deliberately add a little random noise to the summary.

Noise blurs fine distinctions, so any detail the network wants to preserve it must record strongly enough to survive the blur — and it is simply not worth spending that effort on accidental junk. By controlling how much noise you inject, you smoothly control how much the summary is allowed to remember: more noise forces more forgetting. And because it is a smooth dial rather than a hard cutoff, the network can learn how much to keep through ordinary small nudges. Noise turns 'forget the unimportant stuff' from an all-or-nothing choice into a tunable pressure that training can actually navigate.

Squeeze too hard and you lose the answer along with the noise

β ↑ ⇒ I(Z;X) ↓, but if I(Z;Y) ↓ too, accuracy ↓
There is a sweet spot, and you can overshoot it. Turn the forget-harder dial up gently and the summary sheds accidental junk while keeping the identifying signal — accuracy improves, because the leftover clutter was only distracting.

But keep turning it, and eventually the pipe grows so narrow that it starts strangling the signal too, dropping detail the answer genuinely needed — and accuracy falls. So compression helps only up to the point where you are discarding real noise; past that you are discarding meaning. The art is to squeeze exactly hard enough to crush the accidents and no harder, and knowing where that line sits — how much your particular data will let you forget before the answer suffers — is the whole balancing act.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Methods here turn a few dials on the same idea.

How hard to squeeze. The forget-harder dial sets the balance; too gentle keeps clutter, too hard crushes the signal. How forgetting is enforced. Injected noise that blurs away weakly-held detail, or a hard limit on how much the summary can carry. What the summary is for. A cleaner representation that generalizes better to new examples, or a compact code for storage and transmission. Where the balance sits. Tuned to the data, since noisier data can afford to forget more of the input. The thread never changes: keep the part that predicts the answer and forget the accidental part, and treat the summary as a limited pipe whose width you tune to pass signal and block noise.

A worked numeric example

In the toy run at analysis/ib/toy_ib.json, the representation has one label bit and one accident direction. With β=0, the information about the input is high, I(Z;X)=2.07, but accuracy is lower because the accident survives. At β=0.3, accident kept drops to 0.40 and accuracy peaks at 0.938. At larger β, the label information also falls, so compression stops helping. The trade shows the bottleneck in action: keep less of X, but not less of the part of X that predicts Y.

1Variational bottlenecks
L = E[-log p(y|z)] + β·KL(q(z|x) || p(z))
The KL term (Kullback-Leibler divergence: a measure of how far one distribution is from another) charges the encoder for each new message it sends about the input. Prediction loss pays back only if the bits help predict the target. The trade scales up and down with β: high β makes the encoder compress hard; low β lets it keep more.

2Invariant views
keep information shared by x₁ and x₂, drop information private to one view
Take two different versions of the same input (e.g., two crops of the image, or two time steps of the same scene). Both share the object but not every detail: not every crop angle, color shift, or noise pattern. Agreement between the two becomes a filter for stable information. What changes between views is usually accident; what stays the same across views is usually the pattern.

Connection to self-supervised learning
The shared-view signal is the mutual information (shared information between two things, measured in bits) worth keeping; view-specific accident is the nuisance to drop.
3Token pruning
choose S with |S|≪n while preserving p(y|tokens_S)
A finite token budget forces the model to keep the visual bits that change the answer and throw away patches that repeat or don't matter. Each token costs computation and memory, so the model must spend them where they buy predictive power. This is discrete compression: either keep the token or drop it entirely.

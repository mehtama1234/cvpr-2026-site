1 · The action head: sample, don't average

A VLA takes what the robot sees and what it is told and turns that into an action, and the piece that actually produces the numbers is the action head. Here is the trap: the demonstrations we train on are multimodal — to reach around a mug there is a valid path to the left and an equally valid path to the right, and a human teacher will do both on different days. If you train the head with plain MSE regression, it minimizes error by predicting the average of the two, and the average path drives straight into the mug — a perfectly reasonable-looking prediction that is physically invalid. The cure is a generative action head that models the whole distribution of good actions and samples one committed mode instead of splitting the difference. Two paradigms dominate: diffusion, which starts from noise and denoises into a coherent trajectory, and autoregressive tokens, which quantize actions into a discrete codebook the model predicts one symbol at a time. The companion fix is action chunking — committing to a short burst of future actions at once — which stops the tiny per-step errors of open-loop imitation from compounding into drift.
ROBOTICS'25The Ingredients for Robotic Diffusion Transformers
Problem
Diffusion is a strong generative action head and Transformers are the strongest general architecture, but nobody had a systematic recipe for combining them. The design choices that make a diffusion Transformer policy actually work — especially for long, dexterous, two-armed tasks — were poorly understood and mostly folklore.
Approach
The authors run careful ablations across the design decisions and distill them into DiT-Block Policy, an architecture that folds the diffusion denoising step directly inside the Transformer blocks. The head does not regress a single action; it starts from noise and iteratively denoises toward a full action trajectory, so it can represent many valid ways to do a task instead of collapsing them. Because it samples from the learned distribution, it commits to one coherent mode rather than mode-averaging into a stuck-in-the-middle action that a plain regressor would produce. This lets it handle 1500-plus-timestep bimanual ALOHA tasks where a mode-averaged prediction would fail immediately.
Why it matters
It shows that when you build the generative head right, diffusion Transformers keep improving as you add data and can sustain the long, coordinated action sequences that break simpler policies.
ROBOTICS'25Diffusion-Based Approximate MPC: Fast and Consistent Imitation of Multi-Modal Action Distributions
Problem
Model predictive control is powerful but slow, so people try to imitate it with a fast neural net. The problem is that MPC solutions are genuinely multi-modal — obstacles and nonconvex constraints create several equally good answers — and an L2-regression net averages them into a single blended solution that satisfies none of them.
Approach
Instead of regressing one answer, this work learns the full distribution of MPC solutions with a diffusion model, so both the go-left and go-right ways around an obstacle are represented. At runtime it draws several samples in parallel and scores each against the cost and constraints, picking the best genuine mode rather than a mode-average. Gradient guidance during the denoising steps keeps the chosen mode consistent across time so the robot does not flip-flop between modes and jam. This runs at 250 Hz on a 7-DoF arm, over 70x faster than solving the MPC online.
Why it matters
It is a clean demonstration that regression literally averages valid solutions into an invalid one, and that a sampling-based generative head recovers real, committed control at kilohertz speeds.
ROBOTICS'25Discrete Policy: Learning Disentangled Action Space for Multi-Task Robotic Manipulation
Problem
As you ask one policy to do more and more tasks, the mixture of actions it must represent gets increasingly multimodal and tangled. A single head trying to cover all of it tends to blur distinct task behaviors together, which caps generalization and performance.
Approach
This paper uses the token paradigm rather than diffusion: vector quantization maps action sequences into a discrete latent codebook, and the model learns task-specific discrete codes decoded back into actions given the image and language instruction. Because each mode of behavior gets its own discrete code, the head can pick a clean, disentangled action instead of averaging across tasks into a muddy middle. The quantized codebook effectively separates the modes so the policy commits to one, sidestepping the mode-averaging that hurts a continuous regressor. It works on both single-arm and bimanual setups.
Why it matters
Discrete Policy beats Diffusion Policy by 26 to 32.5% in multi-task settings, and the gap grows with more tasks — evidence that a quantized-token head is a strong alternative to diffusion for taming multimodal action spaces.
ROBOTICS'25Action Tokenizer Matters in In-Context Imitation Learning
Problem
In-context imitation learning represents actions as discrete tokens, but the usual tokenizers chop trajectories into codes without caring whether nearby actions map to nearby codes. That breaks temporal smoothness, so the decoded robot motion comes out jerky and unstable.
Approach
The fix targets the tokenizer itself, the front end of a token-based action head. LipVQ-VAE trains the VQ-VAE with a Lipschitz-continuity constraint via weight normalization, so small changes in the raw action produce small changes in the quantized code. This carries smoothness all the way through to the codebook entries the in-context model actually predicts and executes. It keeps the benefit of a discrete, sample-a-token generative head — picking one committed action rather than a mode-averaged blend — while removing the roughness that naive quantization introduces.
Why it matters
It raises in-context imitation success by over 5.3% and yields visibly smoother real-robot trajectories, showing the choice of action tokenizer, not just the policy on top, decides whether the generated actions are usable.

2 · Ground the language to behavior

An instruction is made of words, and words are discrete and compositional: you can snap together a handful of them to name a situation the robot has never seen. The world the robot acts in is the opposite kind of thing, a continuous flood of pixels, forces, and motor commands with no clean seams. Grounding is the work of stitching these two together, so that an open-vocabulary phrase actually resolves to this object in this scene, and so that the relations between words (put the small cup inside the big one) map onto real behavior rather than a memorized label. The hard part is generalization: a robot dataset is tiny, so the system has to understand words it never saw a demonstration for. The recurring answer across these papers is to inherit grounding from an internet-pretrained vision-language model, which has already learned to bind millions of words to millions of images. Language then becomes a shared latent, a common bridge that lets vision, touch, goal images, and actions all be described in the same space and swapped for one another.

ROBOTICS'25STEER: Flexible Robotic Manipulation via Dense Language Grounding
Problem

A robot trained on a fixed set of demonstrations tends to freeze when the situation drifts even slightly from what it saw. Handling a genuinely new task usually means collecting more data and retraining, which is slow and does not scale to the open-ended requests people actually make.

Approach

STEER breaks manipulation into small reusable skills and trains each one as a language-conditioned policy, but the trick is dense annotation: instead of one coarse label per demonstration, it attaches fine-grained natural-language descriptions throughout, so the policy learns to bind specific words to specific ways of moving. Because every skill is addressed through language rather than a fixed index, the skills form an open compositional interface you can drive by talking to it. At runtime a vision-language model reads the scene and the goal and composes these primitives into a plan, choosing and sequencing skills to synthesize a behavior nobody demonstrated. Language is the shared latent that lets the pretrained VLM, which already understands words far beyond the robot dataset, hand instructions down to the low-level controller. New tasks are assembled from known parts on the fly, with no extra data and no retraining.

Why it matters

Dense language annotation turns a flat policy into a vocabulary of recombinable behaviors, so the robot inherits the VLM's open-ended understanding and generalizes by composition rather than by collecting more demonstrations.

ROBOTICS'25Beyond Sight: Finetuning Generalist Robot Policies with Heterogeneous Sensors via Language Grounding
Problem

Generalist robot policies are trained almost entirely on images, so they go blind to the world the moment vision is not enough, when a hand is buried in a drawer, an object is out of view, or the useful cue is a sound or a touch. Those non-visual senses carry the decisive information, but the pretrained policy has no way to use them.

Approach

FuSe finetunes an existing visuomotor generalist, whether a diffusion policy or a full VLA, to accept extra sensors like touch and sound without needing a giant paired multimodal dataset. The key is to use natural language as the cross-modal bridge: a contrastive loss pulls every modality toward the same language description of what is happening, and a second loss makes the model generate language grounded in what it feels or hears. Because a word like soft or rattling already means something to the pretrained language backbone, touch and sound get anchored to that inherited meaning rather than learned from scratch. This turns language into a shared latent where vision, touch, and audio are all descriptions of one situation and can be reasoned over together. The result is zero-shot multimodal prompting, telling the robot to grab the squishy object even when the camera cannot tell.

Why it matters

By making every sensor answer to the same language, the policy borrows the VLM's grounding to fold in touch and sound cheaply, so it stays capable exactly when sight fails.

ROBOTICS'25Robo-MUTUAL: Robotic Multimodal Task Specification via Unimodal Learning
Problem

People specify a task in whatever modality is handy, a sentence, a photo of the goal, a pointed gesture, but robots want to see the same goal expressed many ways to learn what they have in common. Paired multimodal data, the same task captured as both language and image, is scarce and expensive to collect.

Approach

Robo-MUTUAL sidesteps the need for paired data by pretraining a robotic multimodal encoder on large piles of unimodal out-of-domain data, plenty of text alone and plenty of images alone. That pretraining, inherited from internet-scale vision-language learning, already lands the modalities in a roughly shared space, and two operations called Collapse and Corrupt close the leftover gap so that a language description of a goal and an image of the same goal become interchangeable latent codes. Once they are interchangeable, language works as the common currency: a policy trained to follow one modality will follow the others for free, because they all decode to the same instruction. This is the shared-latent trick in its purest form, alignment bought with cheap unimodal data instead of rare paired annotation. It was validated across more than 130 tasks.

Why it matters

Making different task specifications collapse into one shared latent lets a robot accept goals in any modality without ever collecting the paired multimodal data that grounding usually seems to demand.

ROBOTICS'25KALIE: Fine-Tuning Vision-Language Models for Open-World Manipulation Without Robot Data
Problem

Getting a robot to manipulate objects it has never seen in an open world normally means gathering large amounts of robot-collected interaction data, which is costly and hard to scale to the endless variety of real objects. The bottleneck is that behavior has to be grounded in the physical scene, and that grounding is what expensive robot data is buying.

Approach

KALIE moves the grounding into a vision-language model and cuts robot data out entirely. It fine-tunes the VLM to read an instruction and an image and output a point-based affordance, concrete pixels on the object saying where to grasp or push, so an open-vocabulary phrase resolves directly to actionable locations in this scene. Crucially it trains on nothing but human-labeled 2D images, and an affordance-aware synthesis pipeline multiplies a handful of human examples into large-scale training data automatically. Because the underlying VLM already binds words to images from internet-scale pretraining, KALIE inherits that open-vocabulary grounding and only has to teach it the thin affordance-prediction layer. The points it emits are the bridge from a symbolic instruction to a continuous motor plan.

Why it matters

Predicting affordance points instead of raw actions lets a pretrained VLM supply the grounding, so KALIE manipulates unseen objects from only about 50 human-labeled images and no robot data at all.

3 · Adapt a VLM backbone, cheaply

The recipe for a vision-language-action model is to take a vision-language-model that already learned to see and read from the internet, then graft a small action head onto it so the same network can output robot commands. The hope is that the robot inherits the backbone's generality: it recognizes objects, backgrounds, and instructions it never saw in any robot demonstration. But two hard problems get in the way. First, when you fine-tune that backbone on a narrow pile of robot data, it quietly overwrites what it knew before — a mug on a cluttered desk becomes unrecognizable the moment the desk looks different, because the general visual skill has been erased (catastrophic forgetting). Second, these backbones have billions of parameters, so retraining all of them for every robot is slow and expensive. The papers here answer with three moves: model-merging that reverts a fine-tuned backbone back toward its original general weights, LoRA and mixture-of-experts that adapt the model by training only a few small add-on modules instead of the whole thing, and weights-frozen run-time fixes that repair the input image instead of touching the model at all.
ROBOTICS'25ReVLA: Reverting Visual Domain Limitation of Robotic Foundation Models
Problem
A vision-language-action model like OpenVLA starts life able to recognize almost anything, because its vision backbone was pretrained on internet-scale images. But fine-tuning it on robot data quietly destroys that skill — the model succeeds on scenes that look like its robot training set and fails the moment the background, lighting, or objects drift out of distribution.
Approach
ReVLA treats forgetting as something you can literally undo. After the robot fine-tuning is done, it does not retrain anything; instead it uses model merging to gradually blend the fine-tuned vision backbone back toward the original pretrained DINO-v2 weights. It sweeps this reversal in steps, restoring general visual features while keeping enough of the fine-tuned behavior to still do the robot task. Because the whole procedure is gradient-free — just weighted averaging of two sets of weights — it costs almost nothing compared to any retraining. The recovered model regains its out-of-distribution eyesight, improving grasping and lifting on visually unfamiliar scenes by 77% and 66%.
Why it matters
ReVLA is the cleanest illustration of the theme's core tension: fine-tuning buys robot skill but spends visual generality, and here the fix is simply to move the weights part-way back — the same weight-arithmetic instinct that makes low-rank adapters so attractive elsewhere on this site.
ROBOTICS'25MoRE: Unlocking Scalability in Reinforcement Learning for Quadruped Vision-Language-Action Models
Problem
Fine-tuning a large vision-language-action model for a walking robot means feeding it messy, mixed-quality data covering many different skills. Doing this by retraining the entire multi-billion-parameter model is expensive, and cramming unrelated skills into one dense network makes them interfere with each other.
Approach
MoRE keeps the big multimodal backbone frozen and bolts on several low-rank adaptation (LoRA) modules, then wires them together as a sparse mixture-of-experts so that only a few small adapters activate for any given input. This means adaptation happens in a handful of cheap add-on weights rather than the whole model, and different experts can specialize in different quadruped skills without stepping on each other. The combined model is then trained as a Q-function with reinforcement learning, letting it learn from automatically collected interaction data of varying quality instead of only clean expert demonstrations. The result outperforms baselines across six quadruped skills and still generalizes to situations it was not trained on.
Why it matters
MoRE is the theme's LoRA-plus-MoE answer made concrete: it shows you can adapt a giant frozen backbone into a capable policy by training only a sparse set of low-rank experts — exactly the cheap-adaptation strategy this site keeps returning to.
ROBOTICS'25Improving Vision-Language-Action Model with Online Reinforcement Learning
Problem
A vision-language-action model trained by copying expert demonstrations can only ever be as good as those demonstrations; to get better it needs to try things and learn from the outcomes. But running reinforcement learning directly on a huge model is unstable and computationally punishing, so most systems never take that step.
Approach
iRe-VLA splits the improvement loop into two alternating phases so neither one blows up. In the reinforcement-learning phase the model explores the environment and gathers fresh trial-and-error data that covers situations the expert demonstrations missed. In the supervised phase it folds that new data back in with stable imitation-style training, which anchors the policy and prevents the wild divergence that direct RL on a large model causes. By bouncing between exploration and stabilization, the large VLA keeps improving from its own interaction without the instability of end-to-end online RL.
Why it matters
iRe-VLA shows the other axis of cheap adaptation: rather than paying for unstable full-model RL, it interleaves light exploration with stabilizing supervision so a pretrained backbone can grow from experience without being torn apart.
ROBOTICS'25Run-time Observation Interventions Make Vision-Language-Action Models More Visually Robust
Problem
Even after enormous training, vision-language-action models are surprisingly fragile to visual clutter — a different background color or an extra unrelated object on the table can cut task success by as much as 60%. The model fixates on things that have nothing to do with the job.
Approach
BYOVLA never touches the model's weights at all — it fixes the picture instead. At run time it probes which regions of the camera image the VLA is actually sensitive to, identifies the ones that are irrelevant to the task, and applies minimal automated edits to neutralize those distracting regions before the model sees them. Because it needs no fine-tuning and no access to the model's internals, it drops in on top of any off-the-shelf VLA. This training-free intervention nearly restores clean-scene performance even when the environment is full of distractors.
Why it matters
BYOVLA is the weights-frozen extreme of the theme: when fine-tuning would risk forgetting and full retraining is too costly, you can instead repair the input on the fly and leave the pretrained backbone completely untouched.

4 · Feed it, and make it fast enough

A vision-language-action model only learns to move a robot if you show it robots moving, and that is the one thing almost nobody has enough of. Every real demonstration means a person teleoperating a real arm through a real task, and the cost of that scales terribly the moment you add a second arm or multi-fingered hands. So the field's first move is to stop collecting and start manufacturing data: replay a handful of real trajectories inside a simulator to swap in new objects, composite the results back onto real backgrounds, and share the same demonstrations across different robot bodies. The second constraint bites at deployment. A billion-parameter model that generates actions one token at a time is far too slow for a control loop that needs to fire at tens or hundreds of hertz, so a whole sub-field exists just to make an already-trained model emit actions faster. Their tricks are surgical and mostly training-free: decode a whole chunk of actions in parallel instead of one step at a time, discretize action chunks into a compact vocabulary the model can predict in a single shot, or stream the denoising so the robot never waits. The two problems are one problem — feed the model, then make it fast enough to actually use.

ROBOTICS'25ReBot: Scaling Robot Learning with Real-to-Sim-to-Real Robotic Video Synthesis
Problem

Training a vision-language-action model well means feeding it huge amounts of real robot footage, but collecting that footage is slow and expensive — every clip is a real arm doing a real task in front of a real camera. That scarcity caps how big these models can get and how well they cope with new objects and new scenes.

Approach

ReBot manufactures the missing footage in three moves. First it takes trajectories a real robot already recorded and replays them inside a simulator, where it can freely swap in new objects the robot never actually touched — this is the real-to-sim step that diversifies what the robot appears to manipulate. Then it renders the simulated arm doing those new manipulations and composites that motion back onto real inpainted backgrounds, so the synthetic clip looks like it came from the real world rather than a game engine — the sim-to-real step. Because the motion is grounded in trajectories that were physically valid to begin with, the generated videos stay physically realistic and temporally consistent frame to frame. The result is a flood of new training clips that never required touching the robot again.

Why it matters

Fine-tuning OpenVLA on this synthesized data lifts in-domain success by up to 21.8% and out-of-domain generalization by 19.9%, showing that manufactured demonstrations can substitute for expensive real ones.

ROBOTICS'25DexMimicGen: Automated Data Generation for Bimanual Dexterous Manipulation via Imitation Learning
Problem

The hardest data to collect is for two-armed robots with multi-fingered hands, because a human has to teleoperate both arms and all those fingers at once — a coordination nightmare. As a result there are almost no large demonstration sets for bimanual dexterous manipulation, which is exactly the skill humanoids need.

Approach

DexMimicGen starts from a tiny seed of human demonstrations and grows it automatically inside simulation. It uses imitation learning to synthesize fresh trajectories that accomplish the same tasks under new object placements and configurations, rather than asking a person to demonstrate each variation by hand. The scale-up is dramatic: 60 source demonstrations become 21,000 synthetic ones spanning a range of two-armed dexterous tasks. A real-to-sim-to-real transfer pipeline then carries the simulated skills back onto physical hardware, so the synthetic data is not stranded in the simulator. It ships with a simulation benchmark built specifically for the bimanual dexterous setting.

Why it matters

It turns the most teleoperation-expensive category of robot data into something a machine can mass-produce, and the demonstrations transfer all the way to a real humanoid robot.

ROBOTICS'25PD-VLA: Accelerating Vision-Language-Action Model Integrated with Action Chunking via Parallel Decoding
Problem

Many VLA models predict a whole chunk of future actions at once for smoother control, but they still generate that chunk one token after another, so inference cost climbs linearly as the chunk grows. On a real manipulator that extra latency makes real-time execution impractical.

Approach

PD-VLA attacks the one-token-at-a-time bottleneck by reframing the decode itself. Instead of treating action generation as a strict left-to-right sequence, it recasts it as a nonlinear fixed-point system — a set of equations whose solution is the whole action chunk — and solves that system with parallel fixed-point iterations. In effect the model refines all the actions in the chunk simultaneously rather than waiting for each one to finish before starting the next. Crucially this is training-free: it changes how an existing model is run, not how it was trained, so no fine-tuning is needed. And because it operates at the decoding level, it stacks on top of other speedup tricks the model may already use.

Why it matters

It reaches 2.52x higher execution frequency with mathematical guarantees that the parallel-decoded actions match the original, proving you can make an already-trained VLA fast enough for real control without retraining it.

ROBOTICS'25Quart-Online: Latency-Free Multimodal Large Language Model for Quadruped Robot Learning
Problem

Putting a multimodal large language model in charge of a walking quadruped means the model has to emit control commands fast enough to keep the legs coordinated. But a big MLLM is slow, and its inference latency falls short of the controller frequency the robot demands, so it cannot run the loop in real time.

Approach

Quart-Online removes the latency at its source by shrinking what the model has to predict. Its core idea, Action Chunk Discretization, compresses continuous action sequences into a small, compact discrete vocabulary — so instead of laboriously producing continuous numbers, the model just picks tokens from a tiny action codebook. The MLLM is then fine-tuned to jointly encode vision, language, and these discretized actions in one shared representation, so understanding and acting live in the same model. Because each decision is now a cheap discrete choice, the system runs at a real-time 50 Hz. Notably, the discretization is designed so it does not degrade the underlying language model's quality.

Why it matters

It is presented as the first latency-free VLA for quadrupeds, showing that discretizing action chunks can buy real-time 50 Hz control without sacrificing the model's language competence.

5 · Split slow reasoning from fast control

A task like clear the kitchen table and put everything away stretches over dozens of steps, and asking a single end-to-end policy to both figure out the plan and move every joint is where most systems fall apart. So the field split the stack in two. A slow, heavy vision-language model reasons about what to do next: it breaks the goal into subgoals, writes little chunks of a plan, or picks the next target. A fast, lightweight controller then handles how to do it: it drives the motors moment to moment to hit that target. The genuinely hard part is not either half on its own but the interface between them — what exactly the slow planner hands down, and whether the fast controller can actually execute it. Get that handoff wrong and the planner proposes things the robot cannot physically do; get it right and you can chain thirty or fifty actions in a row. This is numerically the largest pattern in modern VLA work, and it is precisely where the end-to-end learned-policy family and the classical planner family meet and fuse.
ROBOTICS'25GHIL-Glue: Hierarchical Control with Filtered Subgoal Images
Problem
A popular way to build the two-layer stack is to let a generative image or video model dream up a picture of what the scene should look like a moment from now, then have a low-level policy steer toward that picture. The trouble is the dreamed-up images are often physically impossible or full of visual junk, and the controller dutifully chases them straight into failure. The weak link is not the planner or the controller but the seam where one hands its image to the other.
Approach
GHIL-Glue puts a filter right at that seam. The slow half is a generative model that produces candidate subgoal images; the fast half is a goal-conditioned imitation-learning policy that tries to reach whatever image it is given. Between them, GHIL-Glue inspects each proposed subgoal image and throws out the ones that would not actually move the task forward, passing only the useful ones down to the controller. It also trains the controller to be robust to the visual artifacts that generated images inevitably carry, so a slightly corrupted goal image no longer derails it. The interface, in other words, is a stream of images, and the contribution is a quality gate plus artifact-hardening on that stream.
Why it matters
By fixing the handoff rather than either model, it lifts performance 25% over prior generative-subgoal methods and reaches state of the art on CALVIN with a single RGB camera — showing the planner-controller interface, not the models, was the real bottleneck.
ROBOTICS'25SARO: Space-Aware Robot System for Terrain Crossing via Vision-Language Model
Problem
A quadruped crossing broken outdoor ground needs two very different skills at once: enough scene understanding to judge this is a gap I should jump, that is a slope I should climb, and enough low-level agility to actually keep its footing on each terrain type. Systems that do one well usually do the other badly.
Approach
SARO splits those skills across the two layers. The slow half is a vision-language model that looks at the scene, decomposes the crossing into sub-tasks, and monitors progress in a closed loop, re-deciding when a step succeeds or fails. The fast half is a set of reinforcement-learning locomotion policies, trained per terrain type via a Probability Annealing Selection scheme, that handle the actual footwork. The interface between them is the VLM naming a terrain-specific sub-task — effectively selecting which specialized locomotion skill the low-level controller should run — and then watching the outcome before choosing the next one. This turns high-level semantic reasoning into a sequence of concrete skill selections the RL controller can execute.
Why it matters
Because the VLM only has to pick the right skill and the RL policy only has to execute it, the same system generalizes across varied indoor and outdoor 3D terrains without per-environment tuning.
ROBOTICS'25Local Policies Enable Zero-Shot Long-Horizon Manipulation
Problem
Skills learned in simulation usually break in the real world because they are brittle to exactly where the robot and object happen to sit, and it is hard to build a simulator diverse enough to cover every long, multi-stage task. That fragility is what stops learned manipulation from chaining many steps together reliably.
Approach
ManipGen makes the fast half pose-invariant: it trains local policies in simulation that only care about the object right in front of them, not the robot's absolute position in the room, so each skill transfers to the real world without retraining. The slow half is a stack of foundation models for vision, language, and motion planning that reads the overall task and decides the sequence of skills to invoke. The interface is that the planner localizes the next subtask and hands the controller a fresh local frame — it moves the robot into the neighborhood of the object, then lets the pose-invariant local policy take over from there. Because each local skill starts from a normalized viewpoint, the same handful of policies compose into long, multi-stage tasks zero-shot. The high-level models never touch motor commands; they only stage the scene for the local controller.
Why it matters
This clean split lets the system run 50 real-world tasks zero-shot, beating SayCan, OpenVLA, and VoxPoser by 36-76% — evidence that making the low-level skill pose-invariant is what makes the planner-controller handoff robust.
ROBOTICS'25Guiding Long-Horizon Task and Motion Planning with Vision Language Models
Problem
For very long tasks you can use a VLM, which has broad commonsense but cannot guarantee a motion is geometrically feasible, or classical task-and-motion planning (TAMP), which guarantees feasibility but drowns in commonsense-free search over huge state spaces. Neither alone scales to a 30-to-50-action kitchen job.
Approach
VLM-TAMP fuses the two families directly. The slow half is a VLM that proposes semantically meaningful intermediate subgoals — sensible waypoints a human would recognize as progress. The fast half is a classical task-and-motion planner that tries to refine each subgoal into an actually executable, collision-free trajectory. The interface is a two-way contract: the VLM passes down a subgoal, and if the planner cannot find a feasible trajectory to reach it, that failure is passed back up and the VLM is re-queried to replan. This closed loop means the VLM supplies the commonsense structure while the planner enforces physical feasibility, each covering the other's blind spot.
Why it matters
This is the clearest case of the two VLA families literally fusing, and it pays off: 50-100% success on 30-to-50-action kitchen tasks where non-hierarchical baselines score 0%.

6 · The vision crossover: driving as a VLA

End-to-end self-driving is a vision-language-action model in disguise. A car takes in camera views, reasons about the scene, and outputs an ego trajectory — the path its own wheels should follow next. That is exactly the VLA loop: perceive, then act, except the action is a steering-and-throttle plan instead of a robot gripper. The vision community brings three enormous gifts to this problem: fleets of driving data measured in thousands of hours, perception systems that already read a busy street, and generative world models that can roll out an imagined future — a short video of what happens next — so the car can plan against a rehearsed prediction rather than blindly. What driving still lacks is the embodied half of a true VLA: real hardware and closed-loop physical action, where a chosen trajectory actually moves a two-ton vehicle and the world answers back. These four papers show the crossover in motion — mixtures of experts for perception and skills, visual chains of thought, and latent world models that imagine the road ahead to steer the plan.
CVPR'26DriveMoE: Mixture-of-Experts for Vision-Language-Action Model in End-to-End Autonomous Driving
Problem
A self-driving car sees the world through many cameras at once and must handle everything from routine lane-keeping to rare, sharp maneuvers. Feeding every camera into one network wastes effort on views that do not matter right now, and asking a single policy to cover every driving behavior spreads it too thin.
Approach
DriveMoE is a full vision-language-action model that outputs an ego trajectory, built on the π0 robot-action foundation model adapted for driving as Drive-π0. It adds two mixtures of experts. A Scene-Specialized Vision MoE uses a learned router to pick only the camera views relevant to the current context, cutting the redundant visual clutter before reasoning begins. A Skill-Specialized Action MoE then activates behavior-specific experts — separate specialists for lane following, obstacle avoidance, and aggressive maneuvers — so the model deliberates with the right skill for the moment. Perception (which views to trust) and action (which driving skill to apply) are thus both routed, producing the planned trajectory that steers the car.
Why it matters
This is driving as a VLA laid bare: perceive the scene, route to the right experts, and emit an ego trajectory that is the action. It shows the vision community importing a robotics action model (π0) wholesale, closing the loop on Bench2Drive where the plan is actually driven.
CVPR'26HybridDriveVLA: Vision-Language-Action Model with Visual CoT reasoning and ToT Evaluation for Autonomous Driving
Problem
Most driving VLA models translate the rich visual scene into words to reason, which throws away spatial detail and hurts precision. They also commit to a single waypoint path that silently bundles safety, progress, and comfort together, with no way to weigh those concerns separately — so the one trajectory can turn out unsafe or jerky in crowded, uncertain traffic.
Approach
HybridDriveVLA keeps reasoning inside the visual domain and imagines before it acts. A Visual Chain-of-Thought module generates the next scene — the road three seconds ahead — as an actual image built from discrete visual tokens, using the scene it anticipates as a goal-state to plan toward. Conditioned on that imagined future, a Tree-of-Thought Evaluation module proposes several candidate waypoint sequences and scores each waypoint on explicit safety, progress, and comfort. At inference it walks the tree like a reasoning-guided beam search, keeping the waypoints with the highest cumulative score to assemble the final ego trajectory. Built on a Qwen2-VL backbone with a MoVQGAN image tokenizer, it is trained to both predict the future frame and rank the multi-aspect plans.
Why it matters
Here the world model and the action are one system: the model literally paints the future it expects, then plans the trajectory against that rehearsed image. It is the human loop of look, imagine the next moment, weigh your options — turned into a driving VLA that outputs a scored ego path.
CVPR'26DriveLaW: Unifying Planning and Video Generation in a Latent Driving World
Problem
Driving world models often generate beautiful future video and plan trajectories in the same system, yet keep those two jobs as separate, decoupled tracks. The imagined future and the chosen action then drift apart, so the plan is not truly grounded in what the model forecasts will happen.
Approach
DriveLaW chains generation and planning through one shared latent space instead of running them side by side. DriveLaW-Video is a video generator — a spatiotemporal VAE feeding an efficient diffusion transformer — that predicts high-fidelity future road scenes and, crucially, produces expressive latent representations of them. DriveLaW-Act is a diffusion-based planner that reads those very latents to produce the ego trajectory, so the action is conditioned directly on the generator's internal picture of the future. A noise-reinjection step balances how much the latent compresses versus how faithfully it renders, and a three-stage curriculum learns long-horizon motion, then spatial refinement, then the latent-to-planner chaining. The paper shows these video-generator latents carry more coherent scene meaning than the BEV maps or language features planners usually rely on.
Why it matters
This is the purest form of world-models-feed-action: the plan is drawn straight from the latent of an imagined future video, guaranteeing the trajectory agrees with what the car predicts. It is the vision community's generative forecasting handed directly to the driving action head, setting a closed-loop planning record on NAVSIM.
CVPR'26Latent Chain-of-Thought World Modeling for End-to-End Autonomous Driving
Problem
Driving VLA models that reason in text struggle to capture spatial geometry and the give-and-take of many agents on the road, and generating that text word by word is slow and often misaligned with the actual steering action. Language is simply the wrong medium for reasoning about where a car should go.
Approach
This work (named LCDrive) moves the chain of thought out of words and into latent space. The model alternates between two kinds of internal tokens: action-proposal tokens that suggest a maneuver, and latent world model tokens that simulate the counterfactual future that maneuver would cause — imagining if I do this, the world becomes that — all without ever emitting text. By bouncing between proposing an action and rolling out its imagined consequence in latent form, it reasons about the road and settles on an ego trajectory. Training runs in three stages: a cold start using ground-truth world-model states, then learning the world-model prediction head, then reinforcement-learning fine-tuning with trajectory-level rewards. The result reasons faster than text chains of thought and gains the most from RL.
Why it matters
It fuses the reasoning, the world model, and the action into one latent loop: every thought is an imagined future the plan is checked against. This is driving as a VLA where the world model is not a separate video but the very substrate the car thinks and acts in.

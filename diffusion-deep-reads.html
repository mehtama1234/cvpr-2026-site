1 · Making pixels from meaning

The flagship use of diffusion is deceptively simple to state: you type a sentence, and a picture or a video appears that matches it. Underneath, the model is doing one thing over and over. It starts from a field of pure random noise and, guided by your words, nudges that noise a little closer to a real image at each step, undoing an imaginary corruption process until something coherent emerges. The hard part is doing this stably and at scale — sampling from the distribution of images that fit a caption without the picture drifting, tearing, or collapsing as you push to higher resolution, longer duration, or real-time speed. The papers here all attack that same core problem from different angles, and they converge on a shared toolkit: the transformer-based denoiser (the DiT) as the workhorse, and flow matching — a cleaner way to describe the straight-line path from noise to image — as the training recipe. That combination won because it scales predictably and stays stable where older methods wobbled.

CVPR'26UltraFlux: Data-Model Co-Design for High-quality Native 4K Text-to-Image Generation across Diverse Aspect Ratios
Problem

Diffusion models that make gorgeous 1K images fall apart when you ask them for native 4K across many shapes of frame. Three problems all bite at once: the way the model tracks pixel positions starts drifting and aliasing at large sizes, the compression step that squeezes images into a workable latent form throws away fine high-frequency detail, and the training signal itself becomes lopsided at 4K. On top of that, there was simply no large, well-labeled 4K dataset with a good spread of aspect ratios to learn from.

Approach

UltraFlux fixes the data and the model together. It first builds MultiAspect-4K-1M, a million real 4K images with controlled aspect-ratio coverage and rich metadata. Then it upgrades the denoising transformer on three fronts: a resolution- and shape-aware scheme for encoding pixel positions so the model does not lose track at 4K, a retrained compressor that reconstructs sharp detail better, and a reweighted training objective that pays special attention to the high-noise steps — the early part of the reverse process where the overall layout of the image is decided. A curriculum focuses supervision on exactly those coarse-to-fine steps.

Why it matters

This is the diffusion skeleton pushed to its limit: the same start-from-noise, denoise-step-by-step loop, but re-engineered so the early steps that set global structure and the late steps that carve detail both behave at 4K. The payoff is open-source 4K quality that matches a proprietary system.

CVPR'26PixelDiT: Pixel Diffusion Transformers for Image Generation
Problem

Almost every modern text-to-image model does its denoising not on real pixels but on a compressed latent code, because that is cheaper. But the compressor is lossy — it quietly strips out fine high-frequency detail — and it was trained separately, with goals that do not quite line up with what the generator needs. You end up with a bottleneck baked in before generation even starts.

Approach

PixelDiT throws out the separate compressor and runs the entire diffusion process directly on pixels, end to end. To make that affordable, it splits the transformer into two levels: a patch-level stage that reasons about the global scene and semantics, and a pixel-level stage that refines actual texture. Clever per-pixel modulation and a token-compaction trick keep the pixel-level work from exploding in cost. Because there is no separate compressor, the denoiser and the image live in the same space and can be optimized jointly.

Why it matters

It shows the diffusion skeleton does not need a middleman: you can start from noisy pixels and denoise straight back to clean pixels, preserving detail a compressor would have lost, and still hit near-best image quality.

CVPR'26StreamDiT: Real-Time Streaming Text-to-Video Generation
Problem

Text-to-video models normally produce a short clip all at once, after a long wait — useless for anything interactive. The transformer cost grows quadratically with how much video you process, so generating in real time as frames stream out was out of reach.

Approach

StreamDiT reshapes the reverse process into a moving buffer of frames that are each at a different stage of being denoised — the frame about to leave the buffer is nearly clean, the one just entering is nearly pure noise. As the buffer slides forward it emits a finished frame and admits a fresh noisy one, so video flows out continuously instead of in one batch. It mixes several schemes for assigning noise levels across the buffer to keep motion consistent, uses efficient windowed attention, and then distills the many-step denoiser down to just a handful of steps.

Why it matters

This turns the usual all-at-once diffusion loop into a conveyor belt: the same noise-to-image denoising, but staggered across time so a general video generator can run live at 16 frames per second on a single GPU.

CVPR'26LoL: Longer than Longer, Scaling Video Generation to Hour
Problem

When you generate video one chunk after another, small errors compound and the story drifts. A common fix is to anchor generation to a few stable sink frames — but this backfires into sink-collapse, where the video keeps snapping back to those anchor frames, causing abrupt scene resets and looping. That makes truly long, coherent video hard to produce.

Approach

LoL traces sink-collapse to a specific clash: the periodic math the model uses to encode frame positions lines up too neatly across the transformer's attention heads, so they all pile attention onto the same sink frames. The fix is training-free — it jitters the base frequency of that position encoding differently for each attention head, breaking the lockstep so the heads stop homogenizing onto the anchors. Paired with a streaming position scheme, adaptive noise, and a causal decoder, this lets generation continue indefinitely without the periodic reset.

Why it matters

It keeps the step-by-step diffusion generator running far past its usual horizon — a 1.3B model produces 12 hours of continuous video with little quality decay — by removing the structural glitch that was collapsing the reverse process.

CVPR'26FARMER: Flow AutoRegressive Transformer over Pixels
Problem

Modeling images one pixel at a time, in the way language models predict one word at a time, is appealing because it gives you exact likelihoods and clean scaling. But raw pixels form impossibly long, high-dimensional sequences, so directly predicting a probability for every pixel is intractable.

Approach

FARMER marries two ideas: a normalizing flow — an invertible transform that maps a messy image into a well-behaved latent sequence — and an autoregressive transformer that models that sequence. A self-supervised step sorts the flow's latent channels into informative and redundant groups, so the transformer only has to work hard on the parts that carry signal. It then distills the sampler to a single step and adds a resampling-based guidance trick to sharpen quality, all while retaining exact likelihoods and training straight from raw pixels.

Why it matters

It is a cousin of the diffusion generator: like diffusion it transforms a simple noise distribution into images through an invertible path, but frames that path as a flow you can also read off exact probabilities from, turning noise into pixels with a fully tractable model.

2 · Lifting a 2D prior into 3D

The trouble with generating 3D is that clean 3D training data is scarce. Nobody has scanned billions of objects and scenes the way the internet has photographed and filmed them. But a diffusion model trained on all those images and videos has already, in a quiet way, learned what objects look like from every angle — it knows how a chair turns, how a room recedes, how a moving thing deforms. The shared move in this theme is to reuse that flat 2D prior and lift it into 3D, rather than optimizing a fresh 3D shape from scratch for every new scene. Some papers keep the diffusion skeleton — undo noise step by step — but run it over 3D latents instead of pixels, so the thing being denoised is geometry. Others hijack a pretrained video diffusion model and bolt on a decoder that reads out 3D Gaussians or point maps in a single forward pass. Either way the goal is the same: escape the slow, per-scene optimization loop and let a generative prior supply the shape it already understands.

CVPR'26DIFF4SPLAT: Repurposing Video Diffusion Models for Dynamic Scene Generation
Problem

Building a moving 3D scene from a single photo has been stuck between two bad options. Multi-stage pipelines that stitch together separate tools are slow and fragile, often burning several GPU hours per scene. Fast feed-forward shortcuts, meanwhile, only ever produce a flat 2D video or a frozen static 3D scene — never geometry that actually moves.

Approach

The key idea is to repurpose a pretrained video diffusion model — a model that already knows how the world looks and moves over time — as the engine for 3D. On top of it sits a Video Latent Transformer that reads the diffusion model's internal 2D latent features and, in one forward pass, directly predicts a field of deformable 3D Gaussians: little blobs of geometry that carry both a shape and a motion. Because the video prior already encodes plausible movement, the network can hand back 4D structure (3D that changes through time) without any test-time optimization or cleanup afterward. It was trained on a large 4D dataset with real metric-scale geometry and motion labels, supervised jointly on photometric, geometric, and motion losses so the Gaussians agree with the pixels, the depth, and the flow all at once.

Why it matters

It turns hours of per-scene optimization into a single 30-second pass — about a 60x speedup — while matching or beating the slow methods, and it is the first framework to fuse generation and an explicit dynamic 3D representation in one shot.

CVPR'26Gen3R: 3D Scene Generation Meets Feed-Forward Reconstruction
Problem

Two kinds of models each solve half the problem. Feed-forward reconstructors like VGGT can recover accurate 3D geometry from a few views but cannot dream up new scenes. Diffusion models can generate endless diverse content but have no built-in sense of geometric consistency. The two live in incompatible latent spaces, so nobody could get controllable generation and faithful geometry from the same system.

Approach

Gen3R bridges them by drafting the reconstruction model into service as an encoder. It repurposes VGGT as an asymmetric geometry VAE: a small learned adapter pair squeezes VGGT's scene tokens down into compact geometry latents. Those geometry latents are then concatenated with ordinary appearance latents (from a standard image VAE) into one joint latent — side by side, geometry and looks in a single object. A pretrained video diffusion backbone (Wan2.1) is fine-tuned to denoise this joint latent, so it learns the combined distribution over how a scene appears and how it is shaped. A KL term pulls the geometry latent distribution to match the appearance one, so the video diffusion model — built for pixels — can handle the geometry channel with the same machinery. At inference it generates the joint latent from a text or image prompt, then one decoder reads out 3D point maps while the other reads out the RGB frames.

Why it matters

By turning a reconstruction network into the geometry half of a diffusion model, Gen3R gets scenes that are both freely generated and geometrically faithful — reaching state-of-the-art scene generation on RealEstate10K and DL3DV-10K.

CVPR'26MeshFlow: Efficient Artistic Mesh Generation via MeshVAE and Flow-based Diffusion Transformer
Problem

Artist-grade meshes are hard to generate two ways at once. The autoregressive approach spits out vertices one at a time, so cost grows quadratically with mesh size and coordinates must be chopped into discrete bins, adding quantization error. The alternative — implicit surfaces like SDFs — comes out either bloated with too many vertices or smoothed into mush, unusable for real-time apps or hand editing.

Approach

MeshFlow moves the diffusion into a compact continuous latent space so generation is fast and never touches a discretization grid. A MeshVAE first compresses a mesh — including its tricky connectivity — into a short list of continuous feature vectors, using contrastive learning to encode which vertices connect and vertex normals to fix edge ordering; this alone is 72x more compact than naive tokenizers. Then, instead of predicting vertices one after another, a Rectified Flow transformer runs the diffusion-style denoising process over that latent and generates all vertices and edges in parallel in a single sweep. Rectified Flow is the flow-matching cousin of diffusion — it learns a straight path from noise to data, which is why so few steps are needed.

Why it matters

Going discretization-free and fully parallel yields an 18x inference speedup over the fastest autoregressive mesh generators while raising accuracy — practical, editable meshes for games, VFX, and XR.

CVPR'26CUPID: Generative 3D Reconstruction via Joint Object and Pose Modeling
Problem

Reconstructing a 3D object from one photo means untangling two things at once: the object's own shape and texture in a neutral canonical frame, and the camera viewpoint it happened to be shot from. Pure generative models ignore the camera and produce shapes that look plausible but do not line up with the input view; pure reconstruction methods pin the camera to identity and cannot guess at hidden, occluded geometry. Nobody had modeled the full joint distribution over both the object and its pose from a single image.

Approach

CUPID makes pose part of what the generative model produces. It runs a two-stage Rectified Flow — the diffusion-family denoiser — over a voxel representation of the object built from aggregated DINOv2 features and compressed by a 3D VAE. The clever trick is a UV cube: the camera pose is over-parameterized as a 3D grid where each active voxel stores the 2D pixel coordinate it lands on under the true camera, so generating the pose becomes just generating a 3D object with view-dependent colors. In stage one the flow jointly generates the occupancy (which voxels are solid) and this UV cube, and a PnP solver reads the camera matrix straight out of the resulting 3D-to-2D correspondences. In stage two, now conditioned on that recovered pose, a second flow generates per-voxel appearance features and injects pixel-aligned features — sampled from the input image at each voxel's projected location — so the texture matches the photo instead of drifting. Random masking during training lets it hallucinate occluded parts, and the output decodes to Gaussian splats and meshes.

Why it matters

Fusing generation with recovered pose gives reconstructions that are both complete (it fills in what the camera never saw) and faithful — over 3 dB PSNR and 10% Chamfer gains — and it extends to whole scenes with no task-specific retraining.

3 · Editing and inverse problems

A diffusion model is trained to do one thing: take a noisy picture and guess the clean one behind it. That single skill turns out to solve two very different-looking jobs. The first is editing a real photo you already have. You cannot just start from noise, because you want to keep this face, this background — so you run the process in reverse to find the noise that would have produced your image (called inversion), then re-generate forward under a new instruction, changing only what the words ask for. The second is restoration, where a degraded measurement (blurred, cropped, or almost pure sensor noise) is consistent with countless clean originals — an ill-posed inverse problem. Here the diffusion model acts as a learned prior: at every step it nudges the guess toward what real images actually look like, so the answer both matches the measurement and looks natural — a principled posterior sample rather than a lucky guess. In both cases nothing new is trained; a pretrained denoiser is simply steered, once to preserve-then-alter, once to fill in the missing detail.

CVPR'26FlashIn: Fast and Accurate Image Inversion for Real-time Image Editing
Problem

To edit a real photo with a diffusion model, you first have to find the noise seed that reproduces it — a step called inversion — and only then can you re-generate it with a new instruction. The catch is that faithful inversion traditionally takes hundreds of slow refinement steps, so the tension is between reconstructing the original well and being fast enough for a person to edit interactively.

Approach

FlashIn reframes inversion as a fast optimization: it directly tunes the latent code so the model reconstructs the input image, but replaces the slow plain descent with accelerated updates using adaptive learning rates and momentum. It judges reconstruction with a mix of pixel-level and perceptual (feature-space) losses, so the recovered latent preserves what the eye notices, not just raw pixel values. It refines progressively and stops early once the reconstruction is good enough, and it reuses pre-computed features to cut repeated work. The result is an inversion that lands in a fraction of the usual steps while still faithfully capturing the source image, ready for editing.

Why it matters

It targets the exact bottleneck of the invert-then-regenerate recipe — quickly finding the noise that a pretrained denoiser would have turned into your photo — so real-image editing can happen at interactive speed.

CVPR'26Delta Rectified Flow Sampling for Text-to-Image Editing
Problem

Newer rectified-flow generators (like Stable Diffusion 3) move along near-straight paths for fast synthesis, but bending them to edit a real photo is tricky. Standard editing objectives over-smooth the result — structural detail washes out and untouched background regions drift — while inversion-based fixes add their own errors from approximating the reverse trajectory. The tension is applying the requested change without disturbing everything the instruction did not mention.

Approach

Delta Rectified Flow Sampling (DRFS) is inversion-free: instead of recovering the original noise, it compares two flow trajectories — one for the source image, one for the target edit — and subtracts their residuals. Because the same background drift appears in both, differencing cancels it and isolates only the edit signal. To align the two trajectories in time, DRFS adds a time-dependent shift term that rises and falls across the process, concentrating the edit in the middle timesteps where structure is decided, and it optimizes coarse-to-fine from large noise levels down to small. It runs on SD3 and SD3.5 with no architecture changes, and the authors show it cleanly generalizes prior methods — turning the shift off recovers DDS, and a specific setting recovers FlowEdit. On the PIE benchmark it delivers the best edited-region alignment while strongly preserving the background.

Why it matters

It shows editing does not always need a full inversion: by differencing the source and target flows of a pretrained model, it steers the denoiser to change only what the text asks and leave the rest untouched.

CVPR'26SliderEdit: Continuous Image Editing with Fine-Grained Instruction Control
Problem

Instruction-based editors apply each request at one fixed strength — an all-or-nothing edit. When a prompt bundles several instructions, users cannot dial one up a little and leave the others alone, so precise, gradual control over the intensity of an individual change is missing.

Approach

SliderEdit attaches small low-rank adapter matrices that act on the token embeddings tied to a specific instruction inside a multimodal diffusion transformer. It trains them with a Partial Prompt Suppression loss: the model learns to match what it would produce if a given instruction were removed versus present, which cleanly separates each instruction's effect from the others. Once trained, smoothly scaling an adapter's learned weights turns that instruction into a continuous slider, so its strength can be tuned from off to full. This works for single edits and for disentangled multi-instruction edits, and it drops into strong existing editors like FLUX-Kontext and Qwen-Image-Edit without retraining a separate model per attribute.

Why it matters

It adds a control dial on top of the same invert-and-regenerate editing loop — reusing a pretrained editor and modulating how forcefully its denoiser pushes toward each requested change.

CVPR'26UniLDiff: Unlocking the Power of Diffusion Priors for All-in-One Image Restoration
Problem

Real photos arrive damaged in many ways at once — rain, blur, haze, low light — and often several at the same time. Recovering a clean image from such a degraded one is ill-posed: many clean scenes could explain what the sensor recorded. Prior diffusion-based restorers lean on a single global text prompt that lacks spatial specificity, so they lose fine detail and struggle when degradations are mixed.

Approach

UniLDiff is one latent-diffusion framework meant to handle all these degradation types together. Its Degradation-Aware Feature Fusion injects features from the low-quality input into the denoiser at every step and adaptively modulates them, so the restoration stays anchored to the actual damaged image as it cleans up rather than hallucinating freely. Its Detail-Aware Expert Module sits in the decoder and routes different regions to specialized experts for targeted texture and structure recovery, restoring fine detail that global-prompt methods blur away. Because degradations are inferred implicitly rather than labeled in advance, the same model adapts to compound and even unseen real-world corruptions, reaching state-of-the-art across five restoration tasks.

Why it matters

It uses a pretrained diffusion model as the prior that supplies plausible clean detail for an ill-posed recovery, while step-wise fusion keeps that generated detail faithful to the degraded measurement.

CVPR'26PnP-CM: Consistency Models as Plug-and-Play Priors for Inverse Problems
Problem

Diffusion-based solvers for inverse problems — deblurring, inpainting, super-resolution, MRI — produce excellent reconstructions but need hundreds of network evaluations, too slow for large or time-sensitive jobs. Faster consistency-model alternatives either must be retrained for each specific measurement operator or rely on guidance that becomes unstable for hard operators and cannot handle nonlinear ones. The goal is a single fast solver that works across many degradations without retraining.

Approach

PnP-CM treats a consistency model — which can jump from a noisy state to a clean estimate in just one to four evaluations — as the prior step inside the classic plug-and-play ADMM optimizer. ADMM alternates two concerns: a data-fidelity update that enforces agreement with the actual measurement (solved exactly for linear operators, iteratively for nonlinear ones), and a prior update that would normally require an intractable denoising operator but is here replaced by a single consistency-model evaluation at a chosen noise level. Because the prior and the measurement are decoupled, the same trained model serves deblurring, inpainting, phase retrieval, and more, with the algorithm's convergence guarantees intact. To stay stable in the ultra-few-step regime, PnP-CM adds calibrated noise injection and heavy-ball momentum, and the authors even train a consistency model from scratch on fastMRI — the first use of consistency models for MRI — reaching high quality in about four evaluations.

Why it matters

It cleanly splits the two halves of every inverse problem — match the measurement, look like a real image — and hands the second half to a reusable diffusion-style prior that runs in a handful of steps instead of hundreds.

CVPR'26gQIR: Generative Quanta Image Reconstruction
Problem

Single-photon (SPAD) sensors record ultra-fast binary frames — each pixel simply saw a photon or did not — and at very low light and high frame rates almost every frame is nearly pure noise. Turning such a starved, near-random burst into a clean image is an extreme inverse problem, because the measured data barely constrains what the true scene was. Earlier discriminative methods struggle precisely because they have no generative sense of what real images look like.

Approach

gQIR brings a latent-diffusion prior — a model that has learned rich natural-image statistics — to fill in what the photons cannot. First it trains a Quanta-Aligned VAE whose encoder is pulled, via a latent-alignment loss, to place noisy quanta into the same latent space a pretrained natural-image VAE uses, with a deterministic encoding path so the encoder does not collapse on near-binary input. Then it adapts a latent diffusion U-Net (via LoRA) conditioned on those quanta latents, trained with an adversarial discriminator to sharpen texture and counter diffusion blur. Finally, FusionViT — a small pseudo-3D vision transformer — fuses many quanta frames in latent space, treating time as a depth axis so it can pool evidence across the burst while keeping spatial structure. The pipeline reaches 30.8 dB PSNR on the I2-2000fps benchmark, over 2 dB above the best prior method.

Why it matters

When the measurement is almost all noise, the diffusion prior supplies the missing detail from learned image statistics — a principled generative reconstruction rather than an impossible pixel-by-pixel guess.

4 · The generator as a perception engine

Here is the most provocative idea in the field. A model that learns to paint any image from noise has, along the way, learned what images fundamentally are — how surfaces bend away from the light, where one object ends and another begins, how a street recedes into the distance. Those rich features, absorbed from millions of pictures, quietly encode both geometry and meaning. So instead of building a separate specialist to measure depth or trace object boundaries, people began asking the generator itself to do the seeing. The trick is to run perception as generation: give the model a photo and let it paint the answer — a depth map, a segmentation mask, a set of boxes — the same way it would paint any other image. The sharpest form of this reframe is stranger still: you can hand a diffusion model a hard geometry problem and let it solve it purely by denoising a picture of the solution. In every case the generator is being repurposed as a universal prior over what plausible visual structure looks like.

CVPR'26UniPercept: A Unified Diffusion Model for Generalizable Visual Perception
Problem

Vision has long been split into separate trades: one model classifies, another detects objects, a third carves an image into regions. Each is its own specialist, trained and tuned in isolation, and attempts to fold them into a single network tend to generalize poorly once tasks start to differ.

Approach

UniPercept turns the whole problem into generation. It keeps one diffusion model and simply tells it, per input, which job to do — a task-specific condition steers the usual noise-to-image process toward the answer that task wants. Ask for classification and it paints a class prototype; ask for detection and it paints bounding boxes; ask for segmentation and it paints masks. Nothing about the core machinery changes between tasks — only the conditioning that points the generator at a different kind of output. The same probabilistic engine that would dream up an image is repurposed to draw the label.

5 · Denoising a robot into action

Here is the quiet problem at the center of robot control: for one situation, there is often more than one good thing to do. To get around an obstacle you can go left or go right; both are fine, the average of them drives you straight into it. A normal policy that is trained to predict the correct action learns to output the mathematical mean of everything it saw, so it blends the two good modes into one invalid middle. This is why so many learned robots look hesitant and freeze in the gap. A diffusion policy sidesteps this entirely: instead of predicting one answer, it starts from pure noise and denoises it into a single, coherent action, trajectory, or grasp, and if you sample again it happily commits to the other good mode. It is the exact same denoiser that turns noise into a picture, only the thing being drawn is a motion. And once you are denoising, you can steer the denoiser mid-way with the gradient of a cost or a constraint, so physics, collision avoidance, and stability become guidance terms rather than something you bolt on afterward. Two papers here push the idea to its striking conclusion: sampling-based optimal control and diffusion sampling are, up to an annealing schedule, the same procedure, which means the field's workhorse controller was a diffusion model all along.

ROBOTICS'25The Ingredients for Robotic Diffusion Transformers

ROBOTICS'25Diffusion-Based Approximate MPC: Fast and Consistent Imitation of Multi-Modal Action Distributions
Problem

Model predictive control (MPC) computes an excellent action by solving an optimization problem live, but that solve is far too slow for high-rate control, so people try to imitate it with a fast neural network. The trouble is that MPC's answer is genuinely multimodal, obstacles and local optima mean there are several equally-good solutions, and a network trained with ordinary regression averages those valid solutions into one invalid blend that steers the robot into the very obstacle it was avoiding.

Approach

Instead of regressing to one action, they train a diffusion model to reproduce the full distribution of MPC solutions, so all the good modes survive. During denoising they add gradient guidance that keeps the robot committed to a single mode from one timestep to the next, so it does not flip-flop between left and right in closed loop. They also sample several candidate trajectories in parallel and pick the one that scores best against the actual cost and constraints, which is exactly the guidance-as-cost idea made concrete. Problem

6 · The race for fewer steps

A diffusion model draws a picture by starting from pure noise and cleaning it up a little at a time, one small step after another. The catch is that these steps are sequential — each one waits on the one before it — and a full run can take dozens or even hundreds of them, which is why diffusion is powerful but slow. But researchers keep measuring the same striking fact: most of the quality lands in the first handful of steps, and the long tail of remaining steps only polishes details you can barely see. That gap between where the quality actually is and how long we keep sampling is the opening everyone is racing through. There are three ways to attack it: distill a slow many-step teacher into a fast few-step or one-step student; straighten the path the model walks so it can take giant strides instead of tiny ones (flow matching and rectified flow); or build a smarter solver that integrates the underlying equation more accurately per step. Robotics feels this most acutely — a policy that generates actions has to keep up with a real control loop, so a model that is a fraction of a second too slow simply cannot drive the robot.

CVPR'26MeanFlow Transformers with Representation Autoencoders
Problem

The dream is a generator that makes a full-quality image in a single step instead of many. MeanFlow already does this by learning an average velocity field, but paying for it is brutal — it runs in a big full-resolution latent space and needs hundreds of GPU-days to train, so one-step generation stays out of reach for most people.

Approach

The idea is to shrink the space the model works in and reach one-step generation through a careful warm-up. Instead of the usual bulky latent space, it uses a Representation Autoencoder that compresses a 256x256 image into a much more compact learned code, cutting the compute per forward pass. Because bolting that compression straight onto MeanFlow makes training blow up, they use a three-stage curriculum: first train an ordinary flow-matching model for a stable start, then warm it toward mean-velocity behavior with a trajectory-consistency objective, then finish with full mean-flow distillation for true single-step sampling. A cheap finite-difference trick approximates the mean velocity so they never have to compute an expensive full Jacobian.

Why it matters

It reaches a 1-step FID of 2.03 on ImageNet 256x256 (versus 3.43 for vanilla MeanFlow) while using 38% fewer GFLOPS and 83% less training cost — the strongest evidence yet that top-tier images can come out of a single denoising step.

CVPR'26Phased DMD: Few-step Distribution Matching Distillation via Score Matching within Subintervals

CVPR'26GeoRK2: Geometry-Guided Runge-Kutta Integration for Diffusion Transformer Acceleration
Problem

Sampling from a diffusion transformer is really just numerically solving an equation step by step, and taking fewer, bigger steps to go faster normally distorts the intermediate features and wrecks the image. Standard fast solvers assume the feature space is flat and use plain Euclidean math — but those features actually live on a curved surface, so the shortcuts drift off course.

Approach

Rather than distilling or retraining anything, this builds a better ODE solver that respects that curvature. It treats sampling as following the probability-flow equation along a curved feature manifold and uses a manifold-aware second-order Runge-Kutta predictor (a scheme that takes a trial step, checks it, and corrects — more accurate than a naive single estimate). A metric-preconditioned corrector, built from the features own covariance, keeps each stride pointed the right way, and adaptive stabilization stops the trajectory from drifting apart under aggressive step schedules. Efficient low-rank updates keep the extra geometry cheap.

Why it matters

It delivers 4-5x faster inference on DiT, FLUX.1-dev, and HunyuanVideo with almost no quality loss (delta FID about 0.81), holding up at high speed-ups where other fast solvers fall apart — a purely inference-time win needing no new training.

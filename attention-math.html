Attention — why it works, mathematically

A model often needs to pull together information that sits in many different places — the words of a sentence, the patches of an image. This is how it decides, for each place, which of the others to read from, in a way it can get better at by trial and error.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

Attention asks a plain question: from all the pieces in front of me, which ones should I read right now? It gives each piece a weight, then blends the useful pieces together. The model is not forced to use a fixed local window; it can look where the evidence is.

Where topology and shape enter

In topology and geometry, attention helps compare parts of a shape that are far apart in the raw list but close in meaning. A handle on a mug, two sides of a loop, or matching landmarks across two surfaces may need long-range comparison. Attention gives the model a way to connect those parts.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

It appears in language, images, video, graphs, retrieval, robotics, and recommender systems. Anywhere a system must search a large set of facts and pull out the relevant ones, attention is the basic move.

Why Attention is worth learning

It matters because modern models often fail or succeed based on what they look at. Attention turns reading into a learned operation instead of a hand-written rule.

The key turn — the principle at work

The stuck point. A word, image patch, or object slot cannot be understood only from itself. Its meaning often sits somewhere else: a pronoun needs its noun, a pixel needs a matching edge, and a robot command needs the scene part it refers to.

The mathematical move. The principle is differentiable retrieval. Instead of making a hard lookup, the model gives every possible source a match score, turns those scores into shares that add to one, and reads a weighted blend. The softmax is the mathematical hinge: it turns choice into something smooth enough to learn.

What changes. The model stops carrying fixed messages forward. Each item can ask, at that layer, which other items matter now. The price is the square-law comparison cost: if every item can look at every other item, the number of comparisons grows as n times n.

The problem, as a quantity

Read this sentence: 'The cup fell off the shelf because it was pushed.' To handle the word 'it', a model has to look back through the sentence and work out that 'it' stands for the cup and not the shelf. The clue it needs is somewhere else in the sentence, and nothing marks in advance where.

This is the situation attention is built for, and it comes up everywhere: the items can be words, or patches of an image, or points in a 3D scan — each one stored as a list of numbers. For one item at a time, the model has to gather information from whichever other items are relevant to it, and leave the rest alone. Two things make that hard.

First, 'find the one relevant item and copy it' is a hard yes-or-no choice. But a model learns only by making tiny adjustments that lower its mistakes, and a step that suddenly jumps from picking one item to picking another gives it no direction to adjust in. So a hard pick cannot be learned; the model needs a soft version that still, in the end, leans almost all the way toward the right item.

Second, to know which items are relevant to which, every item has to be compared against every other item. With n items that is n×n comparisons — a cost that grows with the square of how many items there are, and turns into the wall you hit on a big image or a long piece of text.

The core ideas — and why they work

Turning match scores into shares, so the choice can be learned

weights_j = exp(q · k_j / √d) / Σᵢ exp(q · k_i / √d), output = Σⱼ weights_j · v_j
Start by giving every pair of items a match score. The item doing the looking carries a list of numbers called its query, written q. Every item that might be looked at carries its own list, called a key, written k_j. Multiply the two lists together position by position and add up the results — that single number is the match score, and it comes out large exactly when the two lists agree, meaning this key is the sort of thing the query is looking for. (The division by √d is only a size adjustment, explained in the next idea; it changes nothing about which scores are larger.)

Now, for the one item doing the looking, take its match scores against all the keys and turn them into shares: numbers that are all positive and add up to exactly 1. That is all the formula does — raising to the power exp makes every score positive, and dividing by the sum of them all makes the whole set add up to 1. The share weights_j says what fraction of its reading this item draws from item j. Because the shares for one item must add up to 1, whatever weight one item gains, another must lose — the items are made to compete.

Finally, the reading itself. Each item also carries a third list, its value v_j, and the output is the shares-weighted blend of all the values — mostly the value of whichever item won the largest share, plus a little of the others. Because it is always a blend of values the model already has, the result can never fly off to something out of range.

Why this particular turn-scores-into-shares step and not some other? Because it is the one that keeps the weights positive, makes them add up to 1, and stays smooth — a tiny change in a match score makes only a tiny change in the shares. That smoothness is exactly what lets the model keep improving the query, key and value lists by small nudges, instead of being stuck with a hard pick it has no way to adjust.

Why the scores get divided by √d

numerator: exp(q · k_j / √d), denominator: Σᵢ exp(q · k_i / √d), √d is the variance of random dot products in dimension d
The query and key are long lists — say d numbers each. Their match score is a sum of d separate products, and when the lists hold unrelated values, a sum of d such things tends to grow about as fast as the square root of d. So the longer the lists, the larger the raw match scores come out in plain size.

That causes trouble at the next step. Feed large numbers into the positive-making exp step and one of them runs away from the rest: after turning into shares, a single item takes almost the entire weight — 0.999… — and every other item is left with almost nothing. A share pinned that close to 1 or 0 barely moves when you nudge its match score, so the model loses the very direction-to-adjust it needs — the same dead end as a hard pick.

The fix is to shrink every match score by dividing by √d, which is roughly the size those scores tend to reach in the first place. Now the scores sit back in a tame range, the shares stay soft and responsive, and the model can still learn fine distinctions even when the lists are long. The scaling is not a tuning knob you fiddle with; it is set to cancel out exactly how fast the scores grow with list length.

The order of the items does not matter — which is both a gift and a catch

if rows of Q, K, V are permuted identically, rows of output are permuted the same way, the computation Q·Kᵀ is a <em>set operation</em>, not a sequence operation
Attention never asks 'which item came first?'. All it ever computes is, for each item, how well its query matches every other item's key — and the match between two lists is the same number no matter where in the row the two items happened to sit. So if you shuffle the items into a different order, the outputs come out shuffled the same way, but each individual reading is unchanged. Put another way, attention treats its input as a set — a bag of items with no built-in order — rather than as an ordered line of items.

This is a genuinely two-sided fact. It is exactly right for things that truly have no natural order, like patches spread across an image or points in a 3D scan, where insisting on an order would just be making something up. But for things where the order carries meaning — the words of a sentence, the frames of a video — it is a catch: attention on its own cannot tell 'the cup fell on the shelf' from 'the shelf fell on the cup', because both hold the same items. So order has to be added back in on purpose, by tagging each item with an extra number-list that encodes its position before attention ever runs. The matching stays order-blind; the sense of position is bolted on separately.

Why comparing everything to everything costs so much

QKᵀ costs O(n² · d) to compute (n² pairs, each a d-dimensional dot product), then softmax + weighted sum → O(n² · d) total
To fill in every match score you have to compare every query against every key. With n items that is an n-by-n grid of scores, and each score is the position-by-position multiply-and-add of two length-d lists, costing about d steps. Multiply it out and building the grid costs roughly n × n × d operations — the cost grows with the square of the number of items. On a high-resolution image cut into 4,096 patches that is already tens of millions of operations in a single layer; on video, where items pile up with every frame, it grows faster still.

This square-law cost is not a separate flaw you can patch away — it is the direct price of the whole idea, which is to let every item look up every other by its content. So every method that makes attention cheaper gives up a slice of that completeness in one of three ways: only score pairs of items that are already near each other, first summarise many items down to a few and score those, or approximate the full grid with a shortcut that never writes it out in full. Each one buys speed by loosening the every-item-sees-every-item guarantee.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Every version of attention is the same machine with three dials.

Dial one — how much you compare. The plain version scores every pair. Sparse versions score only the pairs that are near each other or specially chosen, cutting the n×n cost down to n times some small fixed number. Grouping versions first bundle items into a few groups and compare inside each group, so the full grid is never built.

Dial two — how you turn scores into shares. The plain version uses the exact turn-scores-into-shares step described above. Faster versions replace it with an approximate rule that can be split into independent pieces and computed without ever forming the full grid — giving up some exactness for a large speed-up.

Dial three — how position gets added. Since the matching is order-blind, a sense of where each item sits is fed in separately: as a fixed pattern of waves tagged onto each item, as a rotation applied to the query and key, or as a small bias added to nearby pairs' scores. A related setting lets the queries come from one input while the keys and values come from another — a picture reading from a caption, a robot reading from its camera — but the pick-and-blend step is exactly the same. The common thread never changes: score how well things match, turn the scores into competing shares, and blend by those shares.

A worked numeric example

Say one item's match scores against three others come out [2, 1, 0]. Turning those into shares — all positive, adding up to 1 — gives about [0.665, 0.245, 0.090], so the reading is drawn mostly from the first item but still takes a real slice from the other two. Now raise that first score to 4 and the shares become about [0.936, 0.047, 0.017]: the reading leans almost entirely on the first item, yet the shares still shift smoothly rather than snapping to a hard all-or-nothing pick — which is exactly what lets the model keep nudging them.

3Telling attention where things are
position embedding as a rotation or coordinate system: before QK dot product, apply learnable per-head rotations (SVD-parameterized), scale-aware projections, or spherical coordinates (r, θ, φ) so that q and k encode their spatial/temporal location in the dot product
Because the matching ignores order, two items at different places produce the same match score on their own — the mechanism is blind to where anything sits. These methods fix that by folding each item's location into its query and key before the two are matched, by rotating the number-lists or writing them in coordinates that carry position. Now the match score itself rises or falls with how far apart two items are, so nearer or farther items naturally win larger or smaller shares, without any separate position machinery bolted on afterward.
These four fold each item's location into its number-lists before any matching happens, since the matching itself is blind to order. 5Fusing senses and reading other agents

The blending step naturally combines items that are already in the same form. To combine different kinds of input — a camera, a touch sensor, spoken words, a laser scan — each is first turned into its own set of items, and then the queries come from one kind while the keys and values come from another, so an item from the camera can read from the words or the touch sensor. Some methods swap the matching for a learned gate that asks a sharper question — does this stream actually influence that one? — but the underlying move is the same: blend across the boundary between senses by weights the model learns.

The through-line. Attention replaces a hand-built rule for combining nearby things with a learned lookup by content: for each item, ask which other items match it, and blend their readings by how strongly they match. Turning the match scores into shares is what makes that choice learnable, since a hard pick gives the model no direction to improve in. Dividing by √d keeps the shares soft enough to keep sending that direction even when the lists are long. And the fact that the matching ignores order shows the core is really about content, not position — so position has to be wired in on the side.

Two plain ideas carry the whole thing: turning scores into shares so a choice can be learned by small nudges, and blending values by those shares so the output is always something the model can trust. The one unavoidable cost — comparing everything to everything — is the price of letting any item reach any other by content, and every fast variant is just a different way of paying less of it.

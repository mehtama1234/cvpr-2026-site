1 · Aligning generation with reward: preference optimization for images and video

Generative models produce outputs trained on data, not optimized for how good an image or video actually is. Reinforcement learning changes this: instead of labeling which output is correct, a reward signal scores quality (style fidelity, detail preservation, temporal coherence) and the model learns through trial-and-error to maximize that score. The danger is reward hacking—gaming the reward without actually improving the thing you care about. This theme collects four papers that align text-to-image and text-to-video diffusion models with human preferences by designing reward models for specific qualities and using preference optimization to train the generator without labels.
CVPR'26PaCo-RL: Advancing Reinforcement Learning for Consistent Image Generation with Pairwise Reward Modeling
Problem
Generating multiple images of the same character or scene consistently is hard: large-scale training data is scarce, consistency requires both visual fidelity and subjective alignment, and existing reward models score only aesthetics or text alignment, not cross-image consistency.
Approach
PaCo-RL trains a consistency reward model by comparing pairs of images and scoring how similar they are in style and identity. It then uses this reward to guide a diffusion model via GRPO (Group Reward Policy Optimization), optimizing at low resolution first to cut computational cost, then applying the learned policy at full resolution.
Why it matters
Rather than hand-label thousands of image sets as consistent or inconsistent, the model learns from a reward signal measuring pairwise consistency. The reward hacking risk here is optimizing similarity without preserving quality—the paper mitigates this by aggregating multiple reward aspects and using efficient resolution-decoupled training.
CVPR'26Style-GRPO: Semantic-Aware Preference Optimization for Image Style
Problem
When transferring artistic style to an image, semantic content often gets destroyed. Existing methods lack a way to reward both style transfer and content preservation at the same time, creating conflicting objectives that manual tuning cannot solve.
Approach
Style-GRPO trains two reward models—one scoring how well the style was transferred, another scoring how much content was preserved—then uses Group Reward Policy Optimization to balance them during model training. The model learns a policy that maximizes both rewards simultaneously.
Why it matters
Instead of asking humans to label which style transfer is best, the method defines two reward signals and lets RL discover the trade-off. Reward hacking here means optimizing one reward (style) at the cost of the other (content); the dual-reward formulation keeps the model honest.
CVPR'26GDPO-SR: Group Direct Preference Optimization for One-Step Generative Image Super-Resolution
Problem
One-step super-resolution models (single denoising pass) struggle to balance fidelity (photorealism) against perceptual quality (texture and fine details). Previous preference optimization methods either need many paired samples offline or ignore local texture variations critical to perceived quality.
Approach
GDPO-SR designs an attribute-aware reward function that dynamically scores a super-resolved image by examining smooth vs. textured regions separately. It combines Direct Preference Optimization (precision on hand-labeled pairs) with Group Reward Policy Optimization (efficient group-level learning), then optimizes the diffusion model's policy to maximize this localized reward.
Why it matters
Instead of rating entire images with a single score, the reward tracks fine details in specific regions. RL learns a generator that maximizes perceived detail quality without relying on human labels for every image pair, though the risk is that the reward might optimize texture noise instead of meaningful detail.
CVPR'26Mind the Generative Details: Direct Localized Detail Preference Optimization for Video Diffusion Models
Problem
Text-to-video models produce videos with flickering, inconsistent motion, and unrealistic details. Global video-level preference scoring misses region-specific artifacts and produces conflicting signals across quality dimensions; multi-sample ranking for preference pairs is computationally expensive.
Approach
LocalDPO creates preference pairs efficiently by taking a real video as positive and a partially corrupted version (with random spatio-temporal regions degraded) as negative, then learns localized preferences only in the corrupted regions via a region-aware DPO loss. This avoids expensive multi-sample ranking while providing consistent reward signals tied to specific artifacts.
Why it matters
The reward signal is spatial and temporal—score the model higher when it avoids artifacts where they actually appear rather than rating the whole video globally. This precise reward hacking guard (rewarding detail quality in specific regions, not globally) aligns the model with human perception of video defects.

2 · Reasoning by reward: GRPO for multimodal LLMs

Large vision-language models learn best when optimized for reward signals instead of supervised labels, especially for perception and reasoning tasks. GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) applies reinforcement learning to VLLMs by shaping model outputs through task-specific rewards—clinical semantics in medicine, perceptual accuracy in detection, grounded evidence in reasoning. The papers here show three critical insights: (1) medical tasks need cross-dataset reward normalization to balance heterogeneous objectives, (2) perception-oriented GRPO differs fundamentally from reasoning-oriented GRPO requiring different exploration and feedback strategies, (3) visual reasoning improves when rewards anchor to verifiable evidence, and (4) video understanding becomes data-efficient by combining multiple reasoning perspectives through RL. Together they demonstrate that reward is a more flexible teaching signal than labels for steering multimodal behavior.
CVPR'26MedGRPO: Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning for Heterogeneous Medical Video Understanding
Problem
Vision-language models struggle with medical video understanding because spatial precision, temporal reasoning, and clinical semantics demand rewards different from natural images. Heterogeneous medical datasets have imbalanced reward distributions across tasks, destabilizing RL training.
Approach
MedGRPO normalizes cross-dataset rewards by mapping each dataset's median to a common value, ensuring balanced optimization. A medical LLM judge provides fine-grained reward feedback on five clinical dimensions (accuracy, completeness, reasoning, safety, clarity), teaching the model to reason like a clinician rather than memorize labels.
Why it matters
Reward from expert clinical evaluation shapes behavior toward diagnostic reasoning, not just surface pattern matching. Trial and error under clinical rewards builds robustness across heterogeneous medical tasks better than fixed supervised labels.
CVPR'26Dr. Seg: Revisiting GRPO Training for Visual Large Language Models through Perception-Oriented Reward
Problem
GRPO training developed for language reasoning assumes it transfers to visual perception tasks like detection and segmentation. But perception needs broader output exploration and finer-grained reward feedback than reasoning, requiring task-specific design.
Approach
Dr. Seg proposes Look-to-Confirm strategy that encourages models to show their reasoning focus before confirming spatial decisions, broadening search space. Distribution-Ranked Reward provides adaptive feedback by tracking performance variation across samples, robust to metric scale differences between detection and counting tasks.
Why it matters
Perception-oriented reward functions teach the model to explore diverse visual hypotheses and adapt confidence based on difficulty. Reward optimization discovered that visual tasks require fundamentally different RL dynamics than language reasoning.
CVPR'26Perceptual-Evidence Anchored Reinforced Learning for Multimodal Reasoning
Problem
Multimodal reasoning requires grounding abstract conclusions in verifiable perceptual evidence from images and text. Supervised learning lacks explicit mechanisms to maintain links between reasoning steps and their visual grounding.
Approach
PEARL uses RL where rewards are anchored to verifiable perceptual evidence from images and text, not abstract correctness. Maintains explicit attention and evidence links throughout the reasoning chain, making the model earn reward only when reasoning is traceable back to visual support.
Why it matters
Reward tied to evidence forces the model to learn that good reasoning is grounded reasoning. Trial and error toward evidence-backed reward builds interpretability and robustness by teaching causal chains, not just correlations.
CVPR'26Incentivizing Versatile Video Reasoning in MLLMs via Data-Efficient Reinforcement Learning
Problem
Multimodal LLMs struggle with long, complex video understanding, overlooking details and hallucinating. Prior RL approaches require massive datasets (165k cold-start plus 260k RL data) to train robust video reasoners.
Approach
VideoReasoner uses multi-task RL with only 3k cold-start and 5k RL data. Combines event reasoning, keyframe reasoning, and direct-response perspectives, with rewards that encourage the model to dynamically sample the best reasoning path per query. Rewards incentivize versatile reasoning by penalizing hallucinations and rewarding visual grounding.
Why it matters
Reward signals for multi-perspective reasoning teach the model which reasoning mode to use for each query type, outperforming single-path supervision. Data-efficient RL shows that reward can be more sample-efficient than labels when shaped to encourage exploration across reasoning modes.

3 · Designing the reward: reward models, curricula, and the hacking problem

Reinforcement learning's promise rests on one fragile assumption: the reward signal tells the truth. Yet in practice, reward design is the hardest part. Coarse-grained rewards miss the behavioral nuances that matter; process rewards are ambiguous across thousands of tokens; trajectory clustering wastes compute on meaningless samples; and RL agents will exploit any loophole in the objective. This theme tackles the central challenge: how to design, attribute, and explore rewards so they steer learning toward genuine capability rather than gaming shortcuts.
CVPR'26FinPercep-RM: A Fine-grained Reward Model and Co-evolutionary Curriculum for RL-based Learning
Problem
RL agents fail on complex tasks because reward signals are coarse-grained and exploration is inefficient, missing the fine-grained behavioral nuances that precision control demands.
Approach
Proposes a fine-grained reward model that captures detailed action quality metrics alongside a co-evolutionary curriculum where task difficulty adapts in lockstep with agent capability, enabling tighter feedback loops.
Why it matters
Coarse rewards are lossy—they collapse rich behavior into a scalar, leaving the agent to guess which aspects of its action led to success. Fine-grained modeling closes that gap, letting the curriculum meet the agent where it is rather than forcing one fixed difficulty path.
CVPR'26Expand and Prune: Maximizing Trajectory Diversity for Effective GRPO in Generative Models
Problem
GRPO training for image generation suffers from reward clustering: many sampled trajectories cluster near the group mean reward, producing near-zero advantage signals and wasting computation on uninformative samples.
Approach
Introduces Optimal Variance Filtering to select high-variance reward subsets, then proposes Pro-GRPO which prunes trajectories in-process at intermediate denoising checkpoints using proxy rewards from single-step ODE jumps, shrinking unnecessary branches early.
Why it matters
Not all trajectories deserve equal compute. By identifying and terminating low-variance (low-signal) samples early, the method decouples exploration breadth from optimization cost—you can afford wider exploration because bad paths exit before wasting steps.
CVPR'26Unlocking Token Rewards via Training-Free Reward Attribution
Problem
Process rewards offer step-level feedback but remain ambiguous across hundreds of tokens per step, while outcome rewards are too coarse to guide token-level RL, forcing a choice between costly training of new token models or fidelity-losing heuristics.
Approach
P2T decomposes coarse process rewards into token-level rewards using gradient-based attribution via first-order Taylor approximation, efficiently attributing reward to each token as the change in process score if that token were replaced with a null token.
Why it matters
Credit assignment is the heartbeat of learning. Without knowing which tokens earned the reward, the agent can't learn which behaviors to reinforce. Attribution bridges the gap between process and token, giving fine-grained signal without expensive retraining.
CVPR'26Are We Ready for RL in Text-to-3D Generation? A Progressive Investigation
Problem
Extending RL from text and 2D to text-to-3D autoregressive generation is largely unexplored, yet 3D generation is far more sensitive to both reward design and RL algorithm choices due to global geometric consistency constraints.
Approach
Conducts systematic study of reward models, GRPO variants, and training scaling for 3D generation. Proposes Hi-GRPO optimizing hierarchical generation via global-to-local refinement and AR3D-R1 using reasoning to guide token-level 3D synthesis, introducing MME-3DR benchmark for measuring implicit reasoning.
Why it matters
3D generation has higher stakes: poor rewards compound across spatial dimensions, breaking coherence. By grounding rewards in reasoning and tuning RL per the 3D structure (global geometry first, local detail after), the method aligns the reward signal to the inherent hierarchy of the task.

4 · Reward for perception: RL where the goal is not differentiable

Reinforcement learning excels when the objective cannot be directly optimized through gradient descent. In computer vision, this arises when the goal is a non-differentiable property: temporal coherence of tracked keypoints, selection of task-critical frames from long video, or discovering controllable skills from raw visual observation. These four papers show how RL trades supervision labels for structured reward signals that target perception goals unreachable by supervised learning. The reward directly optimizes what matters—consistency across frames, information density, end-to-end task utility—making RL the natural fit when perception quality is defined by outcomes, not labels.
CVPR'26From Pairs to Sequences: Track-Aware Policy Gradients for Keypoint Detection
Problem
Video keypoint detection treats frames independently, breaking temporal coherence and losing keypoint identity across the sequence. Supervised per-frame methods cannot enforce that the same keypoint remains consistent across time.
Approach
Uses reinforcement learning with track-aware rewards that measure temporal consistency. Policy gradient optimization directly maximizes keypoint coherence across frame sequences rather than predicting isolated detections. The reward function encodes tracking smoothness—a non-differentiable objective—as a numeric signal the policy learns to maximize.
Why it matters
Supervised loss functions optimize frame-by-frame accuracy, but enforcing temporal identity is a discrete, sequence-level property. RL sidesteps this by rewarding the agent when it maintains consistent tracks, letting the policy learn to balance detection and consistency without labeled sequences.
CVPR'26Efficient Frame Selection for Long Video Understanding via Reinforcement Learning
Problem
Multimodal LLMs use uniform frame sampling due to context limits, missing sparse critical moments in long videos. Finding which frames matter for the downstream task is a combinatorial selection problem with no gradient path.
Approach
Two-stage training: supervised pretraining learns a relevance prior from CLIP, then hierarchical RL fine-tuning optimizes frame selection directly for task performance. The reward measures end-task utility—how well downstream LLM reasoning succeeds with selected frames. An adaptive monitoring mechanism prevents policy drift during optimization.
Why it matters
Which frames help the LLM succeed cannot be predicted from the frame alone; only the full task defines the reward. RL optimizes this non-differentiable objective by trying frame combinations and measuring downstream task quality, something supervised learning cannot directly encode.
CVPR'26EVA: Efficient Reinforcement Learning for End-to-End Video Agent
Problem
End-to-end RL agents for video suffer from sample inefficiency and computational cost. Frame-level processing creates bottlenecks, and the agent must learn to choose actions based on complex temporal patterns.
Approach
Hierarchical two-level RL framework: temporal abstraction layer processes video in chunks, spatial reasoning layer operates on compressed representations. Contrastive learning shapes state representations, and temporal consistency stabilizes training. The reward signal drives the full pipeline from video observation to action.
Why it matters
Video-to-action mapping is inherently non-differentiable—no supervised label defines the right action for a video moment. RL provides the reward signal (task success or failure), and hierarchical abstraction makes this reward-driven learning tractable by reducing state complexity.
CVPR'26Saliency-Guided Representation with Consistency Policy Learning for Visual Unsupervised Reinforcement Learning
Problem
Unsupervised RL from visual observations must discover useful behaviors with no task labels. Successor representations struggle in high-dimensional pixel spaces, focusing on dynamics-irrelevant regions instead of features that enable control.
Approach
SRCP decouples representation learning from the SR objective using saliency-guided dynamics learning. A consistency policy model learns skill-conditioned action distributions via a denoising formulation, with classifier-free guidance balancing diversity and controllability. The reward signal comes from skill discovery metrics, not external labels.
Why it matters
No supervised signal defines what makes a skill useful or what features matter for control. RL discovers structure by rewarding agents that learn diverse, controllable behaviors from unlabeled video, a goal entirely outside the reach of supervised learning on raw pixels.

5 · Learning to move: RL for legged locomotion and agile control

Reinforcement learning is where robots learn to move, from the wobbling first steps of a quadruped on rough terrain to the precise reach of a manipulator handling unknown objects. The core insight is ancient: reward a behavior, repeat it, refine it. A robot gathers trial-and-error trajectories in simulation or on hardware, learns which actions lead to goal states through a learned reward signal, and gradually builds a policy that generalizes to conditions it never saw during training. Whether learning to swim through thruster noise, to carry a shifting load without falling, or to manipulate objects it has never touched before, modern RL distills complex dynamics into policies that feel rather than predict—using force feedback, state inference, and adaptation. These four papers map the frontier: goal learning from video, sim-to-real through randomization and impedance, adaptation under load, and the power of compact state signals over raw images.
ROBOTICS'25On-Robot Reinforcement Learning with Goal-Contrastive Rewards
Problem
Teaching a robot by trial-and-error on real hardware takes too many attempts because the robot gets almost no feedback signal telling it whether it is doing well.
Approach
They train a reward signal from plain videos of a task being done—no robot actions recorded, just video. Two training signals are used: one that rewards the robot for moving toward the goal state, and one that learns to tell successful endings from failed ones using goal-contrastive rewards.
Why it matters
A richer reward signal means the robot gets useful feedback much more often, so it learns faster and succeeds on more tasks. Reward-based learning turns sparse trial-and-error into dense guidance.
ROBOTICS'25Learning to Swim: Reinforcement Learning for 6-DOF Control of Thruster-Driven Autonomous Underwater Vehicles
Problem
Underwater vehicle thrusters are hard to control because water forces are nonlinear and change with payload, so hand-tuned PID controllers need re-tuning for each configuration and react slowly.
Approach
They train a reinforcement learning policy in a fast parallel underwater physics simulator with randomized conditions, then deploy it directly on a real AUV with no extra tuning.
Why it matters
Training across many randomized conditions in simulation makes the policy flexible enough that it works on the real vehicle without adjustment. The reward signal drives the policy to learn robust control despite unmodeled dynamics.
ROBOTICS'25Beyond Robustness: Learning Unknown Dynamic Load Adaptation for Quadruped Locomotion on Rough Terrain
Problem
A four-legged robot carrying an unknown shifting load on rough ground tends to stumble because it cannot easily tell how the load is moving.
Approach
A model that tracks how a carried load moves is built in and fed into a reinforcement learning walking policy. The policy learns to read that load state from the robot's own joint sensors alone, then adjust its gait to keep the load from tipping.
Why it matters
Explicitly modeling load dynamics gives the walking policy enough information to compensate for shifts before they cause a fall. Reward-driven adaptation lets the robot learn compensation without extra sensors by inferring hidden state from its own proprioception.
ROBOTICS'25Watch Less, Feel More: Sim-to-Real RL for Generalizable Articulated Object Manipulation via Motion Adaptation and Impedance Control
Problem
Reinforcement learning policies trained in simulation to open doors or drawers fail on real objects because raw camera or point cloud inputs differ too much between simulation and reality.
Approach
Instead of raw images, the policy uses compact state signals from off-the-shelf perception modules. It tracks how the object has moved over recent steps to infer its properties, and uses impedance control, which adjusts force based on resistance felt, in both training and deployment.
Why it matters
Compact state inputs change less between simulation and reality than raw images do, and force-sensitive control lets the robot feel and adjust to real-world friction and stiffness. Trial-and-error learning optimizes what the robot can sense and feel, not what it sees.

6 · Reward at scale: RL for multi-robot coordination, exploration, and assembly

Reinforcement learning scales to multi-agent systems when reward signals coordinate exploration, learning, and refinement across many robots. Whether robots divide labor to avoid redundant mapping, learn from shared experience to refine precise skills, or discover swarm leaders through active probing, RL-driven policies adapt to collective needs. The challenge is shaping rewards and curricula so that individual agents optimize for group objectives at scale, from frontier selection and communication bandwidth to task success under hardware variation.
ROBOTICS'25Reinforcement Learning Driven Multi-Robot Exploration via Explicit Communication and Density-Based Frontier Search
Problem
When multiple robots explore an unknown space independently, they often scan the same areas repeatedly because they do not know what their teammates have already covered, wasting time and bandwidth.
Approach
Each robot runs an RL policy that picks which unexplored boundary to visit next, guided by a path-planning algorithm (A*) and a compressed map it shares with teammates so everyone knows what has already been seen. The reward shapes frontier selection to avoid redundant coverage.
Why it matters
Sharing compressed maps lets each robot avoid areas teammates have already checked, cutting redundant coverage without needing much communication bandwidth. RL coordinates multi-robot exploration by learning which frontiers to prioritize.
ROBOTICS'25From Imitation to Refinement - Residual Rl for Precise Assembly
Problem
A robot trained by copying human demonstrations can plateau and fail at fine-grained assembly tasks because it replays fixed action chunks without correcting mistakes as they happen.
Approach
Residual RL keeps the original imitation-learned policy frozen and adds a second, lighter policy trained with reinforcement learning that watches what is happening and makes small corrections during execution. Reward signals the gap between intended and observed states.
Why it matters
The base policy handles the broad plan while the residual policy closes the loop on errors in real time, recovering the precision that pure imitation replay loses. RL refines learned skills to human-level accuracy on assembly.
ROBOTICS'25DemoStart: Demonstration-Led Auto-Curriculum Applied to Sim-to-Real with Multi-Fingered Robots
Problem
Teaching a three-fingered robot hand complex tasks through trial-and-error in simulation takes an enormous number of attempts because success is rare when starting from random positions.
Approach
Training begins from positions taken from a small set of simulation demonstrations, gradually widening the range of starting positions as the robot improves (auto-curriculum), and adds random variation so the policy transfers to real hardware. Reward sculpts the curriculum.
Why it matters
Starting near states the robot already knows how to handle means it gets useful feedback right away, learning 100 times faster than without demonstrations. RL with shaped curriculum dramatically reduces sample complexity and enables sim-to-real transfer.
ROBOTICS'25Learning Adversarial Policies for Swarm Leader Identification Using a Probing Agent
Problem
Finding which robot is the leader of a swarm is hard when you can only watch where the robots are relative to each other and do not know how the swarm behaves.
Approach
A separate robot called a prober learns through trial and error (using PPO) to push into the swarm in ways that expose the leader, then reads the swarms reaction to figure out which robot is in charge. Reward signals successful leader identification.
Why it matters
Actively disturbing the swarm and watching who the others follow produces much clearer evidence of the leader than passive observation alone. RL enables one agent to learn how to probe a multi-agent system and extract information.

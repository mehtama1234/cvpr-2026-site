What a LoRA actually is

The mixture page treats one LoRA as a solved building block and moves on to combining many. This page opens that block — what the two matrices are, why a tiny low-rank update can carry a whole skill, the three knobs that define one, and the merge trick that makes it free at inference — with every structural claim checked against a real LoRA we trained on a CPU.

1 · The object, and the bet

A transformer is, mostly, a stack of big weight matrices. Fine-tuning one for a new task means changing those matrices: W → W + ΔW. Full fine-tuning stores a whole update ΔW the same size as W — for a Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct model that is hundreds of millions of new numbers per task. That is the waste. LoRA's entire premise is a single bet about the shape of ΔW:

The bet: the update you need is low-rank. A high-dimensional matrix whose columns mostly repeat the same few directions has low rank — it can be written as a tall skinny matrix times a short wide one. LoRA never forms ΔW directly. It stores the two thin factors and multiplies them on the fly: ΔW = B·A.
ΔW = B × A
(out×in)  =  (out×r)  ·  (r×in)   ·   store 2·d·r numbers, not d·d

If W is d×d, storing ΔW costs d² numbers. Storing A (r×d) and B (d×r) costs 2dr. When r ≪ d — say r=8 against d=896 — that is a hundred-fold saving, per matrix, per task. The forward pass never builds the big ΔW either: it computes ΔW·x = B·(A·x) right-to-left, projecting the input down to r dimensions with A and back up with B.

y = W·x  +  (α/r)·B·(A·x)    # frozen path + the low-rank adapter path

2 · Why the bet is not crazy — intrinsic dimension

Why should a task's update be low-rank at all? Because fine-tuning barely moves the model. A pre-trained network already contains the machinery; adapting it to a downstream task is a small reorientation, not a rebuild. This was measured before LoRA existed — Li et al. (2018) found deep networks can be trained in a random subspace of a few hundred dimensions, and Aghajanyan et al. (2020) showed the same for fine-tuning language models: the update lives in a tiny intrinsic dimension. LoRA simply builds that constraint into the parameterization — it forces the update to be low-rank instead of hoping it turns out that way.

We can watch it happen in our own trained adapter. The two matrices A and B are the whole story: A is a sensor that reads r features out of the 896-dim input; B is a writer that adds r new directions back into the output. r is the width of the bottleneck — the only place the skill can live.

Every number below is measured, not asserted. We trained one honest LoRA (rank 8, α 16, on q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, o_proj) on the frozen Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct — 896-dim, 24 layers — on a laptop CPU, on the arithmetic task. Loss fell 0.6941 → 0.0049 over 96 steps. The rest of this page reads the trained weights back out.

The parameter accounting

Full fine-tuning would train every one of the base's 494,032,768 parameters. Our LoRA trains 1,081,344 — that is 0.219% of the model, a 457× reduction. Same downstream skill, a rounding error of new weights.

And the update is a gentle shove: ‖ΔW‖ is around 0.9% of the frozen weight's own norm. LoRA does not overwrite the model; it tilts it.

Two facts you can check in one line each

  • B starts at zero. At init, A is random (7,168 non-zeros) but B is all zeros (true), so ΔW = B·A is exactly 0 (measured ‖ΔW‖@init = 0.0e+00). Training therefore begins at the unchanged frozen model — no random shock to recover from.
  • The merge is lossless. We checked that (W+ΔW)x equals Wx + (α/r)·B(Ax) to floating-point tolerance: true. So you can fold ΔW back into W and pay zero extra latency at inference.

3 · The three knobs that define a LoRA

Once you accept ΔW = (α/r)·B·A, a LoRA is fully specified by three choices — and each is a real decision, not a default to ignore.

KnobWhat it controlsThe intuition
rank rthe width of the bottleneck = capacitybigger r can express more, but risks re-learning noise and costs more. The effective rank we measured is smaller than r anyway — a reason modest r usually suffices.
α (scaling α/r)how loud the adapter speaks vs the frozen paththe update is scaled by α/r so you can change r without re-tuning strength. (At high r the α/r factor over-shrinks — rsLoRA fixes it with α/√r.)
init (A random, B=0)where training startsB=0 ⇒ ΔW=0 at step 0, so the model starts exactly as the frozen base and drifts away smoothly — no random-init shock. (We measured ‖ΔW‖@init = 0 above.)

And a fourth, spatial, choice: where you attach them. target_modules picks which matrices get an adapter. The original paper found adapting just the attention q and v projections is often enough; adapting all linear layers (including the FFN) buys a little more skill for more parameters. It is a coverage-vs-cost dial — the same tension the rank-as-expert build pushes to its limit by routing within a single adapter's ranks.

4 · The free lunch: merge

Because the adapter path is linear and parallel to the frozen path, you can add it back in:

W' = W + (α/r)·B·A    # one matrix again — the adapter has vanished into the weights

After merging, the adapted model is byte-for-byte a normal model — no extra matrices, no extra FLOPs, no added latency. (We verified the merge is lossless above.) This is what separates LoRA from earlier adapter methods that inserted new layers and paid for them at every inference forever. The flip side: once merged, the adapter is no longer a separable object — and that separability is exactly what mixture-of-LoRA needs, which is why routed systems keep the adapters unmerged and pay a little runtime to switch between them.

5 · The family tree — one idea, seven refinements

Every popular LoRA variant is a first-principles tweak to one of the pieces above — the base, the scaling, the decomposition, the init, the optimizer, the sharing, or the rank budget:

QLoRAQuantize the frozen base to 4-bit (NF4) and train the LoRA on top of it. The base is where all the memory is, so squeezing it — while keeping the adapter in full precision — is what lets a 65B model fine-tune on a single 48GB GPU. LoRA makes the adapter cheap; QLoRA makes the base cheap to hold.
rsLoRAScale the update by α/√r instead of α/r. At large rank the plain α/r factor shrinks the effective learning signal and stalls training; the √r correction keeps gradients well-scaled, so bigger ranks actually help instead of quietly hurting.
DoRASplit each weight into magnitude and direction, and let LoRA adapt only the direction while a separate scalar learns the magnitude. This mirrors how full fine-tuning actually moves weights and closes most of the remaining gap to full FT — for the same parameter budget.
PiSSA · LoftQStop starting from zero. Initialize A and B from the top singular directions of W itself (PiSSA), or to pre-cancel quantization error (LoftQ). A warm, informed start converges faster and lands better than the B=0 cold start.
LoRA+Give A and B different learning rates (B much higher). Because the two matrices sit at different scales, one shared LR is a compromise; decoupling them measurably speeds convergence at no extra cost.
VeRA · Tied-LoRAFreeze a single pair of random A, B shared across all layers, and train only tiny per-layer scaling vectors. Trades a little capacity for another 10× cut in trainable parameters — useful when you serve thousands of adapters.
AdaLoRADon't spend the same rank everywhere. Prune the singular directions that aren't earning their keep and reallocate that rank budget to the layers that need it — adaptive rank instead of a flat r.
And then the branch this whole site is about. Every method above still trains one adapter. The moment you keep many LoRAs and add something that decides, per input, how much of each to use, you have left LoRA-the-module and entered mixture-of-LoRA — the same low-rank object, now an expert in a routed committee. That is the next page, with three hands-on builds; and the full field is laid out on the design-space map.

Backpropagation — why it works, mathematically

A network makes an answer wrong, and to fix it you would need to know how much each of a billion internal numbers contributed to the mistake. Checking them one at a time is hopeless. This is the trick that computes all billion blame-shares in a single backward sweep — almost for free.

The problem, as a quantity

A network turns an input into an answer by pushing it through a long chain of small numeric operations, each governed by adjustable numbers — often billions of them. When the answer comes out wrong, learning means nudging each of those numbers in the direction that would have made the answer a little better.

But that requires knowing, for every single one of the billion numbers, how much it contributed to the error — its share of the blame. The brute-force way is unthinkable: wiggle one number, re-run the whole network, see how the error changed, then repeat a billion times. That is a billion full network-runs for a single learning step. The problem is to get every number's blame-share at once, cheaply enough that training is actually possible — and to understand why, done naively, that blame can fade to nothing or blow up as it travels through a deep network.

The core ideas — and why they work

Blame flows backward as a product of local sensitivities

∂L/∂w = (∂L/∂y)·(∂y/∂w) — chain rule: multiply how each step reacts
Start at the end, with the error, and ask how much one number w deep inside is to blame for it. The number does not touch the error directly — it affects the next operation's output, which affects the one after that, on down the chain to the final answer.

The chain rule says the blame passes along that chain by multiplication: how much the final error reacts to w equals how much the error reacts to the next step's output, times how much that output reacts to w. Each operation contributes one simple local factor — how sensitively its output moves when its input is nudged — and you multiply these factors together along the path from w to the error. So the whole problem reduces to knowing each operation's own little sensitivity and chaining them. Blame is not measured by re-running the network; it is computed by multiplying local reactions back down the chain.

One forward sweep to remember, one backward sweep to hand out blame

δ_l = (W_{l+1}ᵀ · δ_{l+1}) ⊙ f'(z_l), ∂L/∂W_l = δ_l · h_{l-1}ᵀ
Because the blame at each layer is built from the blame at the layer just after it, you compute it in one clean pass from the end backward. First run the network forward as usual, remembering what each layer output. Then start at the error and walk backward: the blame landing on a layer is the blame from the next layer, passed back through that layer's own numbers and scaled by how sensitively the layer reacted.

Each layer, once it knows its own blame-share δ_l, can immediately work out the blame on every one of its adjustable numbers. So a single backward sweep, layer by layer, distributes blame to all billion numbers at once — the earlier layers simply reuse the blame already computed for the later ones, rather than starting from scratch. Forward to remember, backward to assign: two sweeps, and every number knows how to move.

It costs about the same as running the network forward

cost(backward) ≈ cost(forward), not O(#params) separate probes
This is the quiet miracle that makes deep learning possible at all. The naive blame calculation — wiggle each number and re-run — costs one full network-run per number, so billions of runs per learning step, which no computer could sustain.

The backward sweep instead gets every number's blame in a single pass whose cost is roughly the same as one forward run of the network. The reason is the reuse from the last idea: the blame computed for a later layer is exactly what the earlier layer needs, so nothing is recomputed. One forward pass and one backward pass — about twice the cost of just answering — and you have the blame for all billion numbers. Without this, training a large network would take longer than anyone could wait; with it, each learning step is cheap.

Why blame fades or explodes through many layers

∂L/∂h_0 = ∏_{l=1}^{L} J_l ⇒ ‖·‖ → 0 or ∞ as L grows
The very mechanism that makes backprop work — multiplying a local factor at each layer — carries a built-in hazard. To reach the earliest layers of a deep network, the blame must be multiplied through every layer in between.

Multiplying many numbers together is unstable. If the per-layer factors are mostly a bit less than one, their product shrinks toward zero, and the earliest layers receive almost no blame — they barely learn, as if disconnected. If the factors are mostly a bit more than one, the product explodes, and the blame arriving at the early layers is so huge it wrecks training. This is why very deep plain networks are hard to train, and it is the reason several other ideas exist — keeping the per-layer signals well-scaled, or adding shortcuts that let blame skip past the long product — all aimed at keeping this chain of multiplications from collapsing or blowing up.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Everything trainable rests on this one procedure, with a few dials.

What the local factors are. Every operation a network uses — multiply, add, a bending nonlinearity, attention — comes with a simple rule for its own local sensitivity, and backprop just chains them. How the blame chain is kept healthy. Keeping per-layer signals well-scaled (normalization), adding shortcuts so blame can skip the long product (residual connections), and choosing bending functions whose local factor does not vanish. How much is remembered. The forward pass must store its outputs for the backward pass; when memory is tight, some are recomputed on the way back instead. Where it runs. The same backward sweep powers training of every network on this site. The thread never changes: blame is computed by multiplying local reactions backward, not by re-running the network, which is what makes learning affordable.

The through-line. The core problem is assigning a share of the blame for an error to each of a billion internal numbers, cheaply enough to actually train. The chain rule says blame flows backward as a product of each operation's own local sensitivity; running that as a single backward sweep — reusing each layer's blame for the layer before it — gets all billion shares for about the cost of one extra forward pass. The same multiply-through-every-layer structure is also the hazard, since a long product can fade to nothing or explode. Two plain ideas carry it: blame is multiplied backward through local reactions rather than measured by re-running, and that same chain of multiplications must be kept from collapsing or blowing up across depth.

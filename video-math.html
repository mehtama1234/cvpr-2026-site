Video / arrow of time — why it works, mathematically

A video is not a stack of frames — it is a probability distribution that has been factored along time, and the arrow of time is exactly the quantity you can read only when the factors lose information in one direction.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

Video adds time. The model must connect frames, remember what moved, and predict what may happen next. A frame is not just an image; it is one moment in a changing world.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology helps track connected things through time. A body, road, fluid front, or cell boundary should keep an identity even as it moves, bends, splits, or disappears behind another object.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

It appears in surveillance, sports, medicine, robotics, film, weather, and science.

Why Video / arrow of time is worth learning

It matters because many facts only exist across time: cause, motion, intent, and change.

The problem, as a quantity

Stack T frames and you have a massive joint distribution p(x₁, x₂, …, x_T) over all possible sequences of those frame images. The hard part is that this joint is impossible to store or work with directly — the number of possible frame sequences explodes as T grows, so you cannot write down a table for it or pull samples from it. The standard fix is the chain rule: break the joint into a chain of smaller, simpler distributions. But this fix immediately creates a second, deeper problem: in which direction do you break it? Forward — predicting the next frame from all the frames before it — and backward — predicting the current frame from all the frames after it — are mathematically identical ways to break the same joint. Yet in the real world the two are not equivalent: real processes dissipate energy, so predicting forward (what comes next?) is fundamentally different from predicting backward (what was before?). That asymmetry is the arrow of time, and using it cheaply on long videos — where the past fades and the future is open — is the entire challenge.

The core ideas — and why they work

Factorise the joint by the chain rule

p(x₁,…,x_T) = ∏_{t=1}^{T} p(x_t | x_1,…,x_{t-1}) — exact, no approximation
The chain rule is a mathematical fact, not a guess or a shortcut. It says you can always write the probability of a sequence as a chain of predictions: the probability of frame 1 times the probability of frame 2 given frame 1, times the probability of frame 3 given frames 1 and 2, and so on. Each step — predicting one new frame from everything that came before — is a manageable problem you can teach to a neural network. The full sequence is intractable; each single step is learnable. The catch is that to predict step t, the model must remember and compress all prior frames into something finite — that compression is the hard part, and it is what attention and recurrent layers are built to do. Same primitive elsewhere: this is the exact factorisation move that also powers autoregressive language models and learned world models — the trick is that an intractable joint becomes learnable when you condition on a growing history.

The arrow lives in irreversibility, not in the frames

KL(p_fwd ∥ p_bwd) > 0 iff the dynamics are dissipative; KL = 0 ⟺ p(x_{t+1}|x_t) = p(x_t|x_{t+1}) (reversible)
No single frame shows an arrow — a photo of smoke tells you nothing about whether time moves forward or backward. The arrow is a property of the whole sequence, specifically whether predicting the next frame is the same problem as predicting the previous frame. When a process is reversible (a ball rolling, a pendulum swinging without friction), the forward and backward problems are identical — the gap between them is zero — and a classifier trained to read the direction will guess at 50–50 chance. This is correct, not a failure. When a process is dissipative (friction, water mixing, heat spreading), going forward is a compression — information drains into unmeasured places — while going backward becomes more constrained and specific, often impossible. The gap is now positive and grows with how much energy is lost. A classifier can exploit this gap: it is learning to detect information loss, which is exactly what entropy growth measures in physics. In practice: a model trained on reversible clips (a ball rolling) learns nothing about temporal order; the same model on dissipative clips (a splash, a car crash) separates cleanly. The enemy it defeats: the naive 'bag of frames' that ignores order — without the arrow, shattering glass and reassembling glass are the same.

Temporal redundancy as a compression bottleneck

I(x_t ; x_{t+1}) ≈ H(x_t) − H(x_{t+1}|x_t), H(x_{t+1}|x_t) ≪ H(x_{t+1}) for video
Adjacent frames in video are nearly identical: most pixels do not change between frames at 24–60 fps. The entropy — the information — in the next frame given the current frame is tiny compared to the entropy of the next frame all by itself; most of what you need is already on screen. This is the redundancy that video compression (MPEG, H.265) exploits by storing only differences, and it is what makes predicting the next frame a powerful self-supervised training signal: easy for nearby frames (low entropy), hard for distant ones (entropy grows). The compression bottleneck is useful because it forces the model to separate static content (the background, the furniture) from dynamic content (what is actually moving). A model that does not compress the easy part will waste neural capacity on the sky and miss the hand. This is the same information bottleneck idea that makes masked autoencoders work — throw away the easy-to-predict parts, concentrate capacity on the hard-to-predict remainder. Same primitive elsewhere: the idea that compression and low-dimensional signal go together is the same move as LoRA (keeping only the important directions) and vector quantization (a small dictionary covers the useful content) — in every case, compression is not a loss but a discovery that the signal has a simple structure.

Long-horizon memory and the drift of uncertainty

H(x_{t+H} | x_t) → H(x_{t+H}) as H → ∞, i.e. the past eventually tells you nothing about the far future
The entropy of the frame far in the future, given only the current frame, grows as the horizon extends and asymptotes to the entropy of that frame in isolation — the current frame becomes less and less useful. In dissipative systems this happens faster because energy is lost at each step; in reversible systems it is set by how scrambled the dynamics become. For video models this is a concrete constraint: a model that only looks at the last few frames will make sharp short-range predictions but will drift badly over seconds, because each prediction's remaining error compounds. This is why long-video models need ways to compress long-range context — attention over key-frames, recurrent memory that forgets unhelpful stretches, hierarchical representations — rather than simply extending the window. The arrow of time makes the asymmetry clear: if time were reversible, the past and future would decay in symmetric fuzziness from the present; because it is not, the past is written and the future is open.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Every method in this theme answers two questions forced by the chain-rule breakdown: how do you compress the history, and how do you handle growing uncertainty as you predict further ahead? Transformer-based video models answer the first by paying attention to all prior frames at once, treating each frame as a row of tokens; they produce the best quality but cost quadratically in sequence length. Recurrent video models (Mamba-style) answer it with a fixed-size memory updated at each step — they trade some quality for linear-time processing. Video diffusion models factor generation differently — they clean up noise in a frame conditioned on prior frames, using the chain-rule structure as a guide for temporal consistency rather than for next-frame prediction. Temporal self-supervision (learning to sort shuffled clips, to classify forward vs backward, to predict what is missing) turns the arrow itself into a free training signal: the correct order is given without annotation, so any model that can sort shuffled clips or discriminate forward from backward has had to learn the underlying dynamics. The honest limit across all approaches is that the arrow is learnable only where dynamics dissipate — on truly reversible motion, no amount of data or model capacity will beat random guessing at temporal direction.

A worked numeric example

With frames x_1,x_2,x_3, a next-frame model factors a clip as p(x_1)p(x_2|x_1)p(x_3|x_1,x_2). If each step is 95% reliable, ten chained steps are only 0.95^10≈0.60 reliable. Time gives prediction power, but rollout multiplies error.

1What happened, and when
Frame annotation a_t ∈ {1..T}: embed reasoning with explicit timestamp; redundancy reduction via selective attention weight α_t to high-info frames
Irreversibility means the order of events matters, not just what is in them. By labeling each frame with its position in time before the model processes it, the model can reason about causality: Frame 5 has the pouring, Frame 6 has the spreading. Without these labels, the model treats frames as a jumble and loses the arrow. Attention concentration (spending more weight on frames where change is densest) means compute goes where it matters — where the arrow accelerates.
These papers embed time directly into reasoning by marking frame positions and selectively amplifying frames where action is densest. They address the core challenge: without explicit timestamps and selective focus, a model loses the arrow and treats a shuffled video the same as the original.

2What happens next
Predictive mixture p(x_{t+1:T} | x_{1:t}) = Σ_k π_k N(μ_k, Σ_k): decompose future into K modes, weight by feasibility; horizon split t_near vs t_far
The future is inherently uncertain and multi-branching — an object near a wall can go many ways. Rather than collapse to a single prediction, mixture models let each plausible path evolve independently. Splitting the horizon into near and far respects that nearby physics is deterministic (momentum carries the object), while distant physics is ambiguous (will it collide?). This mirrors the core idea: uncertainty grows with prediction distance, so predict confidently nearby, explore multiple paths far ahead.
These papers predict multiple plausible futures by representing them as discrete modes (branches) in a mixture, or by splitting the horizon into near-term (deterministic) and far-term (ambiguous) regions. They respect the core insight: entropy grows with distance, so confidence should concentrate nearby and spread far ahead.

3Stop the flicker, remember the hour

Semantic boundaries are turning points where the action shifts sharply — worth keeping in full resolution.
These papers maintain visual stability by keeping recent frames in full detail, compressing mid-range frames using signal-processing techniques, and marking inflection points where motion changes sharply. They solve the practical problem: how to remember a long video without storing every pixel.

4Learn from the arrow, spend compute where time is dense

5Predicting the future so a robot can act

Robot control needs to know which agents affect which — the causal graph of the scene. Causal gating blocks predictions about irrelevant objects before expensive computation. Latent auto-regressive models compress the state space — each new state comes only from the prior state and action, not from future, enforcing irreversibility.
These papers predict trajectories and world states that respect causal structure: what actions affect what, what the goal is, and how the scene evolves. They combine causal discovery, goal conditioning, and latent world models to make prediction useful for control.

The through-line. A video is one enormous joint distribution over frames, made workable by the chain rule: break it into a chain of next-frame predictions and the intractable joint becomes learnable. The arrow of time is not ornamental — it is the mathematical mark of dissipation, the gap between forward and backward conditionals, and it is exactly what temporal self-supervision detects when it asks the model to sort shuffled frames or classify direction. Redundancy between adjacent frames means most of the information is compressible (the background does not move), so the model's job is to learn what is hidden beneath the redundancy: the rare, high-entropy moments where something actually changes. Long horizons are hard because uncertainty compounds — the chain-rule breakdown trades a single impossible joint for a long chain of small uncertainties that multiply. The underlying moves — factorise by chain rule, compress the redundant, read the direction from dissipation — are the same moves that autoregressive language models and learned world-models use. Video is the case where those moves are forced on you by physics.

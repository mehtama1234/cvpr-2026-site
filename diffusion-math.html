Diffusion — why it works, mathematically

There is no way to conjure a brand-new realistic picture out of nothing — the space of real pictures has no formula you can draw from. Diffusion gets around that by learning to do, in reverse, the one thing that is easy: gradually sprinkling a picture with random specks until it is pure static.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

Diffusion learns to clean. During training, clean data is damaged with noise. The model learns how to remove the damage. During generation, it starts from noise and repeatedly cleans until an image, video, or shape appears.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology becomes important when the generated object must have the right connected structure. A face should not have broken parts, a road network should connect, a vessel tree should not have impossible cuts, and a 3D mesh should not grow accidental holes.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

Diffusion now appears in image generation, video, audio, 3D assets, restoration, molecule design, medical reconstruction, and robot action generation. The same clean-up idea moves across many data types.

Why Diffusion is worth learning

It matters because many creative and repair tasks can be written as 'start messy, then make it more consistent with the goal.' The hard part is keeping the result faithful, not merely pretty.

The key turn — the principle at work

The stuck point. Drawing a realistic image, video, or action in one jump is too hard. The target data lives in a thin, structured part of a huge space.

The mathematical move. The principle is reversing noise by small learned steps. Add noise until data becomes easy random static; then train a model to predict how to step back toward clean data at each noise level.

What changes. Generation stops being one impossible leap. It becomes many easier corrections. The cost is time: many small steps can be slow unless the path is shortened or distilled.

The problem, as a quantity

Suppose you want a computer to produce a brand-new photo of a face that belongs to no real person. A photo that size is a million numbers, and only an unimaginably thin sliver of all possible million-number combinations happens to look like a face — the rest is meaningless speckle. To draw a new face you would need to reach into exactly that thin sliver, and nobody can write down where it is.

So drawing a realistic picture directly is out. But there is one direction that is easy: taking a real picture and slowly sprinkling random specks onto it, a few more at a time, until every trace of the original is buried and you are left with pure static. That corruption has a plain formula — you can do it to any picture in one line of arithmetic.

Diffusion's whole move is to learn that easy process run backwards. If a network could start from pure static and take away specks a little at a time, undoing each stage of the corruption, it would arrive at a fresh realistic picture. The catch is that undoing corruption needs one piece of knowledge at every stage: given a speckled picture, which direction leads back toward a real one. That direction has to be learned from data — and the rest of this page is how.

The core ideas — and why they work

The corruption formula: jump to any amount of static in one step

x_t = √ᾱ_t·x₀ + √(1-ᾱ_t)·ε, where ε ~ N(0,I) and ᾱ_t goes 1 → 0 as t goes 0 → 1
The forward, corrupting direction is a formula you can apply directly, no stepping required. Here x₀ is the clean picture, ε is a picture-sized sheet of pure random specks (each speck drawn from the ordinary bell-shaped spread of values), and t is how far along the corruption you have gone. The dial ᾱ_t slides from 1 down to 0 as t runs from start to finish, and it sets the mix: a shrinking amount of the real picture, √ᾱ_t, plus a growing amount of static, √(1-ᾱ_t).

The two amounts are chosen so that their squares always add up to 1. Keeping them balanced that way means the corrupted picture stays a sensible picture at every stage — it does not blow up bright or wash out to grey, it just gets progressively speckled. At the start you have the clean picture; at the end you have pure static; in between you fade smoothly from one to the other.

The reason this one formula matters so much is that it lets you jump straight to any amount of static in a single step, rather than sprinkling specks on a hundred times over. That means you can show the network pictures at every stage of corruption during training, all mixed together, instead of having to march through the stages in order.

The direction back toward real pictures is just the direction back toward the clean one

∇_x log p_t(x) is the score. By Tweedie's formula: E[x₀ | x_t] = x_t + σ_t² · ∇_x log p_t(x_t). Therefore: ∇_x log p_t(x) ∝ (x₀ - x_t) / σ_t²
Undoing corruption needs, at each stage, the direction that leads from where you are toward a more-likely picture — call it the arrow. Written out, the arrow is the slope of the log of the probability, ∇_x log p_t(x): the way to nudge a picture so that it becomes more probable under real data. The trouble is you have no formula for how probable any picture is, so you cannot read the arrow off directly.

Here is the fact that saves it, a known result from probability called Tweedie's formula. It says the best possible guess of the clean picture, given a speckled one, equals the speckled picture plus the arrow scaled by the amount of static, σ_t². Rearrange that one line and the arrow turns out to be simply the clean picture minus the speckled picture, divided by the amount of static: (x₀ − x_t) / σ_t².

Read plainly, that says the arrow — the direction toward more-likely pictures — points straight from the speckled picture back toward the clean picture it came from. 'Which way is more realistic' and 'which way is back toward the original' are the same direction. So learning the arrow is exactly learning to look at a corrupted picture and point at what the clean one underneath must have been.

You cannot learn the arrow directly, but you can learn to name the specks

Train ε_θ(x_t, t) to predict the noise ε. Since σ_t² · ε = x_t - √ᾱ_t·x₀, predicting ε is equivalent to predicting the score up to a scale factor.
There is no pile of ready-made arrows to copy from — real pictures do not come labelled with their arrows. But during training you made every speckled picture yourself, so you know precisely which sheet of specks ε you sprinkled on. That is the label you train against.

So the network is handed the speckled picture x_t and the stage t, and its whole job is to answer: which specks were added? Call its guess ε_θ(x_t, t). This is ordinary learning-from-examples — corrupt a picture, ask the network to name the specks, check its answer against the specks you actually used, nudge it to do better.

And naming the specks turns out to be the same task as knowing the arrow, differing only by a fixed scaling, because the specks are just the speckled picture minus a shrunk copy of the clean one — the exact quantity the arrow is built from. So this one simple, checkable task quietly teaches the network the arrow at every stage of corruption. One real picture also yields endless practice: every choice of stage and every fresh sheet of specks makes another training example out of the same picture.

Making a new picture: walk the corruption backwards

Reverse SDE: dx = [f(x,t) - g(t)² · ∇_x log p_t(x)] dt + g(t) dW, sampling t from 1 down to 0
To produce a brand-new picture, start from a sheet of pure static and walk the corruption process backwards. Each backward step does two small things: use the trained network to estimate the arrow at the current picture and take a short step along it — toward something more realistic — and then sprinkle back a tiny bit of fresh static. Repeat for many steps and the static thins out while real structure emerges, until you are left with a picture that could have come from the data.

Why add specks back on the way down, when the goal is to remove them? Because always stepping straight along the arrow and nothing else would funnel every run toward the same few over-averaged, blurred pictures. The small random kicks keep the runs apart, so different starting static lands on different realistic pictures rather than all collapsing together. As the steps accumulate, the kicks average out toward nothing and the arrow-following takes over, settling the picture onto the realistic sliver.

Stripped of the formal notation, one step reads: new picture = current picture + a small step along the arrow + a small random kick. The long expression above is just the exact bookkeeping for how large each of those three parts should be at each stage.

The entire training goal in one line

ℒ = E_{t, x₀, ε} [‖ε - ε_θ(x_t, t)‖² ], where x_t = √ᾱ_t·x₀ + √(1-ᾱ_t)·ε
All of training is this single loop: pick a random stage t, use the corruption formula to make a speckled picture x_t, feed it to the network along with the stage, and measure how far its guessed specks ε_θ(x_t, t) are from the real specks ε — squaring the difference so misses in either direction count, and averaging over many pictures, stages, and speck-sheets. Push the network to make that average small.

A small average gap means the network names the specks well at every stage, which — from the previous ideas — means it has learned the arrow everywhere. This squared-gap target is not picked for convenience: making it small is mathematically the same as making the network's eventual pictures match the real data as closely as possible, so minimising the easy-to-measure quantity drags the hard-to-measure one along with it.

Leaning on some stages more than others — caring more about the heavily-speckled steps, or the lightly-speckled ones — is the one real tuning choice, and it trades off getting the big overall shape right against getting the fine texture right.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Every use of diffusion is the same machine — learn the arrow, then walk the corruption backwards — pointed at a different job.

Following an instruction. Feed the network something extra alongside the speckled picture — a caption turned into numbers, a set of photos from other angles, a robot's camera view — so the arrow it learns points not just toward any realistic picture but toward one that fits the instruction. This one change covers making pictures from text, rebuilding a 3D scene that agrees with several photos, and generating a robot's next moves.

Editing an existing picture. Hold the trained arrow-network fixed and push a picture around to satisfy some extra rule — match this outline, keep this region unchanged — while the arrow keeps pulling it back toward looking realistic. The two pressures together produce an edit that obeys the rule without turning fake.

Making it faster. The plain method takes many small backward steps. Faster versions take fewer, larger steps, or train a quick network to imitate the many-step one in a single shot, or reshape the corruption schedule so fewer stages are needed. Each trades a little quality for a lot of speed.

A worked numeric example

If clean data is x₀=1, noise is ε=-0.5, and α_t=0.64, the noised point is x_t=√0.64·1 + √0.36·(-0.5)=0.5. Training asks the model to infer the noise that was added. Sampling runs that inference backward.

6The race for fewer steps
x_{t-Δt} ≈ x_t + Δt · ∇_x log p_t(x_t); fewer-step methods use better ODE solvers (Runge-Kutta, geometric integration) or distill the score into a simpler model that requires fewer evaluations
Standard diffusion requires many reverse steps to integrate the SDE. High-order ODE solvers (second-order and higher) respect the geometry of the probability flow, letting fewer steps stay on track. Teaching methods train a smaller student network to imitate the teacher score, so each step does more work; combined, these cut the forward pass count from 50 steps to 1-4 with minimal quality loss.
These four papers all tackle the problem of diffusion being slow because it takes many steps. One compresses the working space to be smaller; another partitions the cleanup into phases where early steps build structure and late steps add detail; one swaps the step-by-step solver for a better one; the last swaps diffusion for a related method that takes straighter paths.

The through-line. Diffusion turns one impossible act — reaching into the thin sliver of realistic pictures out of nowhere — into a long series of easy ones: at each amount of static, take a small step back toward realism. What makes each step learnable is that the direction toward realistic pictures is the same as the direction back toward the clean picture, and that direction can be taught by the simplest task there is — ask the network to name the specks you yourself added, then check its answer.

Two plain ideas carry the whole method: split one hopeless draw into many small clean-up steps, and learn each step by predicting the noise you put in. Everything else — steering by a caption, rebuilding a 3D scene, editing, cutting the number of steps — is a variation on where you point that same learned direction.

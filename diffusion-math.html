Diffusion — why it works, mathematically

Getting a sample straight from your data distribution is mathematically impossible in high dimensions. Diffusion makes it possible by learning to reverse a known forward process—turning one giant jump into a million tiny, learnable steps.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

Diffusion learns to clean. During training, clean data is damaged with noise. The model learns how to remove the damage. During generation, it starts from noise and repeatedly cleans until an image, video, or shape appears.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology becomes important when the generated object must have the right connected structure. A face should not have broken parts, a road network should connect, a vessel tree should not have impossible cuts, and a 3D mesh should not grow accidental holes.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

Diffusion now appears in image generation, video, audio, 3D assets, restoration, molecule design, medical reconstruction, and robot action generation. The same clean-up idea moves across many data types.

Why Diffusion is worth learning

It matters because many creative and repair tasks can be written as 'start messy, then make it more consistent with the goal.' The hard part is keeping the result faithful, not merely pretty.

The problem, as a quantity

Drawing a sample directly from p(x) is mathematically impossible in high dimensions—there is no formula that lets you ask 'what does the data distribution look like?' and get an answer. Generative models work around this impossibility. Diffusion does it differently: instead of sampling from p(x) all at once, you start by sampling from something simple (pure random Gaussian noise), then learn how to undo a noise-adding process step by step. The key insight is that the noise-adding process is known and works in only one direction—you only have to learn the reverse. But reversing it requires the score: a compass pointing toward regions where data is likely to exist. And the score is not free—you must learn it from data.

The core ideas — and why they work

The forward chain: turn data into noise by a known path

x_t = √ᾱ_t·x₀ + √(1-ᾱ_t)·ε, where ε ~ N(0,I) and ᾱ_t goes 1 → 0 as t goes 0 → 1
The forward process is a formula you can use directly to corrupt any image to any noise level in one jump, without doing many steps. This formula is a mix: at t=0 you have pure image; at t=1 you have pure noise; in between, you fade smoothly through Gaussian space. The coefficients √ᾱ_t and √(1-ᾱ_t) follow a rule (using Pythagoras) where they add up to one, so they stay balanced. The image and noise point in different directions, so you corrupt without destroying the structure—the image stays a valid point in the space, just blurry. By choosing the schedule ᾱ_t, you control how fast corruption spreads. This foundation matters because you can jump straight to any noise level, so you can train the network on all of them at the same time, not one step after another.

The score: the gradient of the log probability

∇_x log p_t(x) is the score. By Tweedie's formula: E[x₀ | x_t] = x_t + σ_t² · ∇_x log p_t(x_t). Therefore: ∇_x log p_t(x) ∝ (x₀ - x_t) / σ_t²
The score (the slope of the log probability) is not something you can compute—log p_t(x) is the log of a distribution you do not have a formula for. But here is the trick: if you know how to reverse the forward process, you must know the score. And if you know the noise-adding formula, you can derive what the score must be using Tweedie's formula, a fact from probability math. Rearranging, the score is proportional to the difference between the clean image x₀ and the noisy image x_t, divided by the noise level squared. In other words: the direction of highest probability always points back toward the original data. Learning the score means learning to identify, from a corrupted image, what the clean image was underneath.

Predict noise, not the probability

Train ε_θ(x_t, t) to predict the noise ε. Since σ_t² · ε = x_t - √ᾱ_t·x₀, predicting ε is equivalent to predicting the score up to a scale factor.
You cannot learn the score directly—you have no ground-truth scores from your data. But you have noisy images and you know the noise that went into them (at least during training). So the network learns to predict noise instead: it sees x_t (the noisy image) and t (the noise level) and learns to output ε (the Gaussian noise that was mixed in). This is a supervised task like any other—you corrupt an image, feed it to the network, and check its prediction against the actual noise. The beautiful part: this prediction is equivalent to the score up to a scale factor. The network is learning two things at once—what the data looks like, and how to move toward it. The loss ‖ε − ε_θ(x_t, t)‖² is plain L2 (average squared difference), but because the network sees the data at every noise level (via random t sampling), it learns to clean up images across all scales. This is a form of data multiplication: one image, many corruptions, many training examples from one pair.

Reverse the SDE: run noise backwards

Reverse SDE: dx = [f(x,t) - g(t)² · ∇_x log p_t(x)] dt + g(t) dW, sampling t from 1 down to 0
The forward process is a stochastic differential equation (SDE, a math recipe for adding noise over time). Probability theory says you can run any SDE backwards if you know the score. The reverse drift flips sign and subtracts the score direction; at each reverse step, you add a tiny bit of fresh Gaussian noise (the dW term), then move in the direction the score points—back toward data. Over many steps, the noise averages to nearly zero and the score dominates, pulling the sample toward likely regions. In practice, this means: start with pure noise, then repeat 'subtract a scaled score direction, add a tiny Gaussian bump' for thousands of steps. The result is an image sampled from p(x). The discrete (step-by-step) version is intuitive: x_{t-1} = x_t - (step size) · ∇ log p_t(x_t) + √(step size) · z, where z is fresh Gaussian.

Training loss: match predictions to ground truth

ℒ = E_{t, x₀, ε} [‖ε - ε_θ(x_t, t)‖² ], where x_t = √ᾱ_t·x₀ + √(1-ᾱ_t)·ε
The training loop is straightforward: pick a random timestep t, corrupt the image to x_t using the known forward formula, feed it to the network along with t, and compare the predicted noise ε_θ(x_t, t) to the true noise ε. Minimizing this loss is provably equivalent to minimizing the reverse KL divergence (the gap between your learned distribution and the data distribution), so you are not just fitting randomly, you are fitting to the right objective. Weighting the loss by time (higher weight on high-noise steps, or vice versa) changes what the model prioritizes; this is a knob for trading off overall shape against fine details.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Text-to-image: feed the text description (converted to numbers) into the denoising network so the model learns to steer toward images that match the caption. 3D reconstruction: run diffusion over a 3D shape representation (voxel grid, learned field, or mesh) and condition on photos taken from multiple angles; the score learns to reverse the photography process—the model solves the inverse problem of 'given photos, find the 3D shape that projects to them'. Editing and inverse problems: freeze the learned score network and optimize the image directly, adding a task-specific loss (e.g., a penalty for not matching a segmentation) and letting the score guide you to stay on the learned data surface. Using the score as a metric: in downstream tasks (classification, detecting objects, assessing quality), treat the score itself as a learned similarity measure—how much do I have to clean up this image to make it look like a different one? Robot learning: treat action sequences as high-dimensional objects to clean up, conditioning on observations. The score learns to correct trajectories away from collisions and toward task success. Accelerating sampling: use ODE solvers (predictable, fewer steps), teach a fast network to mimic a slow one, or learn a faster noise schedule. All trade quality for speed.

A worked numeric example

If clean data is x₀=1, noise is ε=-0.5, and α_t=0.64, the noised point is x_t=√0.64·1 + √0.36·(-0.5)=0.5. Training asks the model to infer the noise that was added. Sampling runs that inference backward.

6The race for fewer steps
x_{t-Δt} ≈ x_t + Δt · ∇_x log p_t(x_t); fewer-step methods use better ODE solvers (Runge-Kutta, geometric integration) or distill the score into a simpler model that requires fewer evaluations
Standard diffusion requires many reverse steps to integrate the SDE. High-order ODE solvers (second-order and higher) respect the geometry of the probability flow, letting fewer steps stay on track. Teaching methods train a smaller student network to imitate the teacher score, so each step does more work; combined, these cut the forward pass count from 50 steps to 1-4 with minimal quality loss.
These four papers all tackle the problem of diffusion being slow because it takes many steps. One compresses the working space to be smaller; another partitions the cleanup into phases where early steps build structure and late steps add detail; one swaps the step-by-step solver for a better one; the last swaps diffusion for a related method that takes straighter paths.

The through-line. Diffusion inverts the curse of high dimensions by splitting one impossible task (sample from an unknown high-dimensional distribution) into a long chain of simple tasks (clean up at each noise level). The score is the compass; the schedule is the map. You learn to reverse a known forward process by predicting the noise at each level, then sample by running that reversal in reverse. Every variant—text conditioning, 3D lifting, inverse problems, robot policies—is just a way to twist the score toward something more useful than general-purpose cleanup. The underlying idea is ancient: if you know how to mix two things (noise and data), you can learn to unmix them.

Variational inference — why it works, mathematically

You need to find a probability shape that explains some hidden data, but computing the real shape is impossible. This is the trick that finds a shape you can actually work with by maximizing a measurable lower bound instead of chasing the unreachable answer.

The problem, as a quantity

Imagine you observe some data and want to understand the hidden causes behind it. The "true answer" is a probability shape that would perfectly explain how the hidden parts and visible parts fit together. Computing this exact shape requires adding up infinitely many possibilities in a way that is just not doable—it's mathematically intractable.

So instead of finding the true answer, you play a different game: pick a shape from a simple family of shapes that are fast to work with, and make it as close as possible to the unreachable true answer. The problem is you cannot directly measure how close they are. You need a trick that lets you measure something simpler instead.

The core ideas — and why they work

The exact answer is intractable, so approximate it with an easy family

q*(z) = argmin_{q ∈ Q} gap( q(z), p(z|x) )
The true answer—the real probability shape over hidden parts given the data—is intractable to compute. So you restrict your search to a manageable family of simple shapes and find the one in that family that minimizes the gap between it and the truth. You are choosing a placeholder that stays within your family but gets as close as possible to where you cannot reach. This is an admission: you are trading perfect accuracy for actually being able to compute something.

You can't measure the gap directly, so maximize a computable lower bound

log p(x) = ELBO(q) + gap ≥ ELBO(q)
The gap between your placeholder shape and the true shape is invisible—you cannot calculate it directly. But here is the trick: log probability of the data equals the gap plus something called the ELBO (evidence lower bound), a term you can calculate. Since the log probability is fixed and the gap is always non-negative, maximizing the ELBO automatically minimizes the gap. You cannot measure the gap, so you measure the gap's complement instead.

The bound splits into fit-the-data versus stay-simple

ELBO = E_q[log p(x,z)] − E_q[log q(z)]
The ELBO splits into two competing goals: one term measures how well your placeholder shape explains the data paired with hidden parts; the other measures whether your placeholder stays simple and close to a neutral guess. When you maximize the ELBO, you are balancing a trade-off—fit the observations better, yes, but not so much that your shape becomes wildly complicated. The first term pulls toward explaining what you see; the second pulls toward staying ordinary and ordinary.

A simple family is fast but cannot capture everything

Q too narrow ⇒ q can't match the true shape (the cost)
A simple family of shapes is fast—you can compute with it, optimize it, sample from it. But simplicity has a cost: the true shape might be stretched across multiple bumpy regions, and your smooth simple shape cannot capture that complexity. If the true shape has a tall spike here and another there, but your family only offers smooth mounds, you are stuck. You cannot fix this by working harder; the family itself is too narrow. The gap will always remain, no matter how perfectly you optimize within your constraints.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Different versions of variational inference dial the same core machine with different simple families (mean-field cuts dependencies, hierarchical adds layers, normalizing flows reshape stepwise), different divergences (KL divergence counts mistakes by false surprises, Wasserstein counts distance, others trade off), and different optimization tricks (the re-routed-randomness trick, score-matching, implicit models). The thread never changes: you cannot compute the true answer, so you maximize a measurable lower bound to find the best approximation within a family you can actually work with.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

One notch deeper: each of the theme's 4 approach-families and the specific mathematical mechanism that makes its papers work — the exact knob turned on the core relations above. Under each, the specific mathematical note for every paper in that family.

1Mean-Field Factorization
Restrict q to q(x₁)q(x₂)···q(xₙ) instead of full q(x)
When you parameterize the approximation as fully independent factors, you make the optimization over all possible distributions turn into separate optimization problems for each variable. This is much faster to compute, but it forces the approximation to miss any correlations the true answer has. The trade-off is speed versus accuracy: factorized families can fit data well when the posterior is already nearly independent, but they understate uncertainty and interdependence when the real answer couples different variables together.
2KL Direction and Mode-Seeking Versus Mass-Covering
Choose KL(p‖q) [forward, averages under p] vs KL(q‖p) [reverse, averages under q]
Forward KL spreads the approximation over all the high-probability regions of the target, because it penalizes missing any mass that p has. Reverse KL locks the approximation onto whichever peak is tallest, because it penalizes putting q-mass where p is close to zero. This directional choice is not a minor detail—it fundamentally changes whether the approximation captures the breadth of possible explanations or commits to a single confident guess. The right choice depends on what downstream task needs the approximation.
3Expected Log Joint Plus Entropy as the ELBO
Maximize E_q[log p(y,θ)] + H[q] instead of minimizing KL(q‖p(θ|y))
You cannot directly compute the reverse KL because it requires knowing the normalization constant of the exact posterior, which is what makes inference hard in the first place. The ELBO rewrites that impossible minimization as a sum of two computable pieces: the expected log joint (fit—how well does q predict the data and prior together) and the spread of q (how much uncertainty does q maintain). Maximizing this sum pulls in two directions: bigger log joint values push q toward high-probability explanations, while higher spread resists collapsing q into a point estimate.
4Reparameterization for Backpropagation Through Samples
Write x = g(θ,ε) where ε is parameter-free noise and g is smooth
Direct sampling from a random variable and then taking its slope with respect to parameters is awkward because sampling is not smooth—small changes in the parameter can flip which sample you get. Reparameterization sidesteps this by expressing the random variable as a deterministic function of fixed noise and learned parameters, so gradients can flow backward through the smooth deterministic map. This unlocks training deep generative models and complex variational objectives by standard backpropagation, turning a random objective into something you can differentiate.
The through-line. The core problem is approximating an unreachable probability shape with a simple shape you can actually compute with, when you have no direct way to measure how close you are getting. Two plain ideas carry it: the gap between any two shapes always decomposes into a measurable lower bound plus an invisible remainder, so maximizing what you can measure pulls you toward the unreachable truth, and the family you choose must be simple enough to optimize fast but rich enough to capture the shape of the real answer—a tension with no perfect answer, only trade-offs.

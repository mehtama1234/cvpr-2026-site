Tokenizing / VQ — why it works, mathematically

Snap continuous perception to discrete language-model words via learned partitions.

The problem, as a quantity

Neural networks live in continuous space (vectors, real numbers), but autoregressive language models eat tokens—discrete symbols. To feed continuous output (images, video, robotics embeddings, sound) into a pretrained LLM, you must convert streams of floats into sequences of integers. But snapping is not differentiable; gradients break at the quantization boundary. And if your partition is unbalanced, most codes go unused (codebook collapse), wasting capacity.

The core ideas — and why they work

Voronoi partitions of feature space

Core mechanism: why quantization works at all
Every continuous feature z gets labeled by its nearest codebook vector e_k. This is nearest-neighbor assignment: q(z) = argmin_k ‖z − e_k‖. Geometrically, this partitions the entire feature space into Voronoi cells—each cell 'owns' one code. Every point in cell k maps to the same integer k. This is lossless in principle (you can reconstruct which cell you landed in), but lossy in practice (the cell center e_k is only an approximation of the full feature distribution inside it).

Straight-through estimator (STE)

How gradients flow despite the discrete snap
The argmin operation is non-differentiable: which cell you land in changes discontinuously as z moves. But you need gradients to learn the codebook. STE is a pragmatic trick: on the forward pass, snap z to q(z) (the integer index and its reconstruction e_q(z)). On the backward pass, pretend the quantizer was the identity—copy the gradient straight through as if q(z) = z. This 'pretend' gradient doesn't match physics, but it works in practice: the upstream layers learn to produce features near codebook centers, and the codebook learns to track clusters. It is a form of gradient surgery that keeps learning alive.

Codebook collapse and dead codes

The partition-balancing problem
If you only use L2 reconstruction loss ‖sg[z] − e‖² (sg = stop-gradient, no gradient flows to e here), the codebook vectors e_k tend to cluster where data is densest and ignore empty regions. Most codes never get assigned any feature and vanish (dead codes). Perplexity drops. The fix: commitment loss β‖z − sg[e]‖² forces features z to move toward assigned codes, and exponential-moving-average (EMA) codebook updates prevent codes from drifting away. EMA samples only codes that were actually used, so dead codes are revived by averaging in a small fraction of unused codes or by resetting them to live data points. The craft is keeping the partition balanced—each code should see roughly equal traffic.

Codebook as a learned compression dictionary

Why this partition is learned, not fixed
If you quantized to a fixed uniform grid, you would waste codes on empty space and run out where data clusters. Instead, learn the codebook {e_k} from data. Each training step, features vote for their nearest codes (forward pass), and codes move toward their assigned features (codebook update). Over time, codes spread out and cluster around high-density regions in feature space. This is like k-means: iteratively partition and update cluster centers. But here it is end-to-end: the quantizer is part of a larger model (e.g., a VAE or video tokenizer) so the feature distribution itself can shift as upstream layers learn. The codebook must adapt or collapse.

The approaches, as variations on the math

1. Fighting collapse: EMA updates, dead-code resets, perplexity monitoring. 2. Better quantizers: product quantization (factorise codebook), residual VQ (hierarchical refinement), straight-through variants. 3. Tokenizers for generation: compress video to 1D token sequences (fewer tokens = faster decoding); add temporal coherence so adjacent tokens are similar. 4. Semantic tokens: use auxiliary losses (e.g., contrastive learning) to ensure tokens capture meaningful features, not just nearest neighbors. 5. Action tokenization: snap robot motion (joint angles, end-effector pose) to a learned vocabulary of 'motion primitives', making discrete planning possible.

3Fewer tokens: video and compact tokenizers

Video and high-resolution images waste tokens on redundant spatial grids. Converting 2D grids to 1D token sequences removes positional locks, letting the model allocate tokens adaptively by motion and detail. Temporal causality (tokens depend on past frames but not future) and learned adaptive length compress further by skipping low-information frames while preserving dynamic regions.

5Snapping actions to words

Robots and imitation learning predict actions as discrete tokens from a learned menu rather than continuous values. This converts trajectory generation to sequence modeling (a strength of transformers) and grounds learned skills in a shared vocabulary. Hybrid approaches keep continuous diffusion trajectories but condition them on discrete style tokens, combining expressiveness of diffusion with interpretability of discrete action categories.

6Motion as words
z_motion_coarse ← VQ_VAE_L1(motion_seq); z_motion_fine ← VQ_VAE_L2(residual(motion_seq − decode(z_coarse))); pose_token ← codebook[argmin ‖hand_pose − codebook_i‖]
Motion (human, hand, crowd, traffic) becomes interpretable when encoded as hierarchical tokens. Coarse tokens capture overall motion intent or category (walking style, reaching direction); fine tokens resolve the details. Hand poses and behaviors snap to discrete codebook entries, making each token a named skill or pose, so generation becomes a sequence of labeled primitives that are human-readable and composable across contexts.

The through-line. A Voronoi partition of feature space maps continuous z to discrete codes. Straight-through estimators let gradients flow through the discrete snap. Balancing the partition (EMA, dead-code revival) keeps the codebook alive. The result: continuous perception becomes a stream of integers that LLMs can eat.

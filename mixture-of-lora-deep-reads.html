1 · Meet the world the giant never saw

A giant model trained on millions of ordinary photos learns an astonishing amount about the visual world — but its training set is still a narrow slice of reality. It has never peered inside a human body, stared down from a satellite, read the heat glowing off a warm object in the dark, or made sense of a laser scanner sweeping the road ahead. Point it at one of those and it stumbles, because the new world does not look like anything it studied. The obvious fix — retrain the whole giant on the new data — is wildly expensive, and worse, it tends to make the model forget the broad competence that made it valuable in the first place. The papers in this cluster all take the cheaper, smarter path: keep the giant frozen, leave its hard-won knowledge untouched, and bolt on a small set of trainable patches that translate the strange new domain into terms the giant already understands. The art is entirely in where those patches go and how they steer the frozen model without erasing what it already knows.
CVPR'26Depth Any Endoscopy: Towards Self-Supervised Generalizable Depth Estimation in Monocular Endoscopy
Problem
Surgeons need a sense of depth from a single endoscope camera to reconstruct 3D shapes and navigate inside the body, but there is no ground-truth depth to learn from during surgery. Worse, every procedure looks different — a colonoscopy and a laparoscopy vary wildly in lighting, texture, and how far away things sit — so a depth model that works for one fails on the next.
Approach
The authors take a large general-purpose vision foundation model, freeze it, and adapt it to the surgical world with two kinds of small patches working together. Inside the frozen network they insert both LoRA patches and Adapter patches, and a little gating rule (a mixture-of-experts) decides which patch to lean on for a given input — that is the intra-model level. On the outside, a second extra-model level uses a strong general vision model (DINOv3) to calibrate what the scene actually contains, nudging the frozen features toward the right semantics for that procedure. Because there are no depth labels, everything is trained self-supervised from multi-view surgical video, and a gradient harmonization trick keeps the depth-and-motion halves of the training from fighting each other.
Why it matters
It is the frozen-giant recipe applied to a place the giant never saw: the broad vision model stays intact, and cheap swappable LoRA/Adapter patches plus a small gate translate the alien world inside the body into depth the model can predict.
CVPR'26CrossEarth-Gate: Fisher-Guided Adaptive Tuning Engine for Efficient Adaptation of Cross-Domain Remote Sensing Semantic Segmentation
Problem
A foundation model for satellite and aerial imagery has to label every pixel — road, building, water — across places it has never flown over. But the gap between the training region and a new one is not one problem, it is three tangled at once: objects appear at different scales and layouts, the same class looks different across geographies, and different sensors leave different spectral artifacts. Most cheap adaptation methods fix only one of these gaps, so they break when the real shift is a mix.
Approach
CrossEarth-Gate keeps the geospatial foundation model frozen and hands it a toolbox of three different kinds of patch, each specialized for one kind of gap: a LoRA patch for spatial structure, an Adapter patch for semantic differences, and a frequency patch for spectral artifacts. The clever part is deciding which patches to switch on. Every so often during training it estimates, using a quantity called Fisher information, which patches at which layers are actually doing the most useful work right now — then it activates only that top handful for the next stretch of training. The selection is balanced so no single patch type hogs the budget, letting the mix of specialists shift as the domain demands.
Why it matters
This is the frozen-base-plus-specialists skeleton made explicit and dynamic: a static giant, a library of cheap specialist patches, and a running rule — here, Fisher information — that decides which specialists to combine for the domain in front of it.
CVPR'26ORION: ORthonormal Text Encoding for Universal VLM AdaptatION
Problem
Vision-language models classify images by comparing them to text descriptions of each class, so the quality of those text embeddings decides everything. The trouble is that a frozen text encoder tends to place similar class names close together, leaving the categories smeared and hard to tell apart. Averaging over many prompts stabilizes things but does not fix the underlying crowded geometry of the space.
Approach
ORION leaves the whole vision-language model frozen and touches only the text side, adapting it with small LoRA patches. It trains those patches with a simple goal: push the text vectors for different classes to point in more independent directions — mathematically, toward being orthogonal — so the categories spread out and become easier to separate. A second term in the objective keeps each class from drifting too far from its original meaning, and the whole scheme is grounded in a clean probabilistic argument. Remarkably, it needs no images and no captions at all — just the list of class names — which makes it a plug-and-play add-on for many different backbones.
Why it matters
The frozen giant stays put; a tiny LoRA patch on its text encoder is the cheap specialist, and its only job is to reshape the class geometry so the model can adapt to new label sets without ever seeing new pictures.
ROBOTICS'25Low-Rank Adaptation-Based All-Weather Removal for Autonomous Navigation
Problem
Self-driving perception relies on models that clean up rain, snow, or fog from camera images, but those models are trained on specific known weather and fall apart on weather they have never seen. Retraining the whole model on each new condition is too expensive, and naive fine-tuning tends to wreck its skill on the original conditions.
Approach
The authors take a pretrained all-weather restoration model, freeze it, and adapt it to a novel degradation with small LoRA patches instead of retraining. Their key addition, called LoRA-Align, tackles forgetting head-on: using a matrix decomposition (SVD), it keeps the adapted weights' internal directions aligned with the original model's, so learning the new weather does not overwrite the old. The result adapts to unseen conditions while still handling everything it could handle before, at a fraction of the training cost.
Why it matters
It is the skeleton in one clean move: a frozen restoration model as the base, a cheap LoRA specialist patch for the new weather, and an alignment rule that lets the patch combine with the original without erasing it.
ROBOTICS'25TASeg: Text-aware RGB-T Semantic Segmentation based on Fine-tuning Vision Foundation Models
Problem
Robots that fuse a normal color camera with a thermal (heat) camera can see in the dark, but segmentation models for this leaned too heavily on raw appearance and confused categories that look alike across the two sensors. They lacked any sense of what each object actually is.
Approach
TASeg starts from SAM, a large frozen vision foundation model, and adapts it to the color-plus-thermal world with small LoRA patches, adding a fusion module in the encoder that learns how to blend the color and heat streams. To fix the confusion between look-alike classes, it pipes in text: CLIP-generated word embeddings for the class names are injected into the mask decoder, giving the model a semantic anchor so it labels by meaning, not just by appearance. All of this adapts the frozen giant with far fewer trainable parameters than retraining.
Why it matters
The frozen SAM giant never trained on thermal imagery; cheap LoRA patches plus injected text embeddings are the specialists that let it meet this new sensor and reason about what things are, not just how they look.
ROBOTICS'25Label-Efficient LiDAR Semantic Segmentation with 2D-3D Vision Transformer Adapters
Problem
Huge vision foundation models are trained on ordinary photographs, but LiDAR sensors produce sparse 3D point clouds that live in a completely different data format — so the point-cloud models that label the road cannot borrow any of that pre-trained image knowledge. That hurts most when very few labeled scans are available.
Approach
BALViT keeps the powerful image Vision Transformer frozen and feeds it a flattened range-view rendering of the LiDAR scan so the point cloud is seen through image-shaped eyes. Alongside it runs a second branch that reads the scene from a bird's-eye-view, and the two are stitched together by novel 2D-3D cross-attention adapters — small trainable bridges that continuously inject 3D LiDAR structure into the frozen image features. This lets a model built for photos contribute real understanding to a 3D sensing task, even when labeled data is scarce.
Why it matters
The frozen photo-trained giant is the base it never trained on LiDAR; the cheap 2D-3D adapters are the specialist patches, and cross-attention is the rule that combines image priors with 3D knowledge in the low-label regime.

2 · Two ideas in one picture

Suppose you have one cheap patch that teaches an image model to draw your dog, and another cheap patch that teaches it to paint in watercolor. You want both at once: your dog, in watercolor. The obvious move is to add the two patches together and run the model. It almost never works. The two patches were each trained alone, so they fight over the same weights, and the result smears the dog's identity into the style or lets the style bleed where it should not. Researchers call this concept interference or entanglement: the what (content, identity) and the how (style) get tangled up when you stack them. The papers here all chase the same prize, composing two or more specialist patches into one clean picture without retraining a fresh combined patch every time. Their fixes range from geometric rules for how to blend patches, to teaching each patch to occupy its own slice of the model, to full gated systems that pick which patch fires where.

CVPR'26CRAFT-LoRA: Content-Style Personalization via Rank-Constrained Adaptation and Training-Free Fusion
Problem

When you take a patch for content and a patch for style and combine them, the two representations run into each other and the model loses the plot. Existing fixes are unstable and usually demand extra training just to make a given pair of patches play nicely together.

Approach

CRAFT-LoRA starts by making cleaner patches in the first place. It fine-tunes with a rank constraint, which forces each patch to live in a small, tightly-defined slice of the model rather than sprawling across it, so the content patch and the style patch naturally settle into separate subspaces and stop overlapping. An expert encoder with specialized branches lets you pull in and selectively mix just the pieces you want. Then, instead of training a merged patch, it corrects the combination at generation time: a timestep-dependent guidance scheme nudges the model's predictions differently across the denoising steps, so the two patches stay balanced without any retraining. The whole point is that if each patch is disentangled from birth, adding them stops being a fight.

Why it matters

A clean take on the core skeleton, keep the foundation frozen, keep the specialist patches cheap, and make the combine rule a training-free correction rather than a costly new patch.

CVPR'26OrthoFuse: Training-free Riemannian Fusion of Orthogonal Style-Concept Adapters for Diffusion Models
Problem

Merging two fine-tuned patches by simply averaging their numbers distorts each one and washes out what made them distinct. There should be a principled way to blend a style patch and a concept patch that preserves both, with no retraining.

Approach

OrthoFuse works with a special family of patches (orthogonal fine-tuning) whose matrices have a clean geometric structure, they sit on a curved surface where straight-line averaging is simply the wrong path. The paper proves these patches form a smooth manifold and then walks the correct curved path between the content patch and the style patch, a geodesic, rather than the naive straight line that caused the distortion. It uses a closed-form formula (built from eigendecomposition) to compute that blend, then a spectra-restoration step corrects any drift so the merged patch keeps the sharpness of both originals. Every layer's patches are blended independently and in parallel, and the whole fusion finishes in under a second with no training. In short, the reason adding two patches interferes is that plain addition ignores their geometry, and OrthoFuse respects it.

Why it matters

Frozen foundation, cheap patches, and a combine rule that is a piece of geometry, blend along the manifold instead of averaging, so no gated router or retraining is needed.

CVPR'26SplitFlux: Learning to Decouple Content and Style from a Single Image
Problem

From a single image you want to grab what is shown and how it is rendered as two independent handles, so you can put the same subject in a new context or restyle it. Standard methods on the Flux model tangle the two, losing the subject's identity and botching re-contextualization.

Approach

SplitFlux first dissects the Flux model and finds that its early blocks control content and later blocks control style, a natural split the model already contains. It then trains separate patches on those specific blocks: a rank-constrained patch (the same trick as CRAFT-LoRA) preserves identity while pulling content and style apart. Its twist is a Visual-Gated LoRA that splits the content patch into a high-rank branch and a low-rank branch, steered by where the image is most salient, so the important subject detail is captured strongly while the background is handled lightly. By anchoring content and style patches to the blocks that actually do each job, it avoids the interference that comes from one patch trying to do everything at once.

Why it matters

Shows the specialist-patch idea working inside one model, placing content and style patches on the specific layers built for each, so the combine rule is really careful placement.

CVPR'26PositionIC: Unified Position and Identity Consistency for Image Customization
Problem

Placing several of your subjects into one scene at chosen spots breaks in two ways: the model mixes up their identities (especially when subjects look alike) and it ignores where you asked each to go. You want faithful identity and precise position for every subject at once.

Approach

PositionIC builds on the FLUX model and updates only a LoRA patch during training. It adds a position module (UnoPE) that conditions generation on each subject's bounding box, so the model knows exactly where each one belongs. The key fix for identity mixing is Visibility-Aware Attention: borrowing the volume-rendering idea from 3D graphics, it estimates how visible each subject's region is and masks attention accordingly, so an overlapping subject cannot bleed its features into a neighbor, the main cause of two concepts contaminating each other. To train all this it synthesizes a large paired dataset (PIC-98K) by composing and then decomposing multi-subject scenes. The interference here is spatial, two subjects fighting over the same pixels, and the visibility mask keeps each in its lane.

Why it matters

The same skeleton pointed at multiple identity patches in one scene: frozen FLUX, a cheap LoRA patch, and a combine rule that is an attention mask keeping concepts from leaking into each other.

CVPR'26PLACID: Identity-Preserving Multi-Object Compositing via Video Diffusion with Synthetic Trajectories
Problem

Studio-grade compositing needs to drop several real objects into one layout with near-perfect identity, correct colors, and no missing or duplicated items. Image models keep altering object details and getting layouts wrong because they lack a strong sense of object consistency.

Approach

PLACID's insight is to borrow priors from a video model instead of an image one: models trained on video already understand that an object stays the same object as it moves and reposes, exactly the identity consistency compositing needs. It adapts a pretrained image-to-video diffusion transformer, feeds in high-resolution object crops plus an optional background through cross-attention, and uses special text tokens to mark which description belongs to which object so captions do not cross-contaminate. It is trained with a single LoRA patch on synthetic clips where objects glide along plausible trajectories from scattered starting spots into the final composition, teaching the model to assemble a scene. The anti-interference move is those per-object tokens plus the video model's built-in object permanence, which together stop objects from blurring into one another.

Why it matters

Keeps the frozen-foundation-plus-cheap-patch recipe but swaps the foundation for a video model, showing the specialist patch can ride on whatever backbone best resists concept blending.

CVPR'26RegionRoute: Regional Style Transfer with Diffusion Model
Problem

Diffusion style transfer treats style as one global wash with no sense of where it should land, so you cannot restyle just one object and leave the rest untouched. Prior fixes lean on hand-drawn masks or messy multi-stage post-processing that leaves boundary artifacts.

Approach

RegionRoute teaches the model where to apply style by supervising attention during training: it aligns the attention of the style tokens with object masks, using a Focus loss (to aim the style at the right region) and a Cover loss (to fill that region densely), so at inference no mask is needed at all. Crucially, it is built on Flux with an explicit LoRA-MoE design, a genuine gated mixture of LoRA. Instead of one patch trying to serve every style, it holds a pool of specialized style experts and routes to them, which is what makes multi-style specialization cheap and keeps distinct styles from interfering. This is one of the clearest examples in the cluster of a real mixture-of-LoRA rather than a static blend.

Why it matters

A genuine gated mixture-of-LoRA: a pool of style-expert patches on a frozen Flux, with a learned router deciding which expert applies where, exactly the skeleton made explicit.

CVPR'26CoLoGen: Progressive Learning of Concept-Localization Duality for Unified Image Generation
Problem

One model that can inpaint, ground objects, follow control maps, insert subjects, and edit by instruction is hard because these tasks want opposite kinds of internal representation, some need conceptual understanding, others need precise spatial localization. Forcing them to share one representation makes the two compete and training becomes unstable.

Approach

CoLoGen names this tension the Concept-Localization Duality: conceptual cues and localization cues occupy rival subspaces, and jamming them together causes interference. Its architecture, Progressive Representation Weaving, sits inside each attention block as a gated mixture of expert patches, a noisy top-1 router picks the single most relevant expert to adjust the model's key and value projections, adding it as a residual. So different tasks and cues get routed to different specialist patches instead of overwriting one shared representation. It is trained in five easy-to-hard stages (a curriculum), first building core concept and localization skills, then adapting them to varied conditions, with a routing-supervision loss that keeps expert usage balanced so no expert is starved or overloaded.

Why it matters

A genuine gated mixture-of-LoRA: a noisy top-1 router over a pool of expert patches on a frozen FLUX, the fullest realization here of a frozen foundation, cheap specialist patches, and a learned rule that selects which one fires.

3 · A committee inside one model

A single dense network faces a squeeze. If you want it to handle wildly different situations — an indoor room and an open highway, a gentle lane-follow and a hard swerve — you either make it bigger, which means every input pays for all that extra weight, or you let the situations fight over the same shared parameters, where learning one skill quietly degrades another. That second failure has a name here: gradient conflict, where the training signal for one task pulls the weights one way and another task pulls them back.

The fix these papers share is conditional computation: instead of one big block of weights that every input runs through, you keep a small team of specialist sub-networks — the experts — and a tiny router that looks at each input and sends it to just one or two of them. Only the chosen experts do work, so you can add many specialists and grow total capacity without making any single input more expensive to process. Left to train this way, each expert quietly drifts toward its own niche — a scene type, an image region, a driving maneuver. That is the same skeleton as a mixture of LoRA adapters: a shared body plus swappable specialists and a chooser that decides which one speaks.

CVPR'26MoRE: 3D Visual Geometry Reconstruction Meets Mixture-of-Experts
Problem

Rebuilding 3D geometry from images has to cope with enormously different worlds — cramped indoor rooms, open outdoors, single objects, people, and moving scenes. A model that packs all of this into one shared set of weights ends up a jack of all trades: it never fully captures what makes any one kind of scene special, and it gets hard to scale up.

Approach

MoRE keeps a strong shared vision backbone and swaps some of its plain processing blocks for a small set of parallel expert blocks. A lightweight router looks at each little piece of the image — each token — and predicts which experts should handle it, then sends it to just the top few; the answer is a weighted blend of what those chosen experts return. Because only a handful fire per token, the model can hold many experts without every image paying for all of them. A load-balancing nudge during training stops one expert from hogging everything, so the specialists actually spread out. Left to learn, the experts drift toward different scene characteristics, which is exactly where the authors find the gain shows up most.

Why it matters

This is the mixture-of-LoRA skeleton applied to geometry: one shared backbone, a bench of expert specialists, and a per-token router that decides who handles what — added capacity without added cost per image. (Here the experts sit on a pretrained reconstruction backbone that is refined rather than strictly frozen — the same committee idea, one step looser on the freezing.)

CVPR'26DriveMoE: Mixture-of-Experts for Vision-Language-Action Model in End-to-End Autonomous Driving
Problem

A self-driving system takes in many camera views at once and must handle everything from routine lane-keeping to rare, sudden maneuvers. Feeding all the cameras through one pipeline drowns the model in redundant views, and asking one shared decision network to cover every behavior means it does none of them especially well.

Approach

DriveMoE puts a committee at two different stages. On the seeing side, a scene-specialized router picks which camera views actually matter for the current moment and ignores the rest, so the model spends its effort on the relevant angles instead of all of them. On the acting side, a skill-specialized set of experts is wired up so that different driving behaviors get their own module — one expert leans into steady lane following, another into obstacle avoidance, another into aggressive maneuvers — and a router activates the ones that fit the situation. Each expert can get good at its own skill without being blurred together with the others, and only the chosen ones run for a given scene.

Why it matters

It is the same shared-base-plus-specialists idea, split across perception and action: a router that routes by scene and a bench of experts that specialize by skill — the driving version of picking the right LoRA for the job.

CVPR'26MoECLIP: Patch-Specialized Experts for Zero-shot Anomaly Detection
Problem

Spotting defects in images the model has never seen before means adapting a general vision model like CLIP without wrecking its broad knowledge. The trouble is that most adaptations treat every part of an image the same, even though different regions — a smooth surface versus a textured edge — have genuinely different structure and need different handling.

Approach

MoECLIP drops a small committee of experts into CLIP's vision encoder, where each expert is a lightweight LoRA adjustment rather than a full heavy network — a thin add-on that tweaks the frozen model's behavior. A router examines each image patch and sends it to the specialist suited to that kind of region, so adaptation happens patch by patch instead of one-size-fits-all. To keep the experts from collapsing into copies of each other, the method pushes their outputs apart geometrically so each stays functionally distinct. The result is that different experts end up owning different sorts of image regions.

Why it matters

This is mixture-of-LoRA almost literally: a frozen CLIP base, a set of LoRA experts, and a router that picks one per image patch — capacity and specialization added on top without disturbing the pretrained model.

ROBOTICS'25MoE-Loco: Mixture of Experts for Multitask Locomotion
Problem

Teaching one legged robot a single control policy that walks over many terrains and switches between gaits runs into a tug-of-war: the training signals for different terrains and gaits pull the shared weights in conflicting directions. That conflict drags down both how well it moves and how efficiently it learns.

Approach

MoE-Loco gives the control policy a set of experts instead of one monolithic network, and lets a router hand each situation to the experts that fit it. Because different behaviors get routed to different experts, the conflicting training signals stop landing on the same weights — each expert can settle into its own locomotion style without being yanked around by the others. The authors find this specialization emerges on its own: distinct experts come to own distinct behaviors across both four-legged and two-legged walking. A useful side effect is that skills can be recombined and transferred, since they live in separable expert modules rather than tangled together.

Why it matters

Same skeleton in a robot's controller: shared framework, behavior-specialist experts, and a router that composes skills — the mixture-of-LoRA idea letting one policy hold many gaits without them interfering.

ROBOTICS'25Generalizing Motion Planners with Mixture of Experts for Autonomous Driving
Problem

Learned motion planners for self-driving often fail to carry over to new test conditions. Part of the reason is that during training the model collapses onto a narrow slice of behaviors and lets easy, common cases dominate the reward, so it never learns the full range it will actually meet on the road.

Approach

StateTransformer-2 builds the planner as a decoder-only transformer with a mixture-of-experts backbone, so instead of one shared stack of weights there is a bench of experts and routing that decides which handle a given situation. Spreading the work across experts stops the model from caving in to a single dominant mode and keeps the rarer, harder cases from being washed out by the easy ones. Crucially, this design keeps paying off as you scale it — feeding it billions of real urban driving scenarios and enlarging the model both yield steady, consistent gains rather than diminishing ones. The experts absorb the diversity that a single dense planner would have flattened.

Why it matters

It shows the payoff of the committee approach at scale: routing among specialists preserves behavioral variety that one shared model would blur — the same reason a mixture of LoRA experts generalizes where a single adapter overfits.

4 · Learn more without forgetting or fighting

Every learning system faces the same tug-of-war. If you push a model hard to master a new skill, the weights that held an old skill get overwritten and the old skill quietly dies — the classic forgetting problem. But if you freeze things to protect the old skill, the model can no longer bend enough to learn the new one. Push in the other direction and a second problem appears: when several skills share the very same weights, their training signals point in different directions and end up fighting, so each one drags the others down instead of helping. Both failures come from the same root — one pile of shared weights is being asked to hold everything at once, and there is no room for a skill to have a place of its own. The fix that runs through all of these papers is the mixture-of-LoRA skeleton: keep the big base model frozen, and give each task or skill its own small module. Because the modules are separate, a new one can be added without touching the old ones (nothing to forget), and two skills can each pull on their own module instead of wrestling over shared weights (nothing to fight over).

CVPR'26DGS: Dual Gradient and Semantic-Shift Guided Low-Rank Adaptation for Class Incremental Learning
Problem

A model is taught to recognize one batch of classes, then a second, then a third — and it must remember all of them. The trouble is that adapting to the new classes rewrites the knowledge that recognized the old ones, so the model forgets. Clamp the updates too tightly to prevent that and the model becomes too stiff to learn the new classes well — the stability-versus-plasticity bind.

Approach

DGS keeps a frozen backbone and learns each new batch of classes through small LoRA modules, so it never rewrites the base network. Its key move is a dual-gradient update: when training on a new task, it blends the raw task gradient with a version aligned to protect old knowledge, mixing the two by interpolation so the model stays plastic enough to learn yet stable enough to remember. It then fixes a subtler leak — as new classes arrive, the final classifier drifts — by estimating that semantic shift from stored prototype statistics and correcting the classifier, plus a patch-level loss that keeps features consistent across tasks. The result is a tunable balance rather than an all-or-nothing choice between learning and remembering.

Why it matters

This is the forgetting side of the skeleton made concrete: a frozen base plus per-task low-rank modules, with the extra insight that how you steer the gradient into those modules decides whether old skills survive.

CVPR'26On Token's Dilemma: Dynamic MoE with Drift-Aware Token Assignment for Continual Learning of Large Vision Language Models
Problem

A vision-language model is taught a stream of new tasks and must not forget the earlier ones. The popular fix is a mixture of experts — give each task its own expert module — but a hidden leak remains: when a new expert is added, the router that decides which expert handles each token starts pulling old-task tokens toward the shiny new expert, so old skills quietly degrade even though their expert is untouched.

Approach

LLaVA-DyMoE keeps a frozen base and LoRA experts inside the MoE layers, then repairs the router itself. It looks at each token and scores how strongly the old and new experts want it, and from that gap sorts tokens into three kinds: clearly-new, clearly-old, and ambiguous ones that could go either way. It then blocks ambiguous and old-belonging tokens from being routed to the new expert — masking those wrong choices outright — so the new expert only captures what is genuinely new. Two extra losses keep the experts cleanly separated and make sure the new expert is actually used. The payoff was a 7% accuracy gain and a 12% drop in forgetting across eight tasks.

Why it matters

It shows that isolated per-task modules only protect old skills if the router is disciplined too — otherwise the traffic cop, not the modules, becomes the place where forgetting sneaks back in.

CVPR'26Spectral Mixture-of-Experts for Continual Learning
Problem

When a model learns a sequence of tasks, later training overwrites earlier weights and the model forgets. The mixture-of-experts idea helps by letting different experts specialize, but plain routing often gives overlapping, muddy specialization, so experts still step on each other.

Approach

This work sharpens the specialization by splitting inputs along their spectral components — think of separating a signal into coarse, smooth structure versus fine detail — and letting different experts own different frequency bands or task subspaces. Because tasks are separated by a more meaningful axis than raw routing scores, each expert claims a cleaner slice of the problem, so new learning lands in an expert that isn't holding old knowledge. Specialized experts that don't overlap mean less overwriting and less interference as the task stream grows.

Why it matters

A reminder that the skeleton works best when the per-skill modules are carved along a natural boundary — here the frequency spectrum — so experts specialize cleanly instead of blurring into one another.

CVPR'26Keep It Frozen: Domain-Routed Conditional Residual Modulation for Multi-Domain Vision Transformers
Problem

One vision model has to work on ordinary photos and on hard medical scans full of shadows, blur, and noise. If you fully fine-tune it for the medical images, it gets worse at everything else — the domains fight over the same shared weights and the model can't be good at both at once.

Approach

As the title says, they keep the ViT backbone frozen and never let the domains touch it. A tiny domain router reads each image and predicts how much it looks like each domain, and a small synthesizer turns that judgment into low-rank residuals — nudges added to selected layers and to the attention — that reshape the frozen features for the domain at hand. So a medical scan and a natural photo flow through the same base but get different lightweight adjustments, chosen per image, instead of competing for one set of weights. A two-level training loop tunes those adjustments per task while keeping the router general, giving gains on ultrasound, CT, and MRI without hurting natural images — at very low cost.

Why it matters

This is the anti-fighting side of the skeleton in its purest form: freeze the shared base, and hand each domain its own small, routed module so the domains never overwrite each other.

CVPR'26FAAR: Efficient Frequency-Aware Multi-Task Fine-Tuning via Automatic Rank Selection
Problem

Train one model to do several dense tasks at once — segmentation, depth, surface normals — and two headaches appear. The tasks fight over shared adapter weights (negative transfer), and you have to guess how big each task's adapter should be, layer by layer, which is expensive and usually wrong.

Approach

FAAR puts small LoRA-style adapters on a frozen backbone and then lets the training decide their size automatically: each epoch it scores how much each rank component actually matters and prunes away the ones that don't, shrinking adapters from rank 64 down to about 5 — capacity flows to where it's needed instead of being set by hand. To manage the sharing-versus-conflict tension, it works in the frequency domain: each task head gets a spectral filter that captures its own spatial patterns, while a cross-task consensus loss lines up the frequency components the tasks genuinely share. So tasks agree on common structure but keep their private specializations, reaching state-of-the-art with only 3.38M added parameters.

Why it matters

It answers the fighting problem by letting each task keep a right-sized private module while explicitly aligning only what should be shared — the mixture-of-LoRA idea with the module budget tuned automatically.

ROBOTICS'25Expert-Enhanced Masked Point Modeling for Point Cloud Self-Supervised Learning
Problem

Self-supervised pre-training on 3D point clouds is supposed to hand downstream tasks a strong head start, but it backfires when the pre-training data looks different from the target data — the mismatched knowledge actively hurts instead of helps, a case of negative transfer where one shared backbone can't serve every domain.

Approach

The fix is to stop asking one backbone to hold everything. After each backbone block they insert a sparse mixture-of-experts layer, and a router sends each token to a domain-specific expert during pre-training, so different kinds of shape data are absorbed by different experts rather than being forced into shared weights. When it's time to fine-tune, the backbone is frozen and only these expert layers are updated — the downstream task simply picks up the experts that fit it and leaves the rest alone. That combination beat a strong baseline by up to 5.86% while training only about 12% as many parameters.

Why it matters

A clean robotics-side instance of the whole skeleton: a frozen backbone plus small trainable experts, where isolating skills into separate modules turns harmful transfer into a menu you can choose from.

5 · Combine specialists after training

Suppose you have already trained a shelf of specialists, one small LoRA adapter per skill, per task, or per taste. The dream is to get all of them at once without a live traffic-cop deciding which to switch on for every input. The obvious move is to just add the adapters together into a single model. It rarely works. Each adapter was trained alone, so it grabbed whatever directions in the weights helped its task and ignored the rest; when you sum them, those private pulls collide and cancel, and a merged model can score near zero even though each part worked fine on its own. The papers here are the offline alternatives to a live gate: some fix the collision and fold everyone into one set of weights, and others skip merging entirely by generating the right adapter from a task description or retrieving it from a library. In every case the combining rule is decided before or at load time, not by a router running on each token.
CVPR'26MergeVLA: Cross-Skill Model Merging Toward a Generalist Vision-Language-Action Agent
Problem
Robot policies fine-tuned separately on individual manipulation skills succeed on their own, but when you merge them with standard averaging the combined model drops to near-zero success. Nobody had pinned down why the pieces refuse to fuse.
Approach
The authors dissect the failure and find two culprits. First, LoRA parameter selfishness: when merging four tasks, over 75 percent of the adapter parameters are claimed by just one task, so summing them buries each skill under three competitors it never agreed with. Second, the action decoder's self-attention layers pile up task-specific dependencies that grow worse in deeper layers, making those weights impossible to average. The fix is to only keep, per task, the parameters that are both strong and point the same way as the merged direction (a consistency mask), and to strip self-attention out of the action expert so nearly all of it can be safely weight-averaged, leaving only the last block or two task-specific. When the task is unknown at test time, a lightweight check projects the input onto each task's learned subspace and picks the best match for the whole episode.
Why it matters
This is the clearest diagnosis of why naive LoRA merging interferes, and its answer is a static combine plus an offline per-episode selector rather than a live per-token gate.
CVPR'26Preference-Aligned LoRA Merging: Preserving Subspace Coverage and Addressing Directional Anisotropy
Problem
Different LoRA adapters each learn useful edits, but they live in different corners of the weight space. Plain averaging smears those distinct corners together and loses the specialized information each adapter carried.
Approach
The insight is that adapters are complementary: each one covers a subspace the others do not, and each has a preferred direction it pushes hardest. Simple averaging ignores both facts, so it dilutes the coverage and lets the loudest directions dominate. This method uses spectral analysis to identify the important subspaces each adapter occupies and merges the weights so that all of that coverage survives, while correcting for the fact that adaptation directions are unevenly stretched (the anisotropy). The result is a merged model that keeps more of what made each specialist good instead of collapsing them toward a bland average.
Why it matters
It is a static merge rule that treats interference as a geometry problem, folding many adapters into one set of weights with no gate deciding anything at run time.
CVPR'26MapReduce LoRA: Advancing the Pareto Front in Multi-Preference Optimization for Generative Models
Problem
Good generation has to satisfy several tastes at once, prompt alignment, aesthetics, correct text rendering, but optimizing them jointly causes an alignment tax where pushing one down drags another. Weighted-mixture training tends to get hijacked by whichever objective is easiest to improve.
Approach
Borrowing the map-reduce pattern, the method trains one LoRA expert per reward signal fully in parallel (the Map step), so each specialist gets to fully satisfy its own taste without a competing objective pulling it off course. Then it combines them in a Reduce step, but not by a single naive sum: it iteratively merges the experts with user-set interpolation weights and refolds the blend back into the base model, so the trade-off can be tuned rather than left to chance. A companion trick learns per-reward token embeddings that can be composed at inference for further control. Training experts in isolation and merging afterward sidesteps the tug-of-war that plagues joint multi-objective training.
Why it matters
It is the parallel-train-then-merge recipe made explicit: many reward-specialist adapters folded offline into one model, with the combine ratio as a dial rather than a live router.
CVPR'26Label-Free Cross-Task LoRA Merging with Null-Space Compression
Problem
Most merging tricks assume all the tasks are the same shape, typically all classification, and quietly break when you try to fuse adapters that span different job types like classification and regression together. Worse, many need labeled data to figure out how much weight each adapter should get.
Approach
The key observation is that LoRA fine-tuning systematically squeezes each task's information into a low-dimensional subspace, and the geometry of that subspace tells you how much each adapter should count, no labels and no peeking at task outputs required. Null-Space Compression sets the merge weights purely from this adapter geometry, so it is label-free and output-agnostic. Because it reads the structure rather than the task type, it can combine adapters that produce completely different kinds of outputs without them stepping on each other. It is validated by merging six adapters across language-inference and vision-language benchmarks.
Why it matters
It is a static combine rule that decides the merge coefficients from adapter geometry alone, an offline alternative to a live gate that needs neither labels nor a running router.
CVPR'26SG-LoRA: Semantic-guided LoRA Parameters Generation
Problem
You have a repository of trained LoRA experts, but a brand-new task shows up with no data to fine-tune on. Prior approaches either blend the existing adapters with fixed hand-set weights or train a generator that only works for tasks it saw before.
Approach
Instead of merging or retraining, SG-LoRA generates a fresh adapter for the new task from its text description. It places the new task and the known expert tasks in a shared CLIP text space and measures which experts are semantically nearest, using a top-K softmax over those similarities. That semantic proximity forms a prior over what the new adapter's weights should look like, and a conditional variational autoencoder samples actual LoRA parameters from that prior. Because it samples rather than deterministically averages, it can produce diverse adapters for open-world tasks it never trained on, with no raw task data needed.
Why it matters
This is the generate-the-adapter shortcut: rather than storing every specialist and routing among them, it synthesizes the right one on demand from a description, an offline combine driven by meaning instead of a live gate.
CVPR'26LoFA: Learning to Predict Personalized Prior for Fast Adaptation of Diffusion Models
Problem
Personalizing a big image or video diffusion model to a new subject normally means an expensive per-subject LoRA fine-tune. Hypernetworks that try to skip this by predicting the whole adapter waste effort guessing weights that do not matter for the concept.
Approach
LoFA predicts the adapter instead of training it, but does so in two focused stages. First it learns a response map: a quick pass flags which weight matrices actually move for a given concept (those that shift by more than about two percent), producing a binary mask of the positions that matter. Then a hypernetwork, fed the base weights, the subject's images, and that response map, predicts LoRA weights only for the positions the mask lit up, rather than spraying capacity everywhere. Because the network focuses its budget on the concept-relevant weights, it delivers a personalized adapter in under four seconds instead of the minutes an optimization run would take.
Why it matters
It replaces per-task training with a hypernetwork that predicts the adapter outright, the fast offline shortcut to producing the right specialist without any live routing.
CVPR'26CARLoS: Retrieval via Concise Assessment Representation of LoRAs at Scale
Problem
Community sites now host thousands of LoRAs, but their creator descriptions are thin and unreliable and popularity says little about behavior, so users pick by trial and error. When the right specialist already exists, the real problem is finding it.
Approach
CARLoS skips both merging and generating and simply retrieves the adapter you want. Rather than trusting metadata, it characterizes each of over 650 real community LoRAs by what it actually does: it runs the adapter across many prompts and seeds and summarizes the visual effect with three numbers in CLIP space, a Direction (the semantic shift it produces), a Strength (how big that shift is), and a Consistency (how stable it is across prompts). A text query is then matched against these behavioral fingerprints, and adapters that are too strong or too erratic get filtered out. Because the representation is measured, not described, retrieval works even when the creator wrote nothing useful.
Why it matters
It is the retrieval branch of combining specialists: when the perfect adapter already lives in a huge library, index them by behavior and fetch the match, no merge and no live gate at all.

6 · Make the router trustworthy

Every mixture of experts hangs on one small, fragile piece: the router that decides, for each input, which experts do the work. It is the beating heart of the whole system, and left to itself it does not beat steadily. A router trained only to minimize the task loss tends to collapse — it keeps picking the same handful of favorite experts, starves the rest until they never learn anything useful, and leaves you paying for capacity you never touch. The usual patch is to bolt on a load-balancing loss that punishes the router for playing favorites, but that pressure fights the task itself: spread the work too evenly and experts stop specializing, so accuracy slips. These three papers step back and rebuild the router so it stays honest on its own — by scoring experts against their own internal structure, by borrowing a steadier teacher to imitate, and by giving experts distinct starting points so they never became clones in the first place. The goal is a router whose choices are stable across training, actually spread across the experts, and legible enough that you can see why a given expert was chosen.
CVPR'26ERMoE: Eigen-Reparameterized Mixture-of-Experts for Stable Routing and Interpretable Specialization
Problem
A normal router learns a separate set of gating logits — scores it invents from scratch to rank the experts — and these scores drift out of sync with what the experts have actually become good at. The result is the familiar trio of failures: unstable routing, whole experts left idle, and a few overloaded ones dragging everything down. The standard fix, a load-balancing penalty, flattens the imbalance but blunts specialization and costs accuracy, and probes show the router still routinely sends tokens to the wrong expert.
Approach
ERMoE throws out the learned gating scores entirely. It rewrites each expert in its own orthonormal eigenbasis — a clean set of directions that describes what that expert represents — and then routes by simply measuring how well an input aligns with each expert's basis, using cosine similarity. Because the routing score is read directly off the expert's own structure rather than guessed by a separate network, the choice can never fall out of step with the expert, and there is nothing left to balance: no gating logits, no auxiliary load-balancing loss. Keeping the experts near-orthogonal also stops them from quietly converging into redundant copies of each other. On ImageNet and image-text retrieval it hits state-of-the-art with noticeably flatter expert loads, and its brain-imaging variant improves brain-age prediction by over 7% with specializations you can actually interpret anatomically.
Why it matters
This is a direct answer to the expert-collapse and load-imbalance failure our lab reproduced in the ablation: instead of fighting a naive router with a balancing loss that hurts accuracy, ERMoE makes the routing score be the expert's structure, so even load emerges for free rather than being forced.
CVPR'26Teacher-Guided Routing for Sparse Vision Mixture-of-Experts
Problem
In a sparse mixture, the router only ever hears back from the experts it picked — the unpicked routes send no gradient — so it is learning to choose while blind to most of its options. This gradient blocking makes the router's decisions lurch around from step to step, and it slides into expert collapse, forever reinforcing the experts it happened to favor early on.
Approach
TGR-MoE hands the sparse router a steadier role model. It takes a pretrained dense teacher — a model where every expert-like path is always active and therefore always gets feedback — and builds a teacher router from its internal features, trained with load-balancing and entropy terms to produce a calm, well-spread routing distribution. The sparse student router is then taught to imitate that distribution via a KL-divergence loss, so it inherits a global sense of which expert should handle what instead of discovering it from the trickle of gradients it sees on its own. This damps the step-to-step fluctuation and keeps expert assignments semantically consistent over the course of training. On ImageNet and downstream tasks it beats standard vision MoE across model sizes, and crucially it keeps improving as you add experts, right where the ordinary router saturates and collapse sets in.
Why it matters
It attacks the same expert-collapse dynamic from the lab's ablation, but from a different angle than a balancing penalty: rather than punishing the router after it misbehaves, it copies a teacher that was never starved of feedback, so the router spreads across experts because it learned to from the start.
CVPR'26Enhancing Mixture-of-Experts Specialization via Cluster-Aware Upcycling
Problem
A cheap way to build a mixture is upcycling: copy a trained dense model's weights into every expert and start there. But that means all the experts begin life as identical twins, and the fresh router has no reason to prefer one over another — so early on they route almost at random and the experts have no distinct basis to specialize from, the seed of collapse.
Approach
Cluster-Aware Upcycling breaks that symmetry before training even begins. It runs the dense model's own activations through spherical k-means to find semantic clusters in what the model already sees, then initializes each expert from the structure of one cluster using a truncated SVD of that subspace — so each expert starts out already leaning toward a different kind of input. The router is not left to guess either: its weights are seeded with the cluster centroids, giving it a meaningful map of which expert owns which region of the input space from step one. An expert-ensemble self-distillation loss then steadies training for the ambiguous tokens the router is unsure about. The payoff is measurably lower similarity between experts, more disentangled representations, and confident, non-random routing — consistently beating plain upcycling on CLIP zero-shot and few-shot benchmarks.
Why it matters
This gets at the root cause behind the collapse our ablation showed: identical experts and a clueless router invite the mixture to fold onto a few favorites, so giving each expert a distinct starting specialty and the router a real prior heads off imbalance before any balancing loss is needed.

7 · Mixture-of-LoRA proper — the keystones

These three papers are the reason this whole idea has a name. Each one, independently, arrives at the exact same skeleton: take a big frozen model, hang a pool of small LoRA specialists off it, and let a small learned gate decide which specialists to lean on for each input. Not one adapter bolted on. Not a static merge of several adapters into one fixed blend. A living pool plus a router that chooses — the same thing the hands-on lab builds by hand. What makes them the keystones is that they land on this pattern from three completely different starting points. TAS-LoRA reaches it while searching for the best Transformer shape, using a router to hand out shared LoRA experts to billions of candidate architectures. MedQwen reaches it while trying to make a medical vision-language model stop tripping over conflicting datasets, using a router driven by the math of the weight matrices themselves. MoRE reaches it inside a walking robot, using sparsely-activated LoRA experts as the body of a reinforcement-learning value function. When an idea shows up unprompted in architecture search, in medicine, and in robotics, that is the signal it is a real structural primitive and not a trick — and that is what these keystones prove.

CVPR'26TAS-LoRA: Transformer Architecture Search with Mixture-of-LoRA Experts
Problem

When you want the best Transformer for a job, one popular trick is to train one giant supernet that secretly contains billions of smaller networks inside it, then read out the best small one. The catch is that all those small networks share the same weights, so when you train them together their gradients get averaged into one shared pile. The result is that every small network ends up learning the same bland, generic features — the paper measures this directly and finds different subnetworks are about 93 percent identical in what they learn, versus only 44 percent when trained separately. The authors call this feature collapse: the shared weights flatten away exactly the distinctive character that made each candidate architecture worth considering in the first place.

Approach

The fix is to give each candidate architecture its own small dose of specialization on top of the shared base — and that dose is LoRA. But there are billions of candidate configurations, so you cannot give each one its own private LoRA. So instead they keep a small pool of LoRA experts and add a lightweight router that, given a candidate architecture, decides how much to weight each expert. The router is a little LSTM that reads the architecture's description — how many attention heads, the MLP ratio, the embedding width, the depth — and outputs a blend over the experts, which is exactly the frozen-base-plus-shared-specialists-plus-learned-gate skeleton, with the twist that the gate is conditioned on the network shape rather than on a token. They seed the router so different experts start out attached to different blocks of the network, which nudges the experts to diversify early. And because LoRA folds back into the base weights, once search is done the chosen subnetwork carries no extra cost at inference.

Why it matters

This is the paper that literally puts the words Mixture-of-LoRA-Experts on the pattern, and it is the cleanest statement of why the pattern exists: a shared base gives you efficiency, but shared weights alone erase specialization, so you re-inject specialization through a small pool of LoRA experts and a learned router that picks among them. The measured drop in feature similarity — from 0.93 back down toward the 0.44 you would get from fully independent training — is direct evidence that the router-plus-experts genuinely restored the per-case specialness that a single shared model had destroyed. It is the purest demonstration that mixture-of-LoRA is not decoration but the mechanism that buys you specialization and sharing at the same time.

CVPR'26Sparse Spectral LoRA: Routed Experts for Medical VLMs
Problem

General vision-language models are impressive on everyday images but wobble in medicine, where the training signal is a messy patchwork stitched together from many different clinical datasets. Those datasets pull in different directions, so training on all of them at once causes cross-dataset interference — learning to read one kind of scan quietly degrades another — and the final model becomes weirdly sensitive to exactly how the datasets were mixed. In real hospitals the data also arrives in a stream over time, so naively continuing to train just makes the interference worse. The authors want a medical model, which they call MedQwen, that can absorb this heterogeneous supervision without the pieces fighting each other, and without paying the cost of fully retraining the base.

Approach

Their move is a mixture of low-rank experts where the experts and the router are both defined by the spectrum of the weight matrix — that is, by its singular value decomposition, the standard way to break a matrix into ranked directions from strongest to weakest. They initialize each LoRA expert from a non-overlapping slice of that spectrum, so by construction the experts cover different directions of the weight space and do not step on one another, which is what tames the interference. A router then sparsely selects which of these spectral experts to activate for a given input, so only the relevant specialists fire — the frozen base, a pool of complementary LoRA experts, and a learned gate over them. The theoretical piece is a scaling rule: they prove how to scale these low-rank updates so that the cheap routed-LoRA mixture behaves like a full-rank, fully fine-tuned mixture-of-experts would, without altering the base architecture at all. The payoff is up to a 5 percent improvement.

Why it matters

This paper matters because it derives the mixture-of-LoRA pattern from the mathematics of the weights rather than assuming it. By carving experts out of non-overlapping spectral segments, it gives a principled answer to a question the pattern usually handles by luck — how do you make the experts actually different from each other? — and its scaling rule is a formal argument that a routed pool of tiny LoRA experts can stand in for an expensive full-rank mixture. It is the keystone that shows the frozen-base-plus-specialists-plus-gate skeleton is not just a convenient hack but a low-rank approximation of a much heavier model, with the theory to back the claim, proven where it is hardest to fake: high-stakes medical imaging.

ROBOTICS'25MoRE: Unlocking Scalability in Reinforcement Learning for Quadruped Vision-Language-Action Models
Problem

The goal is a four-legged robot driven by a large model that can see, read an instruction, and act — a vision-language-action model. Teaching one of these is hard because you have to fine-tune a huge multimodal brain on a pile of robot experience that is diverse and of very mixed quality, some of it good demonstrations and much of it mediocre. Trained the obvious way, the different skills interfere with each other and the data gets used inefficiently, so the model neither scales up cleanly nor generalizes to situations it did not see in training. The authors want to fine-tune this big model across six different quadruped skills at once without the skills fighting, and without demanding clean, curated data.

Approach

MoRE takes the dense multimodal language model and inserts multiple LoRA modules into it as sparsely-activated experts — for any given input only a few of the LoRA experts switch on, and a router decides which ones. That is precisely the mixture-of-LoRA skeleton: a shared frozen backbone, a pool of small LoRA specialists, and a gate that routes. The striking part is how they train it: rather than plain supervised imitation, they treat the whole assembled model — base plus routed LoRA experts — as a reinforcement-learning Q-function, a network that scores how good an action is, and train it with RL on interaction data that was collected automatically and is deliberately mixed in quality. Casting it as a value function is what lets the system learn productively from imperfect data instead of being dragged down by it, and the sparse routing is what lets six distinct skills coexist in one model without collapsing into each other.

Why it matters

This is the pattern proving it survives contact with the physical world. It shows the same frozen-base-plus-LoRA-pool-plus-router structure working not as a classifier or a text model but as the beating heart of a reinforcement-learning controller for a walking robot. The sparse activation is what unlocks the scalability in the title: it is the mechanism that lets one model hold six quadruped skills at once and still generalize to out-of-distribution scenarios from messy offline data. Across NAS and medicine the pattern bought clean specialization; here it buys something even more demanding — the ability to scale a single embodied agent across many skills — which is the strongest evidence that mixture-of-LoRA is a general-purpose way to grow a model's capabilities rather than a narrow fine-tuning tweak.

Reading it whole. Top to bottom, the themes trace the same arc the why page argued: adapt one giant, then compose many specialists, then put the committee inside the model, then keep the specialists from forgetting or fighting, then combine them after the fact, then make the router itself trustworthy — and finally the keystones where all of it lands as true mixture-of-LoRA. The papers differ in domain; the skeleton underneath them does not.

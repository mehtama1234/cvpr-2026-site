EgoMind: Activating Spatial Cognition through Linguistic Reasoning in MLLMs ⌘

The problem. Modern vision-language models can look at a single photo and describe it fluently, but they fall apart when asked to reason about space across a series of frames — for example, watching video from a person walking through rooms and answering "was the couch to the left or right of the door I passed?" Getting machines to do this usually means feeding them costly extras: 3D depth maps, geometric measurements, and hand-labeled multimodal data. That expense keeps good spatial reasoning out of reach for the very applications that need it most, like robots and AR glasses that must understand where things are relative to a moving viewpoint. The core difficulty is that ordinary 2D approaches never build a mental thread connecting what was seen in one frame to what is seen in the next.

The approach. EgoMind teaches the model to reason about space using words instead of geometry — it thinks its way through a scene rather than measuring it. First, a "role-play caption" step has the model pretend it is a person physically walking through the environment, narrating not just what each frame shows but how the viewpoint shifts from one frame to the next, which stitches separate snapshots into one coherent picture of the whole space. Then a "progressive spatial analysis" step works outward like a spotlight: it locates the object the question asks about, expands attention to nearby objects, and finally reasons about how they sit relative to each other. This is a structured chain of thought, and the whole thing is trained cheaply — about 5,000 automatically generated fine-tuning examples plus 20,000 reinforcement-learning examples, with no human annotation and no 3D supervision.

What's unique. The key insight is that the "glue" connecting frames across a moving viewpoint doesn't have to be explicit 3D geometry — it can be language itself, if the model is coached to narrate the transitions between viewpoints as an embodied agent would. Where prior work leaned on expensive geometric priors to bridge frames, EgoMind is the first to show that a carefully staged linguistic chain of thought can recover those cross-frame spatial links and implicit relationships on its own, reaching competitive results across four spatial benchmarks with only a small pile of auto-generated data.

Grounding Everything in Tokens for Multimodal Large Language Models

The problem. When you show a chatbot a picture, it mostly "sees" the whole image as one blurry impression rather than knowing exactly where things are. That's fine for describing a scene, but it breaks down the moment a task demands precision: point to the third cup on the shelf, edit just the dog's collar, tell a robot which drawer to open. The model can talk about objects but can't reliably tie the words it's saying to specific spots in the image. This gap blocks a whole class of useful applications where a machine has to act on, not just chat about, what it's looking at.

The approach. Their idea is to make location part of the model's vocabulary. Normally a language model works with word tokens; here they add "spatial tokens" that stand for specific patches or regions of an image, effectively giving each piece of the picture its own address that the model can refer to. Text tokens and these spatial tokens are trained together, and a matching component learns which words go with which regions, so a language token like "the red mug" can be pinned to the actual patch where the mug lives. Crucially, this all happens inside the ordinary transformer machinery the model already uses, so the model gains fine-grained pointing and region-description ability without being rebuilt around a special detection head.

What's unique. The clever move is treating precise location as just another kind of token the model already knows how to reason over, rather than bolting on a separate localization system. That lets grounding happen at the level of individual words linked to individual regions, densely, while keeping the standard architecture untouched and easy to adopt. They frame this as the first setup to achieve token-level spatial grounding this way, whereas most prior multimodal models kept image understanding holistic and location handling external.

Rationale-Enhanced Decoding for Multi-modal Chain-of-Thought

The problem. When a vision-language model is asked to "think step by step" about an image, it writes out a chain of reasoning and then gives an answer. The hidden assumption is that the model actually reads its own reasoning and lets it steer the final answer. This paper shows that assumption is often false: the model writes plausible reasoning, then largely ignores it when committing to an answer, so the reasoning is more decoration than cause. That matters because the whole appeal of step-by-step reasoning is that it should make answers more grounded and trustworthy, and if the model isn't really using those steps, we get the appearance of careful thinking without the substance.

The approach. Instead of retraining the model to pay attention to its reasoning, they change only how the answer is produced at the moment of generation. For each next word, they compute two separate opinions from the same model: one based on looking at the image, and one based on reading the generated reasoning text. They then combine these two opinions by multiplying them together before picking the word, so a candidate answer only scores high if both the image and the reasoning support it. This forces the final answer to be anchored simultaneously in what's actually in the picture and in the chain of reasoning the model produced, rather than letting either one be quietly dropped. It's a plug-in tweak to the decoding step that requires no extra training and works on top of existing models.

What's unique. The key move is reframing the vague goal of "make the model respect its own reasoning" as a precise, controllable balancing act between two probability signals, and then enforcing that balance purely at generation time by multiplying an image-conditioned and a rationale-conditioned distribution. This gives a guarantee that the answer stays tied to both sources, whereas prior work simply trusted that step-by-step prompting would naturally produce grounded answers. It is also the first to explicitly diagnose that these models discard rationale content, and to fix it without any additional training data or fine-tuning.

Grounded Chain-of-Thought for Multimodal Large Language Models

The problem. When a vision-language model answers a question about a picture, we usually only check whether its final answer is right. But a right answer can be a lucky guess or a memorized pattern rather than proof the model actually looked at the image. This paper confronts that gap: models routinely "hallucinate," describing things that aren't there, and reason poorly about where objects are and how they relate in space. That matters because the moment we hand these models real jobs, like guiding a robot through a room, an answer that is right by accident but not grounded in what the camera sees becomes dangerous.

The approach. The authors force the model to show its work in a way that can be checked against the image itself. Instead of just producing an answer, the model must break a question into reasoning steps and, at each step, point to the exact region of the image it is relying on by giving pixel coordinates. This turns the invisible chain of thought into a grounded chain of thought that can be audited. They build a 1,200-example benchmark for this task and score three separate things: is the final answer correct, are the pointed-to regions correct, and, crucially, does the answer actually follow from the regions the model claimed to be looking at. That third measure, consistency, is what catches a model that says the right words while pointing at the wrong place, or getting the words right for reasons unrelated to what it perceived.

What's unique. The key insight is that answer accuracy and perceptual grounding are separate skills that most evaluations conflate, and measuring the consistency between them exposes hidden hallucination. Running this on twelve leading models, they find many score well on answers and on grounding individually yet fail to connect the two, meaning the two abilities are decoupled, and that this failure does not shrink as models get bigger. This is the first systematic way to diagnose whether a multimodal model's reasoning is genuinely anchored in the image rather than just measuring whether it landed on the right answer.

Saliency-R1: Enforcing Interpretable and Faithful Vision-language Reasoning via Saliency-map Alignment

The problem. When a vision-language model answers a question about a picture, it writes out a chain of reasoning as if it were looking at the image, but often it is really just parroting what usually goes with such questions in text, ignoring what is actually in front of it. This is why these models confidently describe things that aren't there. The deeper trouble is that there's no guarantee the words a model produces have anything to do with the parts of the image it internally paid attention to, so you can't trust its explanation or its answer.

The approach. The idea is to tie a model's spoken reasoning to where its eyes actually go, and to enforce that tie during training. They first cheaply read out where the model is looking: instead of running extra passes, they trace each region's contribution to the final answer straight from the internal signals the model already computes, and they route this through the reasoning tokens so it reflects the path the thought actually took. Then they train the model with a reward system that pays it not only for getting the answer right and formatting it correctly, but for having its visual attention land inside the specific image region its own reasoning claims to be about. Over many rounds the model learns to look where it says it is looking, so interpretability and correctness reinforce each other. At test time it needs no region labels and produces these attention maps on the fly.

What's unique. The clever move is turning a model's own attention into a training reward: rather than measuring grounding after the fact or bolting on a separate explanation tool, they make "did you actually look where you said you looked" a scored objective inside reinforcement learning, so the model is directly pushed to be faithful. This is paired with a saliency readout that costs no extra computation because it is decoded from signals already present, and that is funneled specifically through the reasoning tokens so it captures the causal chain of thought rather than raw attention.

SketchVL: Policy Optimization via Fine-Grained Credit Assignment for Chart Understanding and More

The problem. When a machine works through a chart in several steps — crop this region, read that tick mark, match this bar to its legend — a single small slip early on can quietly wreck everything that follows. The usual way to teach such models by trial and error only tells them whether the final answer was right or wrong, and then treats every step in the attempt as equally to blame or equally to praise. That is unfair and misleading: a right answer can be reached through a sloppy step that got lucky, and a wrong answer can contain mostly good reasoning ruined by one bad move. Without knowing which specific step deserves credit or blame, the model learns slowly and reinforces the wrong habits.

The approach. The model reasons by literally drawing on the picture: at each step it marks the chart — a crop box, a pointer to a tick, a highlight on a bar — and feeds that annotated image back to itself as it continues, so its intermediate thinking becomes visible actions rather than hidden thoughts. A separate scoring model, trained to judge these drawing actions one at a time, rates each individual step for how well it matched what that step was supposed to accomplish. The training method then splits the final reward apart and hands out credit or blame step by step according to those per-action scores, instead of smearing one uniform verdict across the whole attempt. So a good step inside a failed answer still gets rewarded, and a bad step inside a lucky success still gets penalized — the model learns from the parts, not just the outcome.

What's unique. The core insight is turning invisible reasoning into concrete, gradeable visual actions — drawings on the image — so that each step can be scored on its own and credit can be assigned precisely, rather than judging only the final answer as almost all reinforcement-trained multimodal models do. Bringing step-level process rewards into visual reasoning, where the "steps" are marks the model makes on the picture and can be checked individually, is what sets it apart and yields a measurable average gain across chart, natural-image, and math tasks.

MR-RAG: Multimodal Relevance-Aware Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Medical Visual Question Answering

The problem. General-purpose AI systems that look at images and answer questions do not know medicine well enough to be trusted in a clinic. A common fix is to let the model consult an external library of reference cases and pull in relevant material before answering. But in the medical setting this lookup breaks down two ways: the search that finds reference cases judges them on a single crude notion of similarity, missing the layered meaning in a scan-plus-report pair, and once material is fetched the model reads every retrieved document as if equally trustworthy, so a few off-target hits drown out the one case that actually matters. When the stakes are a diagnosis, being led astray by irrelevant retrieved text is not a minor annoyance.

The approach. MR-RAG fixes both the fetch step and the read step. For fetching, instead of ranking reference cases by one similarity score, it compares along several channels at once: image-to-image, text-to-text, and image-to-text across the two, using a small trainable adapter to fuse these signals so the cases it pulls are genuinely aligned with the question. For reading, it computes how relevant each retrieved document really is and then reaches inside the model's attention, turning up the influence of the useful documents and turning down the ones that don't fit, rather than letting the model weigh them all evenly. So relevance is not just used to pick documents but is carried forward as a control knob during answer generation.

What's unique. The core insight is that retrieval relevance should not stop at the moment of retrieval. Prior systems fetch documents and then hand them off blindly, so a good retrieval can still be spoiled by treating a weak match as if it were strong. MR-RAG makes the same multimodal relevance signal do double duty, once to select cases and again to physically steer how much attention each selected case gets while the model composes its answer, closing the gap between finding the right evidence and actually using it.

ACoT-VLA: Action Chain-of-Thought for Vision-Language-Action Models

The problem. Robots that take a camera image and a plain-language instruction and try to produce the right motion tend to jump straight from "what I see" to "what to do," with nothing in between. That works for a single grab-and-place, but it breaks down on tasks that have several dependent steps, because the model has no place to work out the order of operations or what the intermediate goals even are. The result is brittle behavior on anything that requires a plan rather than a reflex, which is exactly the kind of task real-world robots need to handle.

The approach. The idea borrows the trick that made language models better at hard questions: instead of demanding an answer in one leap, let the model "think out loud" through intermediate steps first. Here, before the robot commits to a motor command, the model reasons in language about how to break the task apart and what the in-between goals are, then uses that reasoning to guide the actual action it outputs. So visual understanding and language reasoning are woven together, and the chain of thought is about actions and sub-goals rather than just words, giving the model a scratchpad to lay out a multi-step plan before moving.

What's unique. The clever move is carrying chain-of-thought reasoning across the boundary from text into physical action, so the "steps" being reasoned about are task decompositions and intermediate motions rather than sentences. Prior vision-language-action models mapped perception directly to control with no explicit reasoning layer; this work inserts that deliberate step-by-step planning stage inside the action pipeline itself, which the analysis credits as the first application of chain-of-thought to VLA models.

Deeper Thought, Weaker Aim: Understanding and Mitigating Perceptual Impairment during Deep Reasoning ⌘

The problem. When a vision-language model is asked to think hard about an image, it does something counterintuitive: the longer it reasons, the worse it actually sees. As the model unspools a long chain of step-by-step thoughts, its grip on the actual pixels slips, and it starts making claims that no longer match what is in front of it. This matters because the whole promise of these systems is to combine careful thinking with accurate looking, yet in practice the two pull against each other, so buying more reasoning silently costs you perception.

The approach. The authors first pin down the phenomenon rather than assume it: they trace how a model's attention drifts away from the image and toward its own accumulating text as a reasoning chain gets longer, and they measure the resulting drop in visual accuracy. That gives them a concrete diagnosis: deep reasoning steals the model's focus from the picture. Their fix works on that same mechanism, steering attention back onto the visual input and preserving the original image features so they stay available deep into the reasoning process instead of being crowded out by the growing pile of intermediate thoughts. The result is a model that can keep thinking in long chains while still keeping its eyes on the evidence.

What's unique. The clever move is naming and quantifying a specific failure mode most work had lumped into generic error: that reasoning depth and perceptual sharpness are in direct tension, with more of one buying less of the other. Where prior efforts chased longer, richer reasoning as an unqualified good, this paper shows that improvement has a hidden perceptual tax and then targets the exact attention-drift mechanism causing it, rather than just adding more compute or training data.

IF-Prune: Information-Flow Guided Token Pruning for Efficient Vision-Language Models ⌘

The problem. Modern vision-language models don't look at a picture as one small snapshot anymore; they chop it into thousands of little patches so they can read fine detail like text on a chart or a line in a document. But every one of those patches becomes a token the model has to process, and the cost of thinking grows sharply with the number of tokens, making these models slow and expensive to run. Most of those patches carry nothing useful for the question being asked, so the obvious fix is to throw the useless ones away before the big model does its heavy reasoning. The hard part is deciding which patches are safe to discard without accidentally cutting the one that held the answer.

The approach. The usual trick is to run a small, cheap model first and watch where its attention lands, treating the patches it looks at hardest as the important ones to keep. IF-Prune argues that signal is noisy and unreliable, especially when the small model simply doesn't know enough to handle a tricky question, so its glances point in the wrong direction. Instead, they flip the question around: rather than trying to spot the useful patches, they train the small model to recognize the useless ones, learning what a genuinely non-informative patch tends to look like given the question. They borrow an idea from information theory called the information bottleneck, which is about keeping only what's relevant and squeezing out the rest, and turn it into a scoring rule. Concretely, they compare each patch against a learned baseline of what an uninformative patch would be, and the further a patch is from that baseline, the more it likely matters; this whole judgment is produced in a single fast forward pass and plays nicely with speed-optimized attention libraries.

What's unique. The clever inversion is measuring importance by how much a token deviates from a learned model of worthlessness rather than by how much attention it attracts, giving a principled, query-aware score instead of an attention heuristic that breaks down on hard questions. It is framed as the first probabilistic, information-theoretic treatment of token pruning, casting the whole thing as an inference problem rather than a rule of thumb, and it does this without ever needing the expensive large model's attention weights to guide the cut.

CORE: Compact Object-centric REpresentations as a New Paradigm for Token Merging in LVLMs ⌘

The problem. Modern AI systems that look at an image and talk about it first chop the picture into thousands of tiny patches, and the number of these patches explodes as images get sharper. Every one of those patches then has to be processed by an expensive language engine, and the cost grows faster than the number of patches, so high-resolution vision becomes ruinously slow and memory-hungry. The obvious fix, throwing away or lumping together patches to shrink the count, has always been done blindly at the pixel level, so the system merges pieces that happen to look alike without knowing what they actually are, quietly destroying meaning in the process.

The approach. CORE gives the compression step a sense of what it is looking at before it decides what to combine. It runs a lightweight segmentation step that finds the distinct objects in the image and draws a mask around each one, then uses those object outlines as a guide to collapse all the patches belonging to a single object into one compact token. So instead of an image becoming thousands of anonymous patches, it becomes a small handful of tokens, roughly one per real thing in the scene. A sorting trick keyed to each object's center position then puts these object-tokens back in a sensible spatial order so the language engine still knows where things are relative to each other. Crucially, the object-finder and the vision-language model share the same visual backbone, so adding the segmentation ability costs very little extra.

What's unique. The key insight is to compress along the grain of meaning rather than the grain of pixels: use actual object boundaries as the unit of merging, so each token corresponds to a whole coherent thing instead of a cluster of superficially-similar patches. This lets it push compression to a genuine extreme, keeping only about 2.2% of the original tokens while still holding 97.4% of full performance, because the tokens it keeps are semantically clean rather than arbitrarily fused. Reusing one shared backbone for both segmentation and understanding is what makes this semantic guidance nearly free instead of an added burden.

CodeV: Code with Images for Faithful Visual Reasoning via Tool-Aware Policy Optimization ⌘

The problem. Systems that answer questions about images tend to guess. When you ask one exactly how many people are in a photo, how far apart two objects sit, or what a tiny sign in the corner says, it produces a fluent, confident answer that is often simply wrong — because it is reading the picture through a fuzzy internal impression rather than actually measuring anything. This unreliability matters because as these models get handed real tasks (reading instruments, counting inventory, judging spatial layouts), a plausible-but-false answer is worse than no answer at all.

The approach. Instead of letting the model eyeball the image and blurt out a number, CodeV makes it write a small program that does the looking. The program calls real computer-vision tools — crop, count, read text, estimate depth, detect objects — that operate on the actual pixels and return hard results, and the answer is then derived from those verifiable intermediate outputs rather than from a hunch. The system is trained in two passes: first it learns by example to write this kind of tool-calling code, then it is refined by trial-and-error where it writes many candidate programs, runs them in a real sandbox, and is rewarded both when the executed answer matches the truth and when a separate reviewer model judges that it picked and used the right tools for the question. Because broken or tool-misusing code earns nothing, the model is pushed toward programs that genuinely work.

What's unique. The clever move is rewarding not just the final answer but the reasoning path itself — a judge model checks whether the code chose and applied the correct tools, so the model can't luck into a right answer with sloppy or accidental tool use. Combining this tool-faithfulness signal with execution-based outcome feedback inside a reinforcement-learning loop means the training pressure comes from what actually runs on the image, giving a grounding signal that pure imitation from examples cannot provide.

AdaptVision: Efficient Vision-Language Models via Adaptive Visual Acquisition ⌘

The problem. Systems that answer questions about images have to first turn the picture into a long list of numeric tokens, and the more detail they want the more tokens they carry, which makes them slow and expensive to run. The catch is that most questions do not actually need all that detail. Today's efficiency tricks throw away a fixed fraction of the image regardless of what is being asked, so an easy question wastes effort while a hard one may lose the very detail it needed. The real problem is that the amount of looking is set in advance instead of being decided by the question at hand.

The approach. AdaptVision lets the model look the way a person does: glance first, then zoom in only where needed. It starts by reading a heavily shrunk, low-resolution version of the image using very few tokens, and if that quick glance is enough it just answers. When it is not, the model itself calls a cropping tool to draw a box around the relevant region and pull in a sharper close-up of just that patch, spending extra tokens only where they pay off. So the total amount of visual information gathered varies per image and per question, driven by the model's own judgment rather than a preset ratio. To teach this behavior they use a training scheme, DTPO, that keeps two lessons separate: learning when and how to use the zoom tool, and learning to give the right final answer, so the model does not confuse "did I look in the right place" with "did I answer correctly."

What's unique. The core insight is reframing efficiency as an active decision the model makes turn by turn rather than a fixed compression applied up front: it is the first VLM to cut visual tokens dynamically by choosing to acquire more detail through a tool call only when a sample demands it. The clever supporting piece is the decoupled training objective, which untangles the credit-assignment mess of multi-turn reasoning, separately rewarding good tool use and correct answers so neither optimization drowns out the other.

b-CLIP: Text-Conditioned Contrastive Learning for Multi-Granular Vision-Language Alignment ⌘

The problem. Systems that connect pictures and words, like CLIP, are trained to match a whole image to a whole caption. That teaches them the gist ("this is a photo of a dog on a couch") but not the fine print: which patch of pixels is the dog, which words describe its color, how a phrase maps to a specific spot. The training signal only ever compares the entire image against the entire sentence, so nothing forces the model to line up parts with parts. That gap matters because many real tasks depend on that precise correspondence, such as pointing to the exact region a phrase refers to or checking that an attribute belongs to the right object.

The approach. They train alignment at several zoom levels at once instead of only the whole-image level. On the text side, a sentence is broken into meaningful chunks, noun phrases, verb phrases, and attributes, rather than treated as one blob. On the image side, features are pulled at matching scales: the full image, regional patches, and small salient points. Then the model is pushed to agree at each level, whole image to whole caption, region to phrase, so that the same picture-word matching game is played coarse and fine simultaneously. Crucially, an extra consistency term ties the levels together, using soft alignment maps so that what the model decides at the fine level stays coherent with its coarse-level judgment.

What's unique. The clever move is making the contrastive objective itself multi-granular and self-consistent, rather than bolting a region-matching module onto a globally trained model. By pairing phrase-aware text splitting with a hierarchy of visual features and then explicitly enforcing agreement across granularity levels, it turns a single-scale training recipe into one that learns image, region, and attribute correspondence in the same pass, which prior contrastive methods did not target directly.

MuCo: Multi-turn Contrastive Learning for Multimodal Embedding Model ⌘

The problem. When you search with words and pictures together, the tools behind the scenes learn from frozen snapshots: one image, one caption, matched up. But real searching is a conversation. You ask something, look at what comes back, then refine, then refine again, and each new question only makes sense in light of what you already asked. Systems trained on isolated image-caption pairs have no notion of that running history, so they stumble exactly when the search gets interesting and iterative. The gap matters because most useful visual search in the wild is a back-and-forth, not a single shot.

The approach. Instead of feeding the model a lone image with a lone caption, MuCo lays out each training example as a short dialogue: a sequence of turns, each turn an image plus a question, all of which the model must fold into one search fingerprint that points at the right target image. They build a large teaching set where every image comes with seven different flavors of question (about color, spatial layout, attributes, how parts combine, and so on), so the model sees the same picture interrogated many different ways. They fine-tune an existing vision-language model lightly rather than retraining it from scratch. A subtle but important fix: because many questions in a batch can point at the same image, the model would otherwise punish itself for "wrong" matches that are actually correct, so they simply tell the loss to ignore those same-image collisions.

What's unique. The core insight is that turning a single image into a multi-turn conversation is nearly free but enormously productive: it multiplies the useful training signal about sevenfold while adding only about three percent extra compute, because the shared image content is reused across turns. It is the first embedding approach to train natively on dialogue-shaped queries rather than bolting conversation on afterward, and the small logit-masking trick is what makes that dense, image-repeating batch structure actually trainable instead of self-sabotaging.

ColaVLA: Leveraging Cognitive Latent Reasoning for Hierarchical Parallel Trajectory Planning ⌘

The problem. Robots that take a plain-language instruction and turn it into physical action tend to do fine on short, single-move tasks but fall apart on anything that takes several coordinated steps to finish. A job like "clear the table" is really a chain of sub-goals, each with its own little motion, and the robot has to hold the whole arc in mind while executing each piece. Most systems reason step by step in a slow, one-thing-at-a-time way, so long tasks become brittle and inefficient. Getting machines to plan the many parts of a complex job together, rather than stumbling through them serially, is the gap this work targets.

The approach. ColaVLA sits on top of a vision-language-action model, the kind that reads what the camera sees plus a spoken instruction and outputs robot movements. Instead of thinking out loud in words or planning one motion at a time, it does its reasoning inside a compact internal "thought space" the model computes with directly. In that space it breaks a long job into a hierarchy of smaller sub-goals, then lays out the trajectories for those sub-goals in parallel rather than solving them one after another. The result is that the model can hold the big-picture structure of a multi-step task and work out the several pieces of motion it needs at the same time, which it reports makes tasks succeed more often and planning run more efficiently.

What's unique. The clever move is doing the multi-step reasoning in a hidden latent space and planning several sub-trajectories at once, instead of the usual serial, one-step-at-a-time chain that other action models rely on. Marrying that parallel planning to an explicit hierarchical breakdown of the task is what lets it handle long-horizon jobs that trip up conventional VLA systems.

Jailbreaking Vision-Language Models via Dissonance-Guided Suffix Optimization and Image-Phrase Injection ⌘

The problem. Modern AI systems that see and talk — the ones you can show a picture and ask about it — are trained to refuse harmful requests. But those safety guardrails can be pried open, and studying how is the only honest way to learn how strong they really are. The trouble is that the existing pry bars are weak: attacks that work by tweaking the wording of a prompt tend to get stuck, hammering away at a phrasing that never quite breaks through, while attacks that hide malice inside the image work on the model they were built against but fall apart when pointed at a different model. Neither reliably shows where the real cracks are, which leaves builders guessing about how safe their systems actually are.

The approach. The core trick is to compare two versions of a model side by side: a safety-trained one (the target) and a raw, un-safety-trained one (a guide). Wherever the two sharply disagree about what word should come next, that disagreement pinpoints exactly the words the safety training was working to hold back — and pushing on those words is a far sharper signal than the usual blind trial-and-error of nudging text around. So instead of fumbling for a magic phrase to append, the attack follows this disagreement straight toward the tokens the guardrails are actively suppressing. Then it doubles up through the image: it plants a harmful phrase into the picture itself and tunes not just what the phrase says but how it looks, exploiting the fact that these models are oddly sensitive to text embedded in images. The two prongs — text suffix and image injection — are coordinated so the picture and prompt push in the same direction at once.

What's unique. The genuinely new idea is treating the gap between an aligned and an unaligned model as a map of the safety training itself — the disagreement literally lights up the tokens the guardrails suppress, giving a direction to optimize toward instead of the weak, easily-stuck gradient signals prior text attacks relied on. Pairing that with an image injection tuned for both content and appearance, and coordinating the two, is what lets the attack carry over to commercial black-box systems like GPT-4o-Mini and Gemini rather than only breaking the one model it was trained against.

They combine several lenses: they watch how much attention the network pays to each token and how strongly the final answer would change if a token were altered (a saliency signal), giving a measure of importance; they compare tokens against each other spatially and by meaning to catch pairs that are near-duplicates carrying overlapping information (redundancy); and they trace how these encoding patterns evolve layer by layer through the network. Put together, this maps out which tokens are essentially empty and which few are doing the heavy lifting. The payoff is a data-backed argument that a large share of tokens can be dropped or merged without losing what the model needs to know.

What's unique. Most work on making vision models cheaper jumps straight to a pruning trick and measures whether accuracy survives; this paper instead steps back and diagnoses the phenomenon itself, quantifying token importance, redundancy, and sparsity as measurable properties before prescribing a fix. The one clarifying insight is that the wastefulness is not incidental but structural and large, so heavy compression is safe by design rather than by luck, and the resulting map of where information concentrates turns token pruning from a hopeful hack into something you can reason about.

DASH: A Meta-Attack Framework for Synthesizing Effective and Stealthy Adversarial Examples ⌘

The problem. When people test whether an image classifier can be fooled, they typically hand-craft one kind of trick and see if it slips past the model's defenses. The trouble is that no single trick works everywhere: a model hardened against one style of manipulation often shrugs it off, yet falls to a different one. This makes it hard to honestly measure how safe a model really is, because you can always be fooled into thinking a model is robust simply because you happened to test it with the wrong attack. There is also a competing demand: the fooling manipulation should stay invisible to a human eye, not just be effective, so the test reflects a realistic threat rather than an obvious smudge.

The approach. DASH keeps a toolbox of ten different, well-known ways to fool an image classifier and, instead of picking one, blends them together into a single manipulation. The key move is that the recipe for blending is learned: each attack gets an adjustable weight, and the system tunes those weights automatically for the specific model and image it is facing, leaning on whichever attacks are actually working and dialing down the ones that just add visible noise. It steers this tuning with a single scoring rule that rewards two things at once, pushing the classifier toward the wrong answer while keeping the altered image structurally close to the original so the change stays imperceptible. The whole blend is adjusted repeatedly, like training a small model, until it lands on a combination that is both convincing to the classifier and clean to a human. The result is a manipulation stronger than any of its ten ingredients used alone, tested against seven hardened models on standard image benchmarks.

What's unique. The clever part is treating "which attack to use" as something you learn on the fly rather than choose in advance: DASH turns a fixed menu of ten heterogeneous attacks into adjustable dials and lets an optimizer find the right mix per model and per image. What sets it apart from earlier attempts to combine attacks is folding stealth directly into the same learned objective as effectiveness, so it does not just maximize fooling power but does so while actively suppressing the ingredients that would make the tampering visible, yielding attacks that are simultaneously more effective and harder to see.

RaPA: Enhancing Transferable Targeted Attacks via Random Parameter Pruning ⌘

The problem. When someone wants to fool an image classifier they don't control, they craft a tricky image on a stand-in model they do have, hoping the trick carries over. Making the target model just fail is easy; making it fail into a specific wrong answer you chose is much harder, and it usually breaks the moment you point it at a different, unseen model. The reason is overfitting: the crafted perturbation latches onto quirks of the stand-in model rather than onto something a family of models would all agree on. This matters because understanding when and why these attacks generalize is how researchers gauge how brittle deployed vision systems really are.

The approach. The authors start from an observation: an adversarial image ends up leaning heavily on a tiny handful of the stand-in model's internal weights, so the trick is really a private conversation with those few parameters rather than a general statement about what the object looks like. To break that dependence, at every step of crafting the image they randomly switch off (prune) a different subset of the model's parameters, effectively attacking a slightly different variant of the model each iteration. Because each variant keeps the same overall meaning but has different internal wiring, the perturbation is forced to work across all of them, so it can no longer rely on any single small clique of weights. They show mathematically that this random pruning behaves like a hidden penalty that spreads the attack's reliance evenly across many parameters instead of concentrating it, which is what makes the result generalize to models it never saw.

What's unique. Prior transfer attacks add diversity at the input level (transforming the image) or the model level (ensembling several networks); this is the first to inject randomness at the parameter level of a single surrogate and to show that doing so is equivalent to an importance-equalization regularizer that stops the attack from over-trusting a few weights. That one reframing turns cheap, training-free random pruning into a principled fix, and it pays off most in the hardest case, transferring attacks from CNNs to Transformers, where it lifts success rates by up to 11.7%.

When Token Pruning is Worse than Random: Understanding Visual Token Importance

The problem. Big vision models turn a picture into hundreds of little patches, called tokens, and then do heavy computation on every one of them. That is slow and expensive, so people try to throw away the patches that seem unimportant and keep only the useful ones. The catch this paper exposes is that our commonest ways of guessing which patches matter are so unreliable that throwing away patches at random can actually leave the model working better than throwing away the ones the method singled out. That is a warning sign that we do not really understand what makes a visual patch important, and getting it wrong means either wasted compute or broken accuracy.

The approach. Instead of pitching yet another trimming trick, the authors step back and study the question directly: if you had to rank every patch by how much it matters, what does a good ranking even depend on. They compare the popular importance scores against the honest baseline of removing patches at random, and show that in many cases the fancy scores lose that comparison, which means they are actively pointing at the wrong patches. From there they argue that importance is not one thing but several overlapping ones at once: where a patch sits in the image, what it means semantically, and how relevant it is to the particular task being asked. A patch can look boring by one of these lenses while being essential by another, and methods that judge on only a single lens are the ones that fall below random.

What's unique. The clever move is the diagnostic itself: using random pruning as a truth-teller. Random removal is a floor nobody expects to beat, so when a carefully engineered method drops below it, that is undeniable proof the method's notion of importance is not just weak but backwards. Most work in this area quietly assumes its importance signal is at least directionally right and races to shave off more compute; this paper instead makes the failure measurable and then reframes token importance as a mix of spatial, semantic, and task-specific factors rather than the single attention-based number the field had been leaning on.

VCP-Attack: Visual-Contrastive Projection for Transferable Black-Box Targeted

The problem. Modern AI vision systems can be fooled by images that carry tiny, invisible tweaks — changes no human would notice but that push the model to the wrong answer. The harder, scarier version of this attack is when you don't get to see inside the target model at all (it's a black box) and you want to force it toward a specific wrong answer of your choosing, not just any error. The catch is that a tweak crafted to fool one model usually falls apart on a different one, because each model "sees" pixels through its own idiosyncratic internal wiring. This paper is about making a single crafted attack reliably jump from the model you built it on to models you've never touched.

The approach. The core move is to stop crafting the attack in the raw pixel world and instead craft it in a shared "meaning" space. Many strong vision models are trained with contrastive learning, a recipe that teaches different models to arrange concepts in strikingly similar internal maps — a dog lands near a dog, a truck near a truck, no matter whose model it is. VCP-Attack projects its perturbation into this contrastive embedding space and computes its nudges there, aiming to drag an image's representation toward the target concept rather than merely scrambling its pixels. Because that meaning-space geometry is roughly the same across models, a nudge that works in it tends to survive the jump to an unfamiliar architecture. The attack tunes the perturbation by pushing on a contrastive loss until the image's semantic position lands where the attacker wants it.

What's unique. The key insight is turning contrastive learning's own strength — its habit of producing model-agnostic, aligned feature spaces — into the attack's transfer vehicle, rather than fighting the per-model differences in pixel space where prior attacks struggled. Instead of hoping a pixel-level perturbation happens to generalize, it deliberately operates in the one representation that different models are known to share, which is what lets a black-box targeted attack hit 60–80% transfer rates across diverse architectures.

Mitigating Error Amplification in Fast Adversarial Training

The problem. Neural networks can be fooled by tiny, deliberately-crafted tweaks to an image that a human would never notice, so people train models to resist such attacks by showing them these adversarial examples during learning. But making a genuinely tough example is slow: it normally takes many careful nudges to find the worst-case perturbation, and doing that for every image over a full training run is enormously expensive. The tempting shortcut is to take a single nudge instead of many, which is far cheaper, but models trained this way come out much weaker against real attacks. This paper is about why that cheap shortcut fails and how to make it nearly as good as the expensive version.

The approach. The authors trace the weakness of the one-nudge method to two compounding mistakes: the direction of the nudge is estimated noisily, and the size of the nudge is often wrong for the local shape of the problem, and these small errors snowball as training proceeds. They then attack each mistake directly. To fix the direction, they clean up the estimate by averaging over several small random probes or smoothing it across steps, cutting the noise. To fix the size, they let the step size adapt per sample or per layer instead of using one fixed value, so it matches the local terrain. Finally, they steer training toward the images where the single nudge is most likely to go wrong, spending effort where the errors are largest. Together these keep the cheap single-step attack from producing sloppy training examples.

What's unique. Rather than treating fast adversarial training as a black box to be patched with ad hoc tricks, they open it up and name the actual failure as an "error amplification" process, splitting it cleanly into a direction error and a step-size error and showing how each snowballs. That diagnosis is what sets the work apart: each remedy is aimed at a specific, identified error source instead of at the symptom, letting single-step training close much of the gap to multi-step training while staying several times faster.

Sparse Task Vector Mixup with Hypernetworks for Efficient Knowledge Transfer in Whole-Slide Image Prognosis

The problem. Predicting how a cancer patient will fare from a microscope slide is hard because each cancer type has only a handful of labeled patient outcomes to learn from, and the slides themselves are enormous images. A model trained on one cancer alone rarely learns enough to generalize. The obvious fix, pooling many cancers together or running a patient's slide through many specialist models, is crushingly expensive given the gigapixel size of these images. So the field is stuck between models too narrow to be reliable and combined approaches too costly to be practical.

The approach. Instead of retraining on pooled data or keeping many models around, they work with the differences between models. If you take a specialist model for one cancer and subtract off the starting point it was fine-tuned from, what remains is a "task vector," a compact recipe of the weight changes that encode what that cancer taught the model. Their method, STEPH, blends these recipes: it mixes a target cancer's task vector with those from other cancers, but does so sparsely, keeping only the pieces that actually help, and it uses a small helper network (a hypernetwork) that learns how much of each other cancer's knowledge to pour in and where. The result is a single merged model that has quietly absorbed lessons from many cancers, so at prediction time you run just one model, not a whole committee.

What's unique. The clever move is treating cross-cancer knowledge transfer as arithmetic on model weights rather than as data pooling or multi-model inference, and then making that arithmetic selective and learned instead of a blunt average. Rather than blending every cancer's full task vector uniformly, a hypernetwork decides sparsely which slices of knowledge to borrow and how strongly, sidestepping the gigapixel-scale cost that makes joint training and ensembles impractical while still beating both cancer-specific models and representation-transfer baselines.

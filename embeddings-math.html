Embeddings & distributional meaning — why it works, mathematically

Words are just symbols until you find what they're used for. These number-lists capture that pattern — meaning lives in the company a word keeps, and arithmetic on those lists reveals how meanings relate.

← All themesEvery approachThe mathematics ↗

The problem, as a quantity

A model needs to understand words. But a word is just a symbol — the string "dog" is not inherently more similar to "cat" than to "refrigerator." A computer sees only the label, not what it points to. To learn, the model must first turn each word into a number-list it can use in calculations. But how do you decide what numbers to use?

Raw counting does not work. Rare words stay rare; common words dominate. What matters is not frequency, but the pattern: which words appear near each other, reliably, across a thousand texts. That pattern is meaning. If you capture it in the numbers themselves — so that "dog" and "cat" get similar number-lists because they are used in similar contexts — then the model can start to reason about words without being told what they mean.

The core ideas — and why they work

Turn each discrete symbol into a point in space

word → a number-list v ∈ ℝ^d
Each word gets its own list of numbers, stored as coordinates in a space. Think of it as placing a dot on a map: one dimension might capture "how concrete the word is", another "whether it names a living thing." The dots are not placed by hand — they emerge from training. But once they exist, words live as pure numbers. The model can now ask "how far apart are these two words?", compute on them, mix them. A symbol has become a location in space, a number-list has become a meaning-holder. The space itself has no labels — no axis says "concreteness." But the structure, once learned, encodes real patterns from language.

Meaning comes from company: similar uses land close together

train so that words sharing contexts get nearby number-lists
Words that are used in the same situations should sit close together. Train by looking at millions of sentences, and every time the model sees a word, make it pull nearby words (those appearing in the same context, within a window of a few words) toward the same location. Words that are never close together get pulled apart, or stay far. Over time, "dog", "cat", and "puppy" cluster together because they appear after "the", before "ran", around "bark." But "dog" and "the" stay far apart because they play different roles: one is a noun, one is a function word. The layout is self-organizing — no human labels the dimensions. The training process compresses the meaning of use into the geometry of the space.

Directions carry meaning, so analogies become arithmetic

king − man + woman ≈ queen
A direction in space can capture a difference in meaning. If you take the number-list for "king", subtract the number-list for "man", you get a number-list that points in the direction of "maleness" combined with "royalty." Add that same direction to "woman" and you land near "queen" — the logic of analogy has become arithmetic. This works because the space was learned from contexts: "king" and "queen" appear in similar sentences, so their number-lists are similar, but the difference between them captures something real about gender and status. Any direction in the space points to a meaning-change: singular to plural, past to present, positive to negative. The space itself becomes a logic engine where relationships are distances and differences.

One shared space lets a model compare and combine meanings

closeness = similarity; combine by adding number-lists
Once all words live as number-lists in the same space, the model can directly measure how close any two are, and that closeness stands in for similarity of meaning. It can also combine meanings by adding number-lists: the list for "New" plus the list for "York" points to a place in the space that captures what the pair means together. The model does not store a separate entry for every phrase — it builds a lookup on the fly by mixing the pieces. This is why embeddings are so powerful: one learned space holds not just the words you saw during training, but any combination you ask it to compute, because addition in that space preserves and composes meanings.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Many ways to build the space, but the core idea stays put. You could use word context windows — pull a word toward others in its sentence — or random walks on text — follow paths through related documents to find close neighbors. You could use the model's own predictions as a target — for a language model, predicting the next word gives free training data on what nearby words mean. You could even incorporate structure — syntax, entity types, or hand-built knowledge graphs — to guide where words should sit. The thread never changes: build a number-list for each word by learning from the company it keeps, and that geometry becomes the model's sense of meaning.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

One notch deeper: each of the theme's 4 approach-families and the specific mathematical mechanism that makes its papers work — the exact knob turned on the core relations above. Under each, the specific mathematical note for every paper in that family.

1Learned lookup tables
embedding table: E ∈ R^(V×D), word_i → row_i
Instead of using arbitrary numbers for each word (like king=5, queen=6), the model keeps a big table with one row per word. Each row is a list of learned numbers that get tuned during training. When the model sees a word, it looks up its row in this table — that row becomes the numerical form the rest of the model works with. This turns a meaningless label into a meaningful location in space.
2Similarity by direction
cosine(u, v) = (u · v) / (||u|| × ||v||)
Two words are similar if their number-lists point nearly the same way. The lever computes the cosine: you multiply u and v element by element, add the results (that is the multiply-and-add u · v), then divide by how long each list is (||u|| and ||v||). The result is a number between 0 and 1, where 1 means identical direction (very similar) and 0 means perpendicular (no relation). Distance in this space tells the model which words should affect each other.
3Analogies through number-list arithmetic
word_D = word_B − word_A + word_C
If meaning lives in relationships, then subtracting one word-number-list from another gives you a direction (like 'from king to queen is the gender shift'). You can add that direction to a different word (like 'man') and ask what word-number-list sits nearby — and it finds the analogous word (queen). The model never sees a rule for analogies; arithmetic on the learned positions reveals them. This works because the training placed words so that these relationships hold as consistent directions.
4Context reshaping through layers
embedding(word, context_t) ≠ embedding(word, context_0)
At the very start, a word like 'bank' gets one number-list from the lookup table. But as that word passes through the transformer's layers of attention and processing, its number-list gets reshaped based on context — it becomes a different point in space. A 'river bank' and a 'savings bank' start identical but diverge as the model reads forward. The initial embedding is just the beginning; the real meaning is the transformed version the model builds as it reads, so context can override the table entry and give polysemous words (words with multiple meanings) their true shape.

What breaks without this

What goes wrong. Without embeddings, symbols stay isolated. The model sees cat, kitten, car, and truck as separate labels unless someone gives it a way to compare them.

Why it goes wrong. Meaning depends on relation. Words, image patches, and objects become useful when near things behave alike and far things behave differently.

What this idea gives you. An embedding turns an item into a point. Distance and direction then carry usable meaning.

The through-line. The core problem is representing words in a form a machine can use — not as arbitrary symbols, but as numbers that capture patterns in how language is actually used. You cannot hand-code these patterns; there are too many subtleties and edge cases in any real language corpus. The solution is to learn the geometry from data: watch millions of uses, and adjust a number-list for each word until words used in the same ways land close together in the space. Two plain ideas carry it: meaning is defined by use (so proximity in the space means similarity of context), and that proximity can be measured and optimized directly through the distance between number-lists during training.

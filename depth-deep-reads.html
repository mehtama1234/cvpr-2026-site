1 · One image, instant depth

A single photo is the hardest possible case for seeing in 3D. With one eye, or one flat picture, you can never be sure of absolute size: a real car and a perfect toy car photographed just right make the exact same image. That ambiguity is not a bug you can engineer away — it is baked into the math of projecting a 3D world onto a flat sensor. Yet you read depth from one eye instantly, because your brain leans on a lifetime of learned priors about how big things usually are and how they stack in space. The models in this theme do the same trick at scale: pretrained on millions of images, they read relative depth and shape from a single frame, and the word that keeps recurring in their titles is Any — any camera, any resolution, any panorama. The honest catch, which every paper here bumps into, is that a lone photo gives you the shape of the depth but not its true meters — the absolute scale still has to come from somewhere else.

CVPR'26Depth Any Panoramas: A Foundation Model for Panoramic Depth Estimation
Problem

Ordinary depth models are trained on normal rectangular photos, but a panorama wraps a full 360-degree view onto one warped image — and there is very little labeled panoramic data to learn from, because capturing and annotating it is slow and expensive. Existing panorama depth methods generalize poorly, especially outdoors and across very different distances.

Approach

The authors build a genuine foundation model by first solving the data shortage: they assemble two million panoramas — indoor, outdoor, synthetic, and 1.7 million scraped from the internet — and then run a three-stage pipeline that teaches a first model to label the unlabeled images itself, keeping only the confident guesses as training targets. A large pretrained vision backbone (DINOv3) supplies the general visual priors, and plug-in range heads let the model reason about near-versus-far zones (10, 20, 50, 100 meters) rather than committing blindly. It is trained toward metric depth, so the output aims at real distances, and geometry-aware losses keep edges sharp and surfaces consistent. In effect the model reads the shape of a scene from learned priors, then anchors that shape to distance thresholds it was taught.

Why it matters

It shows the Any ambition in action — one model for indoor, outdoor, real, and synthetic 360 scenes — and it deliberately reaches past relative depth toward true meters, which is exactly the hard step a single image cannot take on its own without heavy supervision.

CVPR'26UniDAC: Universal Metric Depth Estimation for Any Camera
Problem

Depth models learned on standard perspective cameras break on fisheye lenses and 360-degree cameras, because those lenses bend the world in a completely different way. Today you usually need a separate model, or special training data, for each camera type — which does not scale to robots, AR headsets, and cars that all use different optics.

Approach

UniDAC's key idea is to split depth into two jobs: the shape of the scene, and the scale that turns shape into meters. Early features from the vision backbone predict a relative depth map — the shape — which is then normalized to wipe out any absolute-size guess, because a single view genuinely cannot pin size down. Separately, a scale module reads the final, more global features to predict a per-pixel scale map, upsampled with a clever boundary-aware trick that respects object edges without extra parameters. A custom positional-embedding tweak (RoPE-phi) teaches the model that pixels near a panorama's poles are stretched, so it measures distances the way the sphere actually curves. Because the shape part is camera-agnostic and only the scale part is camera-specific, one model trained purely on ordinary photos generalizes to fisheye and 360 cameras it never saw.

Why it matters

This is the relative-versus-metric split made explicit: the paper literally separates the part a single image can give (relative shape) from the part it cannot (absolute scale), and treats scale as its own learned prediction.

CVPR'26Iris: Integrating Language into Diffusion-based Monocular Depth Estimation
Problem

Reading depth from one image is ill-posed — it fails most where the picture is confusing: repeating patterns, blank walls, and tiny faraway objects that a model simply cannot resolve from pixels alone. Even strong diffusion-based depth models struggle to tell shapes, sizes, and spatial relationships apart in those ambiguous regions.

Approach

Iris hands the depth model a second source of knowledge: a text description of the scene. A vision-language model first writes a caption saying what objects are present and how they sit relative to each other, and that text is fed as extra conditioning into a diffusion-based depth model (Marigold, Lotus, E2E-FT) during its step-by-step denoising. This works because those diffusion models were pretrained on huge text-to-image data, so they already implicitly associate words with object shapes and layouts — the language just unlocks and steers that prior. The description narrows the space of plausible depth maps, so ambiguous or small regions get resolved toward what the words say should be there. It also lets you refine specific areas by editing the text, and it speeds up training and inference.

Why it matters

It is a vivid case of borrowed priors: since a single flat image is provably ambiguous, Iris imports outside knowledge — language and image-generation priors — to disambiguate the relative depth the pixels leave unclear.

CVPR'26InfiniDepth: Arbitrary-Resolution and Fine-Grained Depth Estimation with Neural Implicit Fields
Problem

Most depth models output a fixed grid of depth values capped at the resolution they trained on, so pushing to higher detail means blurry upsampling that smears fine geometry. That loses thin structures and sharp edges that high-resolution applications like self-driving and robotics actually need.

Approach

InfiniDepth stops treating depth as a fixed pixel grid and instead makes it a continuous function you can query at any 2D coordinate — a neural implicit field. A vision transformer produces a pyramid of feature maps; then for any point you ask about, a lightweight network gathers the nearby features from that pyramid and predicts the depth there, at whatever fine location you request. Because the representation is continuous rather than pixel-locked, you can render depth at arbitrary resolution — including 4K — without the smoothing that convolutional upsampling introduces. A dedicated query strategy spreads sample points so the recovered 3D surface is evenly covered, which pays off in cleaner novel-view synthesis with fewer holes.

Why it matters

It is another face of Any — any resolution — and it works on both relative and metric depth, underscoring that a good monocular model captures scene shape that can then be sampled as finely as the task demands.

2 · Two eyes are better than one

Point two cameras at the same scene from slightly separated positions and depth stops being a guess and becomes a measurement. A point in the world lands at different horizontal positions in the two images, and that sideways shift — the parallax, or disparity — is directly tied to how far away the point is: near things shift a lot, far things barely move. The baseline between the cameras is the ruler, so multiplying it by focal length and dividing by the shift hands you a metric distance with no learned scale guess. The whole difficulty collapses into one job: matching pixels across the pair, deciding which speck in the left image is the same speck in the right. That fight is brutal on blank textureless walls, on mirrors and glass, and in repeated patterns where every candidate looks identical, so the field scores many candidate shifts at once inside a cost volume and lets the network pick and refine. The 2026 push is to make these matchers work zero-shot on scenes they never trained on and to run in real time, while quietly borrowing the shape priors that single-image depth models already learned.

CVPR'26Lite Any Stereo: Efficient Zero-Shot Stereo Matching
Problem

The best stereo matchers had grown huge, chasing accuracy at the cost of enormous model size. Everyone assumed a small, fast model simply lacked the capacity to generalize — that being lightweight and being zero-shot (working on scenes it never trained on) were mutually exclusive. That accuracy-versus-efficiency tradeoff is what this paper attacks.

Approach

The core machinery is the same as any stereo method: build a cost volume that scores, for every pixel, how well it matches its counterpart in the other image at each candidate sideways shift. Lite Any Stereo keeps a compact but expressive backbone and adds a hybrid cost aggregation module that blends cheap 2D filtering with richer 3D reasoning, so the candidate scores get cleaned up without the usual heavy 3D convolutions. The zero-shot power comes not from size but from data and training: a three-stage recipe of supervised training on synthetic pairs, then self-distillation on that synthetic data, then knowledge distillation on real-world unlabeled images to close the sim-to-real gap. The result matches or beats accuracy-first models while using less than 1% of their compute.

Why it matters

It shows the ruler doesn't have to be expensive: parallax across two cameras yields metric depth directly, and a tiny matcher can read that ruler as well as a giant one.

CVPR'26Fast-FoundationStereo: Real-Time Zero-Shot Stereo Matching
Problem

Stereo foundation models generalize beautifully to unseen scenes but are far too slow for anything real-time. The fast architectures that do run in real time throw away robustness and need costly per-domain fine-tuning to work at all. Before this paper, nothing delivered both real-time speed and zero-shot generalization at once — exactly what a robot or AR headset needs off the shelf.

Approach

Rather than design a new matcher, the authors take the strong-but-slow FoundationStereo and shrink it with a divide-and-conquer acceleration strategy. Knowledge distillation compresses its hybrid backbone into a lean student that still carries both the monocular shape priors and the stereo matching priors. A blockwise neural architecture search then automatically hunts for the cheapest cost-filtering design that fits a latency budget, and structured pruning trims redundancy from the refinement stage using its recurrent dependency graph. To keep generalization intact, an automatic pseudo-labeling pipeline curates 1.4M in-the-wild stereo pairs to supplement synthetic training. The family ships with tunable speed-accuracy settings and runs about 10x faster than the original.

Why it matters

Depth from parallax is only useful to a moving agent if it arrives in time; this makes the two-camera measurement fast enough to act on while still trusting scenes it never saw.

CVPR'26PromptStereo: Zero-Shot Stereo Matching via Structure and Motion Prompts
Problem

Modern stereo methods increasingly lean on monocular depth foundation models for their sense of scene shape, but in doing so they neglect the iterative refinement step that repeatedly polishes the disparity estimate — the very thing that drives zero-shot generalization. The standard GRU-based refinement loop is too small to make good use of those rich monocular priors, so the shape knowledge and the matching machinery never fully cooperate.

Approach

PromptStereo replaces the little GRU with a Prompt Recurrent Unit built directly on the multi-resolution decoder of a monocular depth foundation model like DPT, giving the refinement loop far more representational room. Into that loop it injects two prompts: a Structure Prompt carrying the monocular sense of scene shape, and a Motion Prompt carrying the stereo matching cues that fix absolute scale from parallax. Feeding both as prompts enriches the latent state with true stereo-scale information without distorting the monocular priors already living there. An Affine-Invariant Fusion step then normalizes scale when merging disparities so the two signals combine cleanly.

Why it matters

A single image guesses relative shape; the second camera's parallax pins that shape to an absolute metric scale, and prompting lets the matcher use both at once — cutting Middlebury 2021 error nearly in half.

CVPR'26SPE-MVS: Spatial Position Encoding Enhanced Multi-View Stereo with Monocular Depth
Problem

Multi-view stereo reconstructs 3D geometry by matching a scene across many images instead of just two, and monocular depth estimates make a tempting prior to guide it. But naively bolting single-image depth onto an MVS pipeline tends to lose spatial consistency — the guessed shape and the measured matches drift apart. The open problem is how to feed monocular depth into MVS so the geometry stays coherent.

Approach

SPE-MVS threads the monocular prior in through explicit spatial position encoding rather than raw fusion. It derives a position encoding from the monocular depth, then uses position-aware feature fusion so that when the cost volume scores candidate matches across views, each candidate already carries a sense of where it sits in space. That spatially-encoded cost volume lets the many-view matching resolve ambiguities the images alone can't, and the whole thing is trained end-to-end and differentiable. The payoff is more accurate, more consistent MVS depth on standard benchmarks like DTU and Tanks and Temples.

Why it matters

More viewpoints mean more parallax baselines and thus a more certain measurement of distance, and a monocular prior steadies that measurement exactly where the extra views still struggle — low-texture regions.

CVPR'26LiDAR Prompted Spatio-Temporal Multi-View Stereo for Autonomous Driving
Problem

A self-driving car needs depth that is metrically accurate even where parallax is weak or the surface is blank, stable across video frames, robust when its LiDAR returns only a sparse scatter of points, and reliable on roads it never trained on — all four at once. Monocular models drift on scale, plain MVS collapses in low-parallax driving scenes, and existing fusion methods have no memory across time. No prior method satisfied all four demands together.

Approach

DriveMVS anchors the two-view measurement with a handful of trustworthy LiDAR points. Its Prompt-Anchored Cost Volume keeps two separate scores — the usual MVS relative-consistency cost from image matching, plus an absolute metric cost read off the sparse LiDAR prompts — then fuses them so the parallax gives shape while LiDAR pins the true scale. A Triple-Cues Combiner, a 12-layer masked transformer, blends three streams: cost-volume matching cues, DINOv2 monocular shape cues, and the sparse metric cues, with masking that stays robust when prompts are thin. Finally a spatio-temporal decoder on a DPT backbone adds motion-aware attention across frames with relative pose encoding, so depth stays consistent as the car moves. On KITTI it reaches 0.49m MAE, well past MVSAnywhere and PriorDA.

Why it matters

Parallax across cameras already measures distance directly, and a few sparse LiDAR hits nail the absolute scale — together they beat any single image's unavoidable scale guess.

3 · Images in, geometry out

For decades, turning a pile of photos into 3D meant a rigid three-step pipeline: first calibrate the cameras (figure out where each shot was taken from), then match the same points across images, then optimize everything until the geometry is consistent. That pipeline is slow, and it is brittle — one bad match or a texture-less wall and the whole thing collapses, and it can take minutes per scene. The DUSt3R/VGGT line of work threw out the very first step: instead of solving for the cameras up front, you hand a transformer a bunch of uncalibrated photos and it directly predicts the 3D coordinate of every pixel (a pointmap) in a single forward pass, with the camera positions simply falling out as a byproduct. By 2026 this idea had saturated the field into a whole *3R / *GGT family of models. With the core idea settled, the research frontier moved to the three things a foundation model always chases next: scale it up, speed it up, and free it from expensive hand-built supervision. The papers below are exactly that arc — pushing the one-pass-geometry recipe bigger, more accurate, and cheaper to train.
CVPR'26VGGT-Ω: Scaling Feed-Forward 3D Reconstruction
Problem
Feed-forward reconstruction models already rival the old structure-from-motion pipelines, but nobody had shown they obey the same scaling laws that make large language models work — that more parameters and more data reliably buy more accuracy. On top of that, the global attention these models use is a compute bottleneck, and handling moving objects at scale is hard.
Approach
VGGT-Ω is a systematic study of what happens when you grow the model from 0.5 billion to 10 billion parameters and the training set from a couple thousand to two million scenes. To make that affordable, it slims the architecture: it swaps a quarter of the expensive global-attention layers for scene-token attention that only attends to a small set of summary tokens (cutting those layers to 6% of the compute), and replaces heavy convolutional output heads with lightweight upsampling operators (30% of the memory). It then feeds this with a giant annotation pipeline that mines 800K labeled sequences out of 40M internet videos, plus a DINO-style self-supervised protocol that learns from 18M unlabeled videos. The payoff is a clean power-law: accuracy climbs predictably as size and data grow, and the recipe extends to dynamic, non-rigid scenes.
Why it matters
This is the proof that one-pass 3D reconstruction is a real foundation-model problem — it scales like language models do, so bigger and more data is a reliable path forward, not a dead end.
CVPR'26MoRE: 3D Visual Geometry Reconstruction Meets Mixture-of-Experts
Problem
3D data is wildly heterogeneous — indoor rooms, outdoor streets, single objects, human bodies, moving scenes — and a single shared network is stretched thin trying to be good at all of them at once. Cramming every scene type through one set of weights limits how well the model can specialize and how far it can scale.
Approach
MoRE takes the VGGT backbone and adds a Mixture-of-Experts layer: instead of one shared feed-forward block, it holds several expert blocks, and a tiny router looks at each image token and sends it to the best-matching experts, blending their outputs. This lets different experts quietly specialize in different scene types while keeping the per-image compute roughly flat, plus a load-balancing penalty stops one expert from hogging all the traffic. Two more tricks clean up the supervision: a confidence mask (from a separate depth model) throws out corrupted ground-truth depth so the model does not learn from bad labels, and DINOv2 semantic features are fused in to sharpen surface-normal predictions. It trains in two stages — first the plain dense model, then the experts are switched on for refinement.
Why it matters
This borrows the exact move that let language models grow cheaply — route each input to a specialist — so one 3D model can be great at rooms, streets, and people at once without paying for a bigger network everywhere.
CVPR'26AMB3R: Accurate Feed-forward Metric-scale 3D Reconstruction with Backend
Problem
Pointmap models predict 3D coordinates on a flat 2D grid, which means they never really fuse the same physical point seen in several images into one coherent location — they just regress pixel by pixel. They also usually output geometry only up to an unknown scale, so you cannot read off real-world distances in meters.
Approach
AMB3R keeps VGGT as its front-end for the raw pointmaps but bolts on a backend: a compact 3D voxel grid that gathers all the per-pixel predictions into one shared volumetric scene, reasons over it with a transformer, and injects the cleaned-up geometry back into the pointmaps. A small extra scale head pins the whole reconstruction to true metric scale, so the output is in real units. Because the backend gives an explicit, compact 3D memory, the same trained model powers training-free visual odometry and structure-from-motion over arbitrary numbers of images, using active memory management and a divide-and-conquer strategy to stay scalable. Notably it reaches state-of-the-art across 7 tasks and 13 datasets on only 80 H100 GPU-hours.
Why it matters
By adding a shared 3D scratchpad, AMB3R makes a feed-forward model output true metric geometry and even replace classical SLAM and SfM systems — with no per-scene optimization at all.
CVPR'26Reliev3R: Relieving Feed-forward 3D Reconstruction from Multi-View Geometric Annotations
Problem
The catch with these models is their training data: the ground-truth 3D geometry has to be produced by the very same slow, brittle SfM/MVS pipelines the models are meant to replace. Those annotations are expensive to compute, fail on hard scenes, and cap how much data you can realistically feed the model.
Approach
Reliev3R trains a feed-forward reconstruction model without any of those expensive multi-view geometric labels, using weak supervision instead. It borrows cheap, off-the-shelf signals: pseudo relative-depth maps from a pretrained monocular depth model act as soft targets, with an ambiguity-aware loss that down-weights the regions those pseudo-labels get wrong. Sparse 2D correspondences from a pretrained matcher provide geometric anchors linking the views. The model then jointly refines its predicted camera poses and depths through a differentiable, trigonometry-based reprojection loss that enforces consistency using only the known camera intrinsics. The result matches or beats fully-supervised models while using far less curated geometric data.
Why it matters
This cuts the last expensive tether — the hand-computed geometry labels — so the recipe can finally scale to massive unlabeled video, the same freedom that made image and language foundation models take off.

4 · The cameras, and the whole solid

A depth map only ever tells you how far away the visible front of a scene is, and it quietly assumes you already know exactly where the camera was standing when the shot was taken. But often you do not know that, and often the front is not enough. This theme tackles the two harder jobs hiding behind every reconstruction: recovering the cameras themselves, where each photo was taken or where a moving camera is right now, and recovering the complete solid, the filled-in three-dimensional shape rather than a thin visible shell. Classical camera-solving math, called structure-from-motion and bundle adjustment, is beautifully precise but also brittle, snapping on dark, textureless, symmetric, or barely-overlapping views. So the 2026 papers splice learned feed-forward priors into those fragile solvers, and lean on rigidity theory to know when the geometry is even solvable in the first place. And on the shape side, filling a solid means the model must literally invent the unseen back and interior of objects from what it has learned about how the world is usually built, turning a flat picture into occupancy, meshes, and continuous surfaces.
CVPR'26Global Structure-from-Motion Meets Feedforward Reconstruction
Problem
Before you can build anything in 3D from a pile of photos, you first have to figure out where every photo was taken from, which is the job called structure-from-motion. The classical, hand-derived math for this is extremely accurate on ordinary scenes, but it simply breaks on hard ones, blank walls with no texture, repeated symmetric patterns it confuses, or shots that barely overlap. The newer feed-forward neural methods handle exactly those hard cases, but they are worse at scale, accuracy, and reliability precisely where the old math already worked fine.
Approach
Rather than pick a side, this work builds a hybrid pipeline that hands each part of the problem to whichever tool is best at it. The robust, accurate classical global solver stays in charge of normal, well-behaved scenes. Learned components, a neural feature detector and a neural matcher, are spliced in to find correspondences under difficult appearance changes and lighting. And the feed-forward neural reconstructor is reserved for the failure modes the classical math cannot survive, the low-texture, symmetric, and sparse-overlap views. The result is shipped as open source inside the COLMAP ecosystem, the standard toolkit for this task, and it beats both the pure-classical and pure-neural approaches on their own across five very different datasets.
Why it matters
If you solve the cameras wrong, every later step inherits the error and the entire reconstruction warps, so making the camera-solver robust to the cases that used to break it is what lets everything downstream stand up straight.
CVPR'26Parallel Rigidity Matters for Bundle Adjustment
Problem
Bundle adjustment is the refinement step that jointly nudges all the camera positions and all the 3D points until they best agree with the photos, and it is the backbone of both structure-from-motion and SLAM. But nobody had rigorously asked a basic question: when is its answer actually unique? The hidden failure is that the camera-and-point graph can contain chunks that are free to slide and rescale independently of the rest, which silently drops individual cameras and points into the wrong place.
Approach
The paper brings in parallel rigidity theory, a piece of geometry that tells you exactly when a network of connected pieces is pinned down uniquely versus when parts of it can still float. It analyzes the bipartite graph that links cameras to the 3D points they see, and proves the conditions under which that structure is rigid. From this it derives an efficient algorithm that walks the view-graph looking at the smallest possible loops, four-length loops, to detect and stitch together the maximal rigid subgraphs. Plugged into the GLOMAP global reconstruction pipeline, it strips out the cameras and points that were only floating because of independent rescaling, which also lets it keep more valid points overall.
Why it matters
This is diagnosing why a reconstruction warps rather than just cleaning up afterward: knowing which parts of the geometry are genuinely pinned down, and cutting away the parts that are not, is what stops phantom cameras and misplaced points from poisoning the whole model.
CVPR'26Monocular Open Vocabulary Occupancy Prediction for Indoor Scenes
Problem
A depth map from a single photo only tells you the distance to the visible surface, not which cells of the room are solid versus empty, and existing systems that do predict such 3D occupancy can only label a small fixed list of object categories baked in at training time. That means asking about any object outside the list requires retraining from scratch. The goal here is to predict, from one ordinary indoor photo, which parts of space are filled and what each filled region is, for any object you care to name.
Approach
The method fuses two off-the-shelf abilities into one 3D pipeline. First it runs monocular depth estimation to guess how far away each pixel is, giving it a way to lift flat pixels into 3D positions. Then it uses an open-vocabulary detector driven by a vision-language model, CLIP, so that objects are recognized by matching them against language rather than a closed category list. Combining the two, it projects the language-tagged 2D detections out into space using the estimated depth to produce a semantic occupancy prediction. Because objects are queried through language embeddings, it can answer about categories it was never explicitly trained on, all without needing any 3D annotations to learn from.
Why it matters
A depth surface is not the same as a filled room, so turning the visible front into labeled occupied-versus-empty volume, for arbitrary named objects, is what lets a robot actually reason about and move through a space it has never seen.
CVPR'26SAM 3D Body: Robust Full-Body Human Mesh Recovery
Problem
Recovering a full 3D body mesh from a single photo means reconstructing the complete solid shape of a person, including the hidden back and the parts hidden by occlusion, not just the visible silhouette. Existing methods fall apart on unusual poses, heavy occlusion, and odd viewpoints, and they struggle to reconstruct body, hands, and feet together in one coherent model. Worse, the available training data forces a bad trade: clean lab captures with too little pose variety, or diverse in-the-wild images with poor mesh quality.
Approach
SAM 3D Body borrows the promptable design of the Segment Anything models: a promptable encoder-decoder where a user can drop 2D keypoints or a mask to steer the reconstruction. It uses two separate decoders, one for the body and one for the hands, which removes the tug-of-war where optimizing the body used to degrade the hands and vice versa. Underneath sits the Momentum Human Rig representation, which cleanly decouples the skeleton, how the person is posed, from the body shape, how they are built, so each can be estimated without corrupting the other. To get past the data trade-off, it is trained on 7 million images built by a data engine that uses a vision-language model to actively mine hard poses, then pins down accurate 3D labels with a mix of manual keypoints, differentiable optimization, and multi-view geometry.
Why it matters
A visible-front shell is not a usable solid: recovering the full, hallucinated-back body mesh, robustly even under occlusion and strange poses, is what lets robots and biomechanics systems actually understand and interact with a real person.

5 · Putting the ruler back

Nearly everything upstream in this explainer is scale-ambiguous: a network can recover the shape of a scene beautifully, but the overall size floats free, so the same picture is consistent with a real house and a tiny dollhouse. This is not a bug you can train away — a single flat image provably cannot fix the size, because the camera throws away the one number that would tell a small-close thing from a large-far one. To get real metres back you have to pin the scale with an external anchor: a cheap sensor hit that measures a few true distances, a known camera whose lens geometry sets the scale, or a language or size prior that says roughly how big the scene is. Once you have that anchor, the job becomes propagating it across the whole depth map so every pixel inherits a trustworthy metre value. The papers here each choose a different anchor — sparse 3D points, radar returns, a lightweight time-of-flight sensor, an intrinsics-free edge cue — but they converge on the same recovery step. And the sharpest lesson of 2026 is that one global scale is too crude: because different objects and regions sit at different distances, the scale has to vary spatially, per-segment and per-region, not as a single multiply-everything number.

CVPR'26The Midas Touch for Metric Depth
Problem

Modern relative-depth models generalise across scenes wonderfully but produce a map with no real size — the geometry is right, the metres are missing. They also drift locally, so nearby patches disagree on scale, and the heavy versions are too costly for a phone or robot.

Approach

The anchor here is extremely sparse 3D data — a handful of true points from a range camera or LiDAR. Crucially the method (MTD) refuses to accept a single global scale-and-shift: it first does segment-wise recovery, using a sparse graph optimisation so each region of the image gets its own scale and local inconsistencies are cancelled. It then does a pixel-wise refinement with a discontinuity-aware geodesic cost, letting the correction flow along surfaces but stop at depth edges so boundaries stay sharp. The whole thing is deliberately lightweight and plug-and-play, so it rides on top of any relative-depth backbone. The result is a mathematically interpretable way to turn shape-only depth into true metres from just a few anchor points.

Why it matters

This is exactly where the missing dimension gets its size back: the sparse points inject real metres, and the per-segment scaling spreads that ruler across the map without smearing it into one flat number.

CVPR'26TR2M: Transferring Monocular Relative Depth to Metric Depth with Language Descriptions and Dual-Level Scale-Oriented Contrast
Problem

Metric depth models are trained per-domain and break when the scale shifts; relative-depth models generalise but leave the scale undetermined. Earlier attempts to fix scale from words produced big erroneous blotches and features that disagreed about how far things were.

Approach

The external anchor here is unusual — it is language. A text description of the scene carries a rough size prior (a street is tens of metres, a playroom a few), and TR2M fuses that text with the image through cross-modality attention on frozen embeddings. Rather than one scalar, it predicts pixel-wise rescale maps — a scale and a shift for every pixel — so the size correction varies spatially instead of multiplying the whole image by one number. It is supervised with pseudo metric-depth built under confidence filtering, and a Dual-Level Scale-Oriented Contrast loss classifies features by their depth distribution to keep the global scale consistent across image and pixels. All of this recovers metres with only 19M trainable parameters and no extra sensor or calibration rig.

Why it matters

It shows the ruler can come from a size prior in words alone — the language says how big the world is, and the per-pixel rescale map plants that true scale back into the flattened image.

CVPR'26Radar-Guided Polynomial Fitting for Metric Depth Estimation
Problem

Monocular depth is scaleless, and the standard fix — an affine scale-and-shift — assumes the whole image is off by one consistent amount. But when several objects sit at genuinely different distances, no single global factor can align them all at once, so the affine correction leaves cross-region errors.

Approach

The anchor is cheap radar: ubiquitous, inexpensive sensors that return a few sparse but absolute range measurements. POLAR uses these to move beyond affine, fitting a polynomial that maps scaleless depth to metric depth — the polynomial's inflection points let it bend the correction differently in different depth ranges, fixing the object-to-object misalignments a straight line cannot. The coefficients are predicted from fused radar-visual features so the radar hits actually steer the curve. A first-derivative regularisation enforces piecewise monotonicity, so the correction can flex without scrambling the near-far ordering of the scene. It reports about 25% lower MAE and 33% lower RMSE while running over 40 FPS.

Why it matters

This is the theme's core lesson made concrete: a cheap sensor supplies the real metres, and a spatially-varying curve — not one global multiply — is what actually restores true size across the whole scene.

CVPR'26MD2E: Modeling Depth-to-Edge Cues for Monocular Metric Depth Estimation
Problem

You can sometimes get metric depth from the camera's known focal length, but often you do not have it. Worse, when focal length and scene depth change together they cancel out in the picture, so the RGB image alone gives no reliable cue to the absolute scale.

Approach

MD2E's anchor is an intrinsics-free edge cue — no camera parameters needed. It converts dense depth annotations into edge supervision, then reads the Fourier spectrum of the predicted edge map with a Spectral Quantile Estimator, which outputs a single scalar that acts as a metric-scale proxy. The intuition is that how sharp and how frequent depth edges are changes systematically with how big and how far the scene is, so the edge spectrum secretly encodes scale. The edge predictions also regularise the depth map to keep boundaries crisp, while the spectral score calibrates the absolute metres. It reaches state-of-the-art metric depth across diverse cameras in both zero-shot and fine-tuned settings.

Why it matters

Here the ruler is recovered from the image's own structure — the statistics of its edges — proving the size anchor need not be a sensor at all when the camera is unknown.

CVPR'26LiteSense: Lifting Lightweight ToF with RGB for High-Resolution Metric Depth Estimation
Problem

A cheap time-of-flight sensor gives you true metres but at a tiny 8x8 resolution; a monocular RGB model gives you high resolution but no absolute scale. Neither alone delivers depth that is both metric and detailed, and the usual fixes need expensive LiDAR or studio rigs.

Approach

The anchor is a lightweight ToF sensor whose few real range readings supply the metres, which RGB then upsamples into a dense, sharp map. Beyond the coarse ToF depth, LiteSense also reads each zone's Compact Normalized Histogram — the raw distribution of returning photons — which carries richer distance information than a single averaged number. The key move is Patch-wise CNH Spatial Injection: it splits features into patches and cross-attends RGB-D queries against the histogram of the matching zone, so each region is scaled by its own local sensor evidence rather than one global fusion that would blur zones together. Built on MobileNetV4 blocks, it runs on edge devices and matches a full RealSense unit while using far less compute.

Why it matters

It is the missing dimension restored at the source: a few true photon-timed distances become the anchor, and per-zone injection spreads that real scale into a high-resolution metric map.

6 · Metric, real-time, onboard

In vision, recovering 3D can stop at relative depth — knowing that one object is farther than another is often enough to reconstruct a convincing scene. A robot cannot stop there: it needs real metres, it needs them right now, and it needs them computed on its own hardware. The reason is unforgiving — a wrong depth is not a lower reconstruction score, it is a collision or a missed grasp. But a lone camera is cursed with scale ambiguity: it recovers the shape of motion and depth only up to an unknown scale, so a small nearby object and a large distant one look identical. Robotics defeats this villain by forcing the metres back in from something it physically knows — an IMU, a stereo baseline, the robot's own kinematics, or a LiDAR map. And the recovered geometry is judged not by how pretty the reconstruction looks, but by whether the arm closes on the object and the drone clears the wall. That is the punchline of this theme: vision reconstructs, but robotics must act on metric geometry, live.
ROBOTICS'25MAC-VO: Metrics-Aware Covariance for Learning-Based Stereo Visual Odometry
Problem
A robot tracking its own motion from a stereo camera (visual odometry) has to decide which visual features to trust and how much to trust them. Existing learning-based systems either trust every matched point equally or fall back on hand-tuned rules, so a single mismatched or blurry feature can quietly corrupt the whole trajectory. What is missing is a sense of how uncertain, in real metres, each 3D point actually is.
Approach
MAC-VO learns a metrics-aware covariance — for every matched feature it predicts not just where the point is, but a full error ellipse describing how far off it could be along each axis, and how those axis errors correlate. That learned uncertainty does double duty: it filters keypoints, throwing out the ones the network itself judges globally inconsistent, and it weights the residuals in the pose-graph optimization so confident points pull hard and shaky ones barely nudge the estimate. Because the stereo baseline fixes the true scale, these covariances are in real metres, not arbitrary units. The result is a spatially calibrated notion of trust replacing the old scale-agnostic or heuristic weighting. On hard benchmarks it beats prior VO systems and even some full SLAM pipelines.
Why it matters
A metric, per-match error model lets the robot know which parts of its own motion estimate are reliable and by how many centimetres — something a relative reconstruction, which never commits to a scale, simply cannot report.
ROBOTICS'25KineDepth: Utilizing Robot Kinematics for Online Metric Depth Estimation
Problem
A single ordinary camera can predict depth, but only relative depth — the picture is right up to an unknown overall scale, so the robot cannot tell whether an object is thirty centimetres away or three metres. For manipulation that gap is fatal, and bolting on a dedicated depth sensor is expensive and not always possible. The scale has to come from somewhere the robot already knows.
Approach
KineDepth turns the robot's own body into a measuring stick. As the arm moves along a path whose geometry it knows exactly from its joint encoders, the camera sees the scene shift by a known amount, and that known motion is precisely the physical ruler needed to convert scale-ambiguous depth into real metres. An LSTM regressor is trained online from a single calibrated camera, and a probabilistic filter fuses successive views to refine the estimate over time. No external depth sensor, no offline dataset — the kinematics self-calibrate the scale as the robot works. It is, to the authors' knowledge, the first system to exploit robot kinematics this way for online metric depth.
Why it matters
By borrowing metres from the one thing a robot measures perfectly — its own motion — KineDepth hands manipulation a metric depth map from a bare RGB camera, which a scale-free monocular reconstruction could never provide.
ROBOTICS'25Monocular One-Shot Metric-Depth Alignment for RGB-Based Robot Grasping
Problem
Powerful pre-trained monocular depth models are only affine-invariant — their output is ambiguous in both scale and offset, so the numbers are proportional to real depth but not equal to it. For 6D grasp pose estimation the robot needs true metres, and retraining a foundation model on new metric data is slow and costly. The goal is metric depth from an off-the-shelf RGB model without any new data collection.
Approach
MOMA (Monocular One-Shot Metric-depth Alignment) does a one-shot alignment rather than a retrain. During a single camera-calibration step it gathers a handful of sparse ground-truth depth points, then solves for the scale, rotation, and shift that snap the pre-trained model's relative predictions onto real-world metric depth. That one small correction, computed once, unlocks metric depth for every subsequent frame — no new dataset, no full retraining. An optional fine-tuning path handles transparent objects, where ordinary depth sensors fail outright. The aligned depth feeds a grasp pipeline that succeeds at high rates on tabletop and bin-picking tasks.
Why it matters
One cheap calibration converts a general-purpose relative-depth model into a metric one the robot can grasp with — turning a reconstruction that only ranks distances into geometry that says exactly where to close the gripper.
ROBOTICS'25A Control Barrier Function for Safe Navigation with Online Gaussian Splatting Maps
Problem
A drone building a dense 3D Gaussian Splatting map of its surroundings as it flies needs a safety guarantee that keeps it from hitting anything in that map. But such a map is hundreds of thousands of Gaussian blobs, and the safety check must run live without stalling the map-building that is happening at the same time. A stale or slow geometry check is, quite literally, a crash.
Approach
The paper synthesizes a Control Barrier Function — a safety filter — directly from all the Gaussian primitives in the live map. Instead of reasoning over a reconstructed surface, the barrier is computed against the raw scene Gaussians, running at 15 Hz on the GPU with tiny memory overhead so it never blocks the ongoing map training. It plugs into ROS through a component called SplatBridge, so the drone can grow its Gaussian map and filter its own commanded actions for safety in the same loop. Because the geometry is metric and onboard, the filter reasons in real space and real time. It runs 20 to 50 times faster than comparable NeRF-based safety methods and is less conservative, meaning fewer needless slowdowns.
Why it matters
Computing the safety filter live from the actual scene geometry lets the robot act — dodge and press forward — on a map it is still building, which a static, offline reconstruction judged only by visual fidelity could never make safe in flight.
Reading it whole. Top to bottom, the themes walk the same missing dimension back in: first read shape from a single image, then measure metric depth from a second view, then regress the whole geometry in one feed-forward pass, then recover the cameras and the full solid, then pin the absolute scale — and finally hand it all to a robot that must have it metric, live and onboard. The scenes and sensors differ wildly; the problem — a photo deleted a dimension and we are putting it back — never changes.

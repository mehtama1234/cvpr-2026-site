The four things you can do to data

Modern AI is bottlenecked by data, not cleverness. There are exactly four moves you can make on a dataset — shrink it, generate it, curate it, understand it. Here they are from first principles, each run live.

The bottleneck is data

For a decade the story of progress in AI was "bigger models." But the quieter truth is that models mostly turn data into behavior — and data is the part that's expensive, scarce, private, redundant, and mysterious. You can't always get more of it (rare diseases, new products, sensitive records), you often have too much of the wrong kind, and you rarely know which parts actually taught the model what it knows.

So the deepest lever isn't the network — it's the dataset. And there turn out to be exactly four things you can do to a dataset. You can make it smaller without losing what it teaches. You can manufacture new data when you don't have enough. You can select the parts worth keeping and drop the rest. And you can trace which examples caused which behavior. Shrink, generate, curate, understand. Everything in data-centric machine learning is one of these four — and we ran three of them live below.

1 · Shrink it — dataset distillation

Boil a dataset down to a few synthetic "super-examples" that train a model almost as well as the whole thing.

Most of a dataset is redundant — the ten-thousandth cat photo teaches almost nothing new. Distillation asks: what is the smallest synthetic set whose effect on a trained model matches the full data? Dataset Distillation by Influence Matching matches each example's influence on the final weights (not the training path); Multimodal Distribution Matching extends this to image-text pairs; HierAmp generates distilled sets coarse-to-fine. We ran InfMatch live on the scarce-data page — 100 synthetic images reproduced ~61% of full CIFAR-10 (the paper reports 72.5% at full scale). This is the move we've already done; the other three are new below.

2 · Generate it — synthesize new training data

When you can't collect more real data, make it. A generative model conjures training examples on demand.

Distillation compresses data you have; generation manufactures data you don't. With modern image generators you can turn a class name into training pictures. The corpus pushes this several ways: Accelerating Diffusion Training under Minimal Budgets condenses data specifically to train diffusion models cheaply; HierAmp amplifies a tiny seed into a full synthetic set; Diffusion-Based Native Adversarial Synthesis generates the hard on-manifold cases a medical segmenter needs. The catch is always the reality gap — synthetic images live in a slightly different distribution than real ones, so a model trained on them can stumble on real inputs.

We ran it. We used a fast image generator (SDXL-Turbo) to synthesize 10 images per class, then trained the same small network on three different 100-image sets and tested on real CIFAR-10 — a head-to-head of the three ways to get 100 training images.

What we found. Look at those generated images — crisp, centered, photorealistic; to a human they look better than real CIFAR-10's gritty 32-pixel thumbnails. Yet a network trained on 100 of them scored only 21.7% on the real test set — worse than 100 randomly-picked real images (29.7%). That is the reality gap made concrete: the generator's images live in a slightly different world (framing, resolution, texture, lighting) than the target data, and a model trained on that world stumbles on the real one. Meanwhile the distilled 100 images reached ~61% — but that bar uses the paper's stronger training recipe (augmentation, soft labels), so it isn't a clean head-to-head; the fair comparison is real-vs-generated. The lesson: at a fixed tiny budget, how you obtain the images matters more than how good they look — and naive generation is not free data.

3 · Curate it — keep the examples that matter

The dual of distillation: don't synthesize a small set, select one. Score every real example, keep the informative core, throw the redundant rest away.

If most data is redundant, you can often delete the bulk of it and lose almost nothing — if you keep the right examples. The trick is scoring: which examples carry information? Batch Loss Score for Dynamic Data Pruning scores examples by loss in batch context; Concept-Aware Batch Sampling builds batches for diversity or rarity on the fly during pretraining; Scaling-Aware Data Selection picks driving data by modeling each domain's scaling law; LoPrune prunes via an influence score projected onto the LoRA subspace. A classic scorer is EL2N — how wrong the model still is on an example early in training; high error means the example is hard and informative, low error means it's easy and redundant.

We ran it. We scored 10,000 CIFAR-10 images by EL2N, then retrained from scratch on the hardest 20% versus a random 20% versus the full set.

What we found — and it surprised us. Full data got 68.8%. Keeping a random 20% got 52.8%. But keeping the 20% the model found hardest (high EL2N) collapsed to 20.8% — far worse than random — and keeping the easiest 20% (48.5%) didn't beat random either. So a naive difficulty ranking, in either direction, fails to beat plain random selection here. Why: at an aggressive keep-rate, the "hardest" examples are disproportionately ambiguous or mislabeled outliers, so a set made of only those is toxic; the "easiest" are redundant near-duplicates that miss the decision boundary. What random gets for free is coverage — a spread across the whole distribution. That's the real lesson, and exactly why the serious methods in the corpus (Concept-Aware Batch Sampling's diversity, coverage-aware and class-balanced selection) score examples in context rather than sorting by a single difficulty number. Curation works — but only when it preserves coverage, not just picks the "informative" extremes.

4 · Understand it — data attribution

Given something a model did, which training examples caused it? The diagnostic move — and the mathematical cousin of distillation.

Distillation used influence to build data; flip it around to explain data. Attribution asks, for a specific prediction, which training images pushed the model toward it. This is how you debug a dataset — find the mislabeled example poisoning a class, or the handful of images a decision really rests on. CHIPS combines curvature (Hessian) analysis with influence to select data for adapting CLIP; LoPrune's influence score does the same for pruning. Under the hood it's a dot product: a training example is a "proponent" of a test prediction when their loss gradients point the same way.

We ran it. We trained a small network, then for a few test images found the training images whose gradients most aligned with them — the examples that most shaped that call.

Each row: a test image (left) and the four training images that most influenced the model's prediction on it. When the proponents share the test image's class and look alike, the model is reasoning from sensible evidence.

The through-line

Four moves, one idea: information, not volume, is what matters in a dataset. Shrinking concentrates information into fewer synthetic examples; generating manufactures it where you have none; curating keeps the informative real examples and drops the redundant ones; understanding traces where the information came from. Notice how tightly they connect — the same influence math powers both distillation (shrink) and attribution (understand); pruning (curate) is just distillation's select-don't-synthesize twin; and generation (make) is the mirror of distillation (compress).

Where it stands: curation and few-shot generation are practical today; distillation is impressive at small scale but not yet a drop-in for real data; attribution is powerful for debugging but expensive at scale. The frontier for all four is the same — moving from clever demos on small images to reliable tools on production-scale data.

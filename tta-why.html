Test-time adaptation — learning after deployment

A model is trained once, then sent into a world that never holds still: new lighting, a dirtier lens, weather, a domain nobody anticipated. Its accuracy quietly falls, and it has no labels for the new conditions to retrain on. Test-time adaptation is the idea that a model need not be frozen at deployment — it can keep learning from the unlabelled data streaming past it, right now. This page is the plain story of that idea, its free recovery, and its sharp double edge, on a real run.

Mathematical core

Hidden quantity. The page is really about estimating the correction needed after deployment when the test data shifts.

Evidence. The model sees unlabeled test batches, entropy, confidence, augmentations, batch statistics, and pseudo-labels.

Rule. The fundamental rule is to adapt using only deployment evidence while preventing the model from reinforcing its own wrong guesses.

Failure test. Test-time learning can become self-confirming collapse if confident mistakes are treated as truth.

1 · The problem — the world shifts, the model doesn't

A model is trained on one distribution and tested on the same — that is the comfortable assumption. Reality breaks it the moment the model ships: the camera changes, the weather turns, the domain drifts. Accuracy degrades, sometimes catastrophically, and the obvious fix — collect labels for the new conditions and retrain — is exactly what you cannot do at deployment, where labels are absent and decisions are due now.

The problem, stated once: a trained model meets a shifted world at deployment, with no labels for the new conditions — how can it recover accuracy without being retrained?

2 · The reframe — learn from the test data itself

The idea: the unlabelled test data is itself a teacher. You cannot check the answers, but you can exploit two things that need no labels. The model's internal normalisation was tuned to the training data's statistics — so recompute those statistics on the incoming test data (a free recalibration). And a confident, low-entropy prediction is usually a more correct one — so nudge a few parameters to make the model more decisive on the test batch. Both signals come straight from the test stream; neither needs a label.
adapt at test time: recompute batch-norm stats on the new data + minimise prediction entropy     # no labels — the unlabelled test batch is the only supervision, so it must be used gently

It recovers a surprising amount for free — and it can just as easily destroy the model. The run shows both.

3 · Seen on a real run — collapse, recovery, and a cliff

1 · A shift, and the collapse

We trained a classifier on clean FashionMNIST — 92% accurate. Then we deployed it into a shifted world: the test images corrupted with blur and noise, the kind of change a real camera meets. With no labels to retrain on, it falls apart — 19%, a fraction of its clean self. This is the everyday failure of a fixed model: the world moved, and it did not.

clean data (no shift) — 92%
after the shift — no adaptation — 19%
batch-norm recalibration only (free) — 47%
full test-time adaptation (best) — 49%
adapted too hard (worst) — 34%

2 · Recovering ground with no labels

Two moves claw it back, using only the unlabelled test images. First, just recomputing the network's internal batch-norm statistics on the shifted data — a free recalibration, no training — lifts it from 19% to 47%. Then a few steps of entropy minimisation (nudge the model to be more confident on the test batch, which sharpens its decisions) adds a little more, to 49% — over a third of the lost ground recovered, without a single label.

3 · The honest catch — adapt too hard and it breaks

But this is a knife that cuts both ways. Push the adaptation too far — too many steps, too high a learning rate — and making the model ever more confident on unlabelled data has no ground truth to anchor it, so it drifts and collapses: down to 34%, below even the un-adapted baseline. Free self-supervision at test time is powerful and unstable in the same breath; the whole craft is knowing when to stop.

4 · Why it matters — models live in a moving world

Nothing stays in-distribution for long once deployed, so the ability to absorb shift without a labelling round is the difference between a model that degrades silently and one that holds up. Test-time adaptation shows up as recalibrating a classifier under corruption, prompt-tuning a frozen foundation model to a new task on the fly, or adapting to the particular video in front of you — a cheap layer of robustness bolted onto an already-trained model. Its instability is the price, which is why so much of the work is about adapting safely.

5 · The surprise — a robot must adapt or fail

A robot meets distribution shift as a matter of course — a new floor, a rainy street, a worn gripper — and it cannot pause to be retrained. So it adapts online from its own experience: self-supervised signals from its sensors, meta-learned fast updates, tactile feedback correcting a grasp. The same idea as vision, with the volume turned up: the shift is constant and the stakes are physical.

Same adaptation, but a wrong update moves a body. Online adaptation of driving policies, self-supervised on-robot learning, and sim-to-real gap-closing are a serious thread in robotics — and the over-adaptation cliff you saw is not a cosmetic risk here but a safety one, since a model that adapts itself into nonsense drives a real machine. Vision adapts a prediction; a robot adapts an action. The in-the-wild page lays out both sides.

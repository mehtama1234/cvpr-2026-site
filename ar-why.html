Autoregressive generation — writing an image one piece at a time

There are two ways to make an image with a neural network. A diffusion model refines a whole noisy canvas at once, over many steps. An autoregressive model does the opposite — it writes the picture the way a language model writes a sentence: one piece at a time, each new piece chosen from everything written so far. This page is the plain story of that second way, built on a real run where a tiny GPT learns to write little images — and where you can watch its two famous quirks appear: a temperature dial with a cost, and errors that snowball.

Mathematical core

Hidden quantity. The page is really about estimating the next token, pixel, frame, action, or latent given the past.

Evidence. The model sees previous sequence elements, causal masks, likelihood, and rollout error.

Rule. The fundamental rule is to factor a hard joint distribution into a chain of next-step predictions.

Failure test. Early mistakes become part of the future context, so errors compound over long rollouts.

1 · The problem — how do you generate a whole image?

An image is a huge, structured object — a grid of thousands of pixels that all have to agree with each other to look like anything. You cannot just sample each pixel independently; you would get noise. You need to model how every part depends on every other part. Diffusion answers this by denoising the whole canvas together. But there is an older, language-shaped answer: turn the image into a sequence and generate it the way you generate text.

The problem, stated once: generate a coherent image (or video, or shape) where every piece fits the others — by turning it into a sequence and predicting the next piece from all the pieces before it.

2 · The reframe — predict the next piece

The idea: flatten the image into a string of tokens and train a model to predict the next one. Read the pixels in a fixed order (say left-to-right, top-to-bottom) so the image becomes a sequence. Train a causal transformer — the same kind that powers language models — to predict each token given only the tokens before it. To generate, start from nothing and sample one token at a time, feeding each choice back in as context for the next. The picture writes itself, pixel by pixel, exactly like a GPT writes a sentence word by word.
P(image) = ∏ P(token_i | token_1 … token_(i-1))     # a causal transformer; sample left-to-right, feeding each token back in

This inherits everything the language-model world built — one decoder-only transformer, next-token training, the ability to read and write in one model. It also inherits two headaches, and the run shows both.

3 · Seen on a real run — a tiny GPT that writes clothes

We flattened little 16×16 images into sequences and trained a small GPT to predict the next pixel. On held-out data it predicts the next token with 84% accuracy — it has learned the grammar of these images. Now let it write from scratch. The one knob at sampling time is temperature — how boldly it picks each token:

Turn the temperature down and the model plays it safe — samples collapse toward a bland, repetitive average (low detail). Turn it up and it gets reckless — the picture dissolves toward noise (detail shoots past the real images ~0.08). Somewhere in the middle is a sweet spot where the samples look like actual garments. There is no free lunch: boldness buys variety and costs coherence.

The error that snowballs — exposure bias

Here is the deeper quirk. The model was trained on real pixels as context, but when it generates it must feed on its own output — and its own output has mistakes. Give it the true top half of a real image and let it complete the bottom:

Coherent near the seam, drifting far from it. Right below the real pixels the completion is faithful; the further down it generates — the more it is conditioning on its own guesses rather than real data — the more it wanders. Small early errors compound into the rest of the image. This is exposure bias, and taming it (train on the model's own rollouts, inject a global plan) is a huge slice of autoregressive research — exactly the drift that diffusion, refining everything at once, sidesteps.

4 · Why it matters — the language-model paradigm for pixels

Autoregressive generation is the second-largest generative paradigm in vision, behind diffusion — and its trump card is unification: because it is just next-token prediction, one decoder-only transformer can both read images (understanding) and write them (generation), the way an LLM reads and writes text — something diffusion cannot natively do. The cost is speed (one piece at a time) and drift, so most of the field is busy borrowing diffusion's tricks — predict a coarse scale at a time, decode many tokens in parallel, train against your own rollouts — to keep the language-model backbone while closing the gap.

5 · The surprise — a policy that writes its own moves

Point the exact same next-token recipe at control. Turn a robot's continuous motion into a small vocabulary of action tokens, and a transformer can write the robot's behavior one action at a time — a sentence of moves. Now a policy inherits the whole language-model playbook: learn a new task from a few demonstrations in its context, scale like an LLM, unify vision, language, and action in one model.

Same recipe, higher stakes on speed. A chatbot can take a second to finish a sentence; a manipulator cannot — every extra token is latency it pays while the world moves. So the one-token-at-a-time price that is a mild annoyance for images becomes an existential bottleneck for control, which is why robots grow a whole branch of parallel action decoding.

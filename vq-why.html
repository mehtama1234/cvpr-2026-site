Tokenizing the visual world — turning an image into a vocabulary of words

The models that write text and, increasingly, make images and video are next-token predictors: they think in a finite alphabet of discrete symbols. An image is the opposite — a smooth grid of continuous pixels with no symbols anywhere. This page is the plain story of the bridge between them: how you compress a picture into a small grid of visual words drawn from a learned dictionary, watch it rebuilt on a real run, meet the one failure that haunts every such model — a dictionary that quietly goes dead — and see why the very same trick lets a robot describe what it does.

1 · The problem — words are discrete, pictures are not

A language model works because text is already made of discrete pieces: a fixed vocabulary of tokens, and the model just predicts the next one. That single fact — a finite alphabet — is what makes generation, in-context learning, and the whole transformer toolkit work. An image has no alphabet. It is a grid of continuous colour values, and there are infinitely many of them. If you want to bring the transformer's machinery to vision — to generate an image the way a model generates a sentence — you first need to invent an alphabet for pictures.

The problem, stated once: turn a continuous image into a short sequence of discrete symbols from a fixed vocabulary — losing as little as possible — so a picture can be read and written one token at a time, like a sentence.

2 · The reframe — snap each patch to the nearest word in a codebook

The idea: keep a dictionary of a few thousand little vectors — a codebook — and describe every patch of the image by the closest one. An encoder squashes each patch of the image to a vector. Then comes the one move that makes everything discrete: instead of keeping that vector, you snap it to the nearest entry in the codebook and record just that entry's index. The image becomes a small grid of integers — its tokens. A decoder reads the grid back and paints the picture again. The encoder, decoder, and the codebook itself are all learned together, end to end.
patch → encode → snap to nearest codebook vector → its index (a token)     # a 256×256 image becomes, say, a 16×16 grid of integers — a sentence of 256 visual words

Snapping is not differentiable — you can't take the gradient of "pick the nearest" — so training uses a small trick (the straight-through estimator) to pass the gradient past the snap as if it weren't there, plus a term that pulls each encoded patch toward the word it chose. Hold onto that pull: it is exactly where things go wrong.

4 · The failure that haunts every tokenizer — the dictionary goes dead

Here is the honest, measured heart of it. Give the model a dictionary of 512 words and train it the naive way, and something quietly breaks: a handful of words get used for almost everything, and the vast majority are never used at all. You paid for a big vocabulary and the model speaks in a dozen words. Worse, the rebuild is worse for it. The standard fixes fight back — gently updating the words toward what actually lands on them, and reviving any word that has gone unused by resetting it into the thick of the data:

Codebook collapse is the field's signature enemy. Almost every advance in visual tokenizers — lookup-free quantization, rotation tricks, better commitment losses, dictionary reinitialization — is, at bottom, a different way of keeping the vocabulary alive and used. A dead dictionary is wasted capacity and a blurry generator.

They track each other at small K, where every word is needed. But as K grows the naive tokenizer leaves more and more of the dictionary dead — and its quality stalls, then actually drops at the largest size, because it is drowning in words it never learned to use. The revived tokenizer keeps every word alive and its quality keeps climbing. A bigger alphabet is not just wasted but harmful unless you win the collapse fight — the vocabulary is only as large as the part you actually speak.

5 · Why it matters — this is the on-ramp to generating images like text

Once an image is a sequence of tokens, every tool built for language applies to vision. You can train a plain next-token predictor to write an image one visual word at a time; you can compress video by tokenizing time as well as space; you can put images and text in a single vocabulary so one model both reads and draws. In every one of these, the tokenizer is the floor on quality: the generator can never reproduce detail its vocabulary threw away.

6 · The surprise — a robot tokenizes what it does

The trick is not really about images. It is about turning anything continuous into words. In robotics the continuous thing is action — the exact stream of motor commands — and the same quantizer snaps chunks of motion to a codebook of skill words. Now a policy can predict its next move the way a language model predicts its next token, and a library of reusable skills becomes a vocabulary you can compose.

Same trick, different alphabet. In vision, tokenizing turns pixels into words so a model can generate a picture. In robotics, it turns motor commands into words so a model can generate an action — the discrete action heads and skill libraries behind many of the vision-language-action policies.

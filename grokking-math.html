Optimization landscapes and grokking — why it works, mathematically

A network trained on a math task quickly gets every training example right — it looks finished. But on new examples it is no better than guessing; it merely memorized. Then, long after training seemed done, it suddenly 'gets it' and leaps to perfect on new examples too. Why the late, sudden understanding?

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

Grokking is the pattern where a model first memorizes the training examples and only later finds the simple rule that works on new examples. The training score looks good before the model truly understands.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology helps explain why the simple rule may be a cleaner shape in the solution space. Many parameter settings can fit the training data, but only some form a stable, simple region that extends to unseen cases.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

The idea matters in math tasks, code, reasoning, games, and any setting where examples are few but the hidden rule is simple.

Why Optimization landscapes and grokking is worth learning

It matters because early success can be fake. We need to know whether a model learned the rule or merely stored the cases.

The key turn — the principle at work

The stuck point. A model can fit the training set long before it learns the underlying rule. For a while, memorization and understanding can look the same from training loss alone.

The mathematical move. The principle is competing solutions with the same training error. Optimization may first find a memorizing solution, then later drift toward a simpler rule that generalizes better.

What changes. Training stops being judged only by when loss reaches zero. The path through solution space matters, because different zero-loss answers can behave very differently on new data.

The problem, as a quantity

During training a network is scored only on the examples it is shown, and it can reach a perfect score on those while still being useless on anything new — it has memorized the answers rather than learned the rule behind them.

That alone is a puzzle: how can a perfect training score coexist with total failure on new cases? Stranger still is what sometimes happens next. Keep training well past the point where the seen examples are all correct, and much later the network abruptly starts getting new examples right too, jumping from chance to near-perfect. The problem is to understand why fitting the seen examples does not pin down the rule, what tips a network from memorizing toward genuinely understanding, and why that switch, when it comes, arrives as a sudden leap rather than a gradual climb.

The core ideas — and why they work

Fitting the examples you have seen does not pin down the rule

many θ satisfy L_train(θ)=0, but L_test(θ) differs
Here is the root of it. A network is scored during training only on the examples it is shown, and there are many, many different internal settings that all get those seen examples perfectly right.

One kind of setting simply memorizes the answer table — a lookup that is flawless on what it has seen and useless on anything new. Another kind discovers the actual underlying rule, and so gets new examples right as well. Both score a perfect zero error on the training examples, so the training score by itself cannot tell them apart. Getting every seen example right is just not the same thing as understanding, and nothing in the raw training signal distinguishes the two. Which one the network settles into, and why, is what the rest of the page is about.

A gentle pressure toward simplicity decides which solution wins

min θ L_train(θ) + λ||θ||²
Among all the settings that fit the seen examples perfectly, you can tip the balance by adding a mild preference for simpler ones — a steady pressure that keeps the network's internal numbers small, all else being equal.

A memorized lookup table is bulky: storing every individual answer needs a lot of distinct machinery. The clean rule is compact: once found, it explains all the examples with far less. So the simplicity pressure quietly favors the rule over the lookup — but only once the rule has actually been found, and finding it takes time. This is exactly why the network memorizes first (the quick, bulky solution) and only later reorganizes into the rule (the compact one the pressure rewards). The pressure decides the destination; the slow search decides how long the trip takes.

The rule clicks into place when the parts match the problem's structure

f(a,b)=a+b mod p requires equivariance to cyclic shifts
What does 'finding the rule' actually look like inside? For a structured task — say addition that wraps around, like the hours on a clock — the clean solution is a little mechanism whose internal parts mirror the structure of the problem itself: they arrange into the circular pattern that wrap-around addition has.

As training grinds on under the simplicity pressure, the network's pieces gradually rotate toward that matching arrangement, and once they align, a compact general-purpose mechanism exists where before there was only a lookup table. The rule is not installed all at once; the parts drift toward the problem's natural shape until they snap into a working mechanism. Until that alignment happens, the network has nothing general to fall back on and leans on memorization.

The sudden leap is one solution overtaking the other, not magic

test accuracy ≈ 1[circuit strength > memorizer strength]
Why does understanding arrive as a sudden jump rather than a gradual climb? Because two solutions coexist throughout training — the memorized lookup, strong early, and the forming rule, growing slowly in the background — and the network's answer on new examples is dominated by whichever is currently stronger.

For a long stretch the lookup dominates and new examples stay at chance, even as the rule quietly strengthens underneath, invisibly. The moment the growing rule finally overtakes the fading lookup, the network's behavior on new examples flips over almost at once — the leap. It looks like a flash of insight, but it is really a slow, hidden crossover reaching its tipping point. Nothing magical happened at that step; the balance simply tipped from the memorized solution to the general one.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Work on this turns a few dials on the same picture.

How hard the simplicity pressure is. Too little and the network is content to memorize forever; enough and it is eventually pushed to reorganize into the compact rule. How much data. More examples make memorizing relatively harder and the general rule relatively easier to find, shrinking the delay before the leap. What the task's structure is. Tasks with clean underlying structure have a compact rule to snap into; unstructured ones may have none, so understanding never arrives. How the switch is watched. Tracking the hidden strengthening of the rule reveals the crossover coming before the score visibly jumps. The thread never changes: fitting the seen examples underdetermines the rule, and a simplicity pressure plus a slow search decides whether, and when, understanding wins.

A worked numeric example

The run at analysis/grok/toy_grok.json trained modular addition over p=97 (addition where you wrap around after 97). Training accuracy reached 1.0 by step 100, while test accuracy stayed around 0.40 for many checkpoints. Around step 2600, test accuracy crossed 0.968 and then reached 1.0. That gap is the point: fitting the observed table happened quickly; discovering the compact rule took longer.

The through-line. The core puzzle is that getting every seen example right does not reveal the rule — a bulky memorized lookup and a compact general rule both fit the training data perfectly. A gentle simplicity pressure favors the compact rule, but only after a slow search finds it, which is why memorization comes first and understanding comes late. And because the two solutions coexist with the rule strengthening invisibly, the switch to understanding arrives as a sudden crossover, not magic. Two plain ideas carry it: fitting the seen examples underdetermines the rule, so a simplicity pressure picks the general solution over the memorized one; and the late 'aha' is just a hidden compact solution finally overtaking the bulky one.

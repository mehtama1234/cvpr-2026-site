NeRF — how a neural network becomes a 3D scene

Before there were clouds of Gaussian blobs, there was a stranger idea that cracked capturing the real world from photos: store the entire 3D scene inside a neural network. Hand the network a point in space and it tells you the colour and how solid the space is there; to make a picture you march a ray through the scene and add up what the network says along it. This page is the plain story of that idea — the volume-rendering trick at its heart, the one ingredient without which it fails, and the single cost that made a simpler rival overtake it.

1 · The problem — a scene you can re-view from anywhere

You have a handful of photos of a place and you want to fly a camera through it — to see it from angles no photo was taken from. That needs the actual 3D scene behind the pictures, and a way to draw it. A mesh of triangles is hard to recover accurately and bad at fuzzy things; storing every viewing angle is impossible. You want a compact, continuous representation you can render from any new viewpoint.

The problem, stated once: from a few photos, build a representation of a 3D scene that can be rendered from any new viewpoint — compact, continuous, and recoverable by ordinary optimization.

2 · The reframe — the scene is a function you query

The idea: don't store the scene — store a function that answers questions about it. Train a small network that takes a 3D point (and a viewing direction) and returns the colour and the density (how solid space is) there. Nothing is ever placed in space; the whole scene lives in the network's weights. To render a pixel, shoot a ray into the scene, sample many points along it, ask the network about each, and blend the colours front-to-back weighted by how much each point blocks the light — that summing step is volume rendering. The network is trained by comparing its rendered pixels to the real photos.
colour(ray) = Σ T_i · (1 − exp(−σ_i)) · c_i T_i = exp(−Σ_(j<i) σ_j)     # march the ray, query (σ, c) at each sample, accumulate colour by how much light survives

It is a beautiful idea — a continuous scene, recoverable from photos by gradient descent. It has one ingredient it cannot live without, and one cost that eventually cost it its crown. The run shows both.

3 · Seen on a real run — the trick, and the cost

1 · A coordinate network cannot see sharp edges — unless you help it

We built a little 2D scene of coloured blobs, rendered a handful of views of it through the volume-rendering equation, and asked a small coordinate network to learn the whole scene from those views. Then we rendered a brand-new angle it never saw. We did it twice — once feeding raw coordinates to the network, once wrapping them in Fourier features (a bank of sines and cosines at many frequencies):

with Fourier features — 34.31 dB
the true scene

The plain network is a blur — novel-view quality 23.25 dB — because a small MLP fed raw coordinates is biased toward smooth, low-frequency functions and simply cannot represent a crisp edge. Wrap the input in Fourier features and the same size network snaps the scene into focus (34.31 dB). Positional encoding is not a nicety; without it a neural field cannot see detail at all. This one trick is what made NeRF work.

2 · The cost that Gaussian splatting beat

Here is the catch that reshaped the field. To colour a single pixel, a NeRF must sample many points along its ray and query the network at every one — in this toy, 48 network evaluations per pixel (3,072 to render one small view). A splatting renderer, by contrast, just projects each of its 4 primitives onto the screen once and blends — no per-pixel network at all. That gap — hundreds of queries per pixel versus one project per blob — is exactly why Gaussian splatting overtook NeRF as the default way to capture a scene.

4 · Why it matters — and why it stepped aside

NeRF proved you could capture the real world as a continuous function and render it photorealistically — and it lit off an explosion of neural-field work. But its per-pixel query cost was a wall, and Gaussian splatting — which paints the scene with explicit blobs and rasterizes them in one pass — is far faster, so it took over ordinary photorealistic capture. Neural fields did not disappear; they retreated to where a continuous coordinate-function is genuinely the right model: 4D dynamics, pose-free surfaces, and non-RGB physical signals — X-ray attenuation, radio waves, plasma around a black hole — where there are simply no blobs to splat.

5 · The surprise — a field a robot queries for free space

In vision you query the field for colour — a prettier new view. A robot asks it a different question: where is there free space, and where is the surface? The same coordinate-in network, trained to output a signed distance instead of colour, answers is-this-spot-free and how-far-to-the-nearest-obstacle in a single query — and because it is smooth and differentiable, those answers pour straight into a motion planner.

Same field, a map to act in. A robot grows the neural field online from its own moving sensor and queries it for the geometry it must grasp, avoid, and plan around — and NeRF's slow per-ray cost is a real obstacle a robot cannot afford, which is exactly why many moved to splatting or hybrid maps. Vision asks the field for a picture; a robot asks it where it can go. The in-the-wild page lays out both sides.

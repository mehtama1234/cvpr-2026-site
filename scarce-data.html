Learning from scarce data — making a little carry a lot

When you can't get more examples, you concentrate the information you have — into the data, the model's structure, or a handful of parameters. The connecting idea behind a whole cluster of CVPR 2026 work.

The problem — from first principles

Deep learning runs on examples. A model does not "understand" a cat the way you do; it sees tens of thousands of cats until the statistical shape of "cat" settles into its weights. That recipe works beautifully when examples are cheap and endless. But most problems that actually matter are the opposite. A rare disease has two hundred scans in the world, not two million. A new product has no labeled photos yet. A robot is shown a task a handful of times. A deployed model must learn a new category tomorrow without being allowed to revisit everything it learned yesterday. In every one of these, the usual move — pour in more data — is simply not available.

The lazy conclusion is that less data must mean a worse model, full stop. This whole cluster of work is a sustained argument that this is false — if you are careful about where the information actually lives. Here is the key observation. Raw data is enormously redundant. Fifty thousand cat photos do not contain fifty thousand photos' worth of distinct, useful information — most of it is repeated, correlated, or irrelevant to the decision the model has to make. So the quantity that truly governs learning is not the number of examples. It is the amount of non-redundant, decision-relevant information those examples carry.

That reframes the entire problem. Scarcity of data is not really the enemy; low information density is. And density is something you can raise. Information can be moved — packed into fewer examples, folded into fewer parameters, preserved as a geometric structure — without being destroyed. If you can make each example, each parameter, and each training step carry more, then a few of them can do the work of many.

So the real question stops being "how do I get more data?" and becomes "how do I concentrate the information I already have?" Every approach below is a different place to do that concentration.

The approaches — five places to concentrate information

The papers here look unrelated on the surface — one synthesizes fake images, one stops a model forgetting, one adapts a medical model, one generates network weights. But they are all answering the same question, each by concentrating information into a different container.

1 · Concentrate it into the data — dataset distillation

Don't train on the real images. Manufacture a tiny set of synthetic 'super-examples' engineered so that training on them reproduces training on the whole dataset.

The idea sounds almost like cheating: replace 50,000 real photos with, say, 100 or 500 artificial images — sometimes as few as ten per class — chosen so that a model trained on the fakes ends up nearly as good as one trained on the originals. The synthetic images are not meant to look natural. They are optimized to carry the dataset's teaching signal in as few pixels as possible, so they come out looking like dense, blended prototypes.

The deep question is: what does it even mean for a small set to 'stand in' for a big one? Each paper answers differently. Dataset Distillation by Influence Matching — the paper we run live below — says the synthetic set is right when it has the same influence on the final trained weights as the real data: don't match how the images look, match where they push the model. Beyond Soft Label (Orthogonal Gradient Matching) argues the direction of the learning signal matters more than its size, and aligns gradient directions instead of whole gradients, avoiding over-smoothed synthetic data. Multimodal Distribution Matching extends distillation to paired image-and-text data, matching the joint distribution rather than one modality. The shared move across all of them: first define precisely what the real data does to a model, then build the smallest synthetic set that does the same thing.

2 · Concentrate it into the model's structure — exemplar-free incremental learning

A different scarcity: not too few examples, but no permission to keep them. Preserve the geometry of what was learned, and you can throw the old data away.

Sometimes the constraint isn't that data is rare — it's that you're not allowed to store it. A model has to learn new classes over time (privacy rules, limited memory, or the old data is simply gone), and it must not forget the old ones. Train naively on the new classes and the new learning overwrites the old — the classic failure called catastrophic forgetting.

The insight that unites this group: you do not need the old examples if you preserve the old structure — the geometric arrangement in the model's internal representation that holds different classes apart. Exemplar-Free Class Incremental Learning via Preserving Class-Discriminative Structure does exactly this, explicitly protecting both the spread within a class and the separation between classes so new learning can't collapse the old boundaries. HAD self-distills at each stage to carry forward knowledge even across different kinds of tasks. Octopus prevents forgetting by steering each new task's gradient to be orthogonal to old ones — with no stored history or replay buffer at all. Quantized Residuals to Continuous Prompts tackles the hardest version — new classes from only a few samples — by turning class-distinguishing cues into discrete codes it recomposes into prompts. The shared move: keep the shape of what was learned; discard the data that made it.

3 · Concentrate it into a few parameters — minimal fine-tuning

Don't retrain a giant model on your small task. Freeze it and tune tiny inserted modules on a handful of labels.

Adapting a billion-parameter model to a narrow task the ordinary way needs a lot of task data and tends to overfit when you don't have it. The alternative: freeze the whole model and train only small modules — adapters — slipped into it. Because you're fitting so few new numbers, a few labeled examples are enough, and the frozen model supplies all the general knowledge.

Towards Efficient Medical Reasoning with Minimal Fine-Tuning Data is the clean case: it adapts a large pretrained vision model to read medical scans using adapter-based few-shot learning and only a small handful of labeled examples — precisely because labeled medical data is expensive and scarce. This is the same modular-correction principle we take apart in depth on the mixture-of-LoRA page: a small correction can steer a big frozen model, and a small correction needs little data to fit. Few-shot prompt-learning papers in the corpus push the same lever from the input side.

4 · Concentrate it into a generator of learning — weight generation

The most radical: skip training on the new task's data entirely, and instead predict the weights a trained model would have had.

If a trained network is ultimately just a set of numbers, can you produce those numbers for a new task without running the usual data-hungry training loop? Learning to Learn Weight Generation via Local Consistency Diffusion does this — it uses a diffusion process, meta-learned across many tasks, to generate network weights for a new task, learning from the intermediate steps of past training runs so the generated weights actually converge to something usable. It turns 'collect data and train' into 'describe the task and synthesize the solution' — the same instinct as SG-LoRA generating a LoRA from a text description on the mixture-of-LoRA page, applied to whole networks.

5 · The guardrail — don't lose the prior you started from

Almost all scarce-data learning leans on a big pretrained model as a reservoir of general knowledge. Protecting that reservoir is part of the problem.

Every approach above quietly depends on a pretrained model already holding a vast amount of general knowledge — that reservoir is what lets a few examples or a few parameters go so far. So a real danger is spending it without noticing. The Blind Spot of Adaptation makes this concrete: fine-tuning a vision-language model on a narrow task (driving) silently erodes the broad world-knowledge it needed for exactly the rare, long-tail cases that matter most — a self-defeating loop. It builds the first benchmark to measure that forgetting and routes adaptation through prompt-space to keep the prior intact. The lesson runs through the whole cluster: when you concentrate hard on the little you have, take care not to spill the great deal you already had.

The through-line. Compress a dataset into super-examples; fold a whole learning history into a preserved geometry; pack an entire fine-tuning run into a few adapter weights; or synthesize the weights outright. It is one operation wearing four costumes: separate information from redundancy, and keep only the information. Make every sample, every parameter, and every bit of computation count.

We ran one — InfMatch, live

Talk is cheap, so we ran Dataset Distillation by Influence Matching end-to-end on a GPU. Reduced from the paper's research scale (3 expert networks instead of 20, 1,000 condensation steps instead of 200,000, 10 synthetic images per class), but the real method: freeze a network, then optimize a tiny synthetic CIFAR-10 set so its influence on the trained weights matches the full 50,000-image dataset.

The result: a fresh network trained only on those 100 synthetic images (right) reaches ~61% top-1 accuracy on the real 10,000-image CIFAR-10 test set. Look closely at the distilled panel versus the real one: the images have been softened and pushed toward dense, class-representative composites — no longer natural photos, but carriers of the dataset's teaching signal. That is 50,000 images' worth of learning pressure squeezed into 100 engineered ones. (A full-scale run — 20 experts, 200,000 iterations, 50 images/class — pushes this well higher; ours is a deliberately small, fast reproduction.)

Reproduce it: colab_experiments/infmatch_run.py — clones the repo, rebuilds CIFAR from a fast mirror, runs pretrain→condense→eval on one GPU.

Where it stands

Basically working: few-shot adaptation via adapters/prompts (approach 3) is mature and already deployed; dataset distillation (approach 1) produces genuinely impressive results at small scale (1–50 images per class) and is the most active, fastest-moving corner.

Still open: distilled sets still degrade at larger scale and higher compression, and transferring a distilled set across model architectures is fragile — which is the exact gap InfMatch targets by matching influence rather than trajectories. Exemplar-free incremental learning still trails methods allowed to keep a few real examples. Weight generation is early and unproven at scale. The honest frontier for all of it: moving from "clever on small benchmarks" to "reliable at production scale."

1 · Keep the senses separate

When one model must juggle image tokens, text tokens, and sometimes audio or 3D geometry all at once, a single shared network tends to blur those modalities together, so the features it learns for a picture start bleeding into the features it learns for words. That overlap is interference: the senses fight for the same weights and none of them ends up sharp. The papers here take a different route. They split the network into many smaller experts and add a router that decides, for each token, which specialists should handle it. Some route by modality (text-biased experts, vision-biased experts), some add a dedicated geometry expert alongside a semantic one, and some adapt to whichever modalities happen to be present in a given sample. The payoff is sparse scaling: the model can grow to many experts while each token only ever touches a few, so it keeps its senses separate instead of smearing them together. This is one of the largest and most active clusters of Mixture-of-Experts work in vision.
CVPR'26Soft Modality-Guided Expert Specialization in MoE-VLMs
Problem
Mixture-of-Experts vision-language models add lots of experts, but nobody tells those experts which sense to specialize in. Left alone, the experts end up learning nearly the same thing for both text tokens and image tokens, so the specialization is wasted. That overlap also means tokens get shuffled across more experts than necessary, which piles on communication cost when the experts are spread over many machines.
Approach
This work, called SMoES, nudges experts to specialize by scoring how text-like or image-like each token is, but softly, without hard-wiring any token to a fixed expert. It computes that modality score two ways at once: one tracks how modality labels flow through the model's attention, and the other fits a running Gaussian for each layer and asks how well a token fits the text versus vision distribution. Experts are then sorted into text-biased and vision-biased bins. During training a mutual-information objective pushes the token's modality and the bin it lands in to line up, so specialization emerges rather than being forced. Because tokens stop wandering across the wrong experts, the model routes far more cleanly.
Why it matters
By gently steering text and image tokens toward different experts, the model stops the two senses from overwriting each other and cuts expert-to-expert communication by 56.1% while nudging accuracy up on both multimodal and language-only tasks. It is a clean demonstration that keeping the senses separate makes sparse scaling both sharper and cheaper.

CVPR'26G2VLM: Geometry Grounded Vision Language Model with Unified 3D Reconstruction and Spatial Reasoning
Problem
Vision-language models are good at naming what is in a picture but weak at reasoning about where things are in space, because they only ever learn geometry implicitly from flat 2D image-text pairs. They never actually learn to reconstruct the 3D shape of a scene, so their spatial understanding stays shallow. Collecting 3D labels at test time to fix this is impractical.
Approach
G2VLM borrows a lesson from human vision, which splits into a what pathway for recognition and a where pathway for spatial location, and builds two matching experts inside one model. A Geometric Perception Expert is trained from scratch to reconstruct real 3D structure from 2D images, producing depth maps, 3D point maps, and camera poses. Alongside it a Semantic Perception Expert, initialized from a pretrained vision-language backbone, handles language and recognition. The two experts share the same self-attention layers in every block, so the geometry the first expert perceives can quietly inform the reasoning the second expert does. Training goes in two stages: first the geometry expert learns on 3D datasets while the semantic side is frozen, then both are fine-tuned together on spatial tasks.
Why it matters
Giving spatial geometry its own dedicated expert, rather than hoping it emerges from a single shared stream, lets a small 2B-parameter model beat much larger systems on spatial reasoning. It shows that a whole extra sense, 3D perception, can be added as a specialist expert without disturbing the language one.

CVPR'26MOON2.0: Dynamic Modality-balanced Multimodal Representation Learning for E-commerce Product Understanding
Problem

CVPR'26OneCAT: Decoder-Only Auto-Regressive Model for Unified Understanding and Generation
Problem
Most multimodal systems bolt together separate pipelines, one to understand images and another to generate them, often leaning on extra external parts like a vision encoder and a diffusion module. That modular design creates bottlenecks that limit how deeply the modalities can fuse and slows inference down. OneCAT asks whether a single clean architecture can do understanding, generation, and editing at once.
Approach
OneCAT is a pure decoder-only autoregressive model with no external encoders at all, turning raw images into tokens with just lightweight patch embeddings. Inside that single backbone sits a modality-specific Mixture-of-Experts that sends three different token types to three sets of specialists: continuous vision tokens for understanding, discrete vision tokens for generation, and text tokens for language. Because the routing happens inside one decoder, the modalities can fuse early and deeply rather than being stitched together at the end. A scale-aware adapter lets the model generate images coarse-to-fine, from low resolution up to high, and multi-stage training first warms up the individual experts before a unified objective ties them together.
Why it matters
Putting modality-specific experts inside one decoder-only backbone lets a single model understand, generate, and edit images while running much faster than diffusion-based rivals, especially at high resolution. It shows that keeping the senses separate at the expert level and unifying them at the architecture level are not in conflict.

2 · The fragile heart

The router is the part of a Mixture-of-Experts that reads each incoming piece of data and decides which experts should handle it, and it is where the whole design lives or dies. When routing goes wrong, most of the experts sit idle while a favorite few do all the work, so the extra capacity you paid for is simply wasted. Worse, in a sparse model the router only gets a learning signal through the experts it actually picked, so the unselected ones stop receiving useful gradients and never improve. Left alone, this feedback loop makes the router collapse onto a small handful of experts and lock the rest out for good. The papers in this cluster all attack the same weak spot from different angles, redesigning how inputs get assigned so that routing becomes content-aligned, RL-optimized, teacher-distilled, and drift-aware. This is the most methodologically deep corner of the MoE work, and the recurring goal is the same: routing that stays stable and spreads out, so the model genuinely uses the capacity it was built with.
CVPR'26ERMoE: Eigen-Reparameterized Mixture-of-Experts for Stable Routing and Interpretable Specialization
Problem
Ordinary MoE routers learn a separate set of gating weights that decide which expert gets each token, but those weights drift out of sync with what the experts actually represent, so the router keeps picking the wrong expert. The usual patch is an auxiliary load-balancing loss that forces work to spread evenly, but this fights against specialization and drags down accuracy, and even then experiments show routers frequently choose suboptimal experts.
Approach
ERMoE rebuilds each expert inside a learned orthonormal eigenbasis, meaning every expert is described by its own clean, non-overlapping set of representational directions. Instead of a separate learned gate, it routes by an Eigenbasis Score — the cosine similarity between the token and each expert's basis — so a token simply goes to the expert whose subspace it already aligns with. Because assignment is tied directly to what each expert represents, utilization stabilizes on its own and the auxiliary balancing loss can be thrown out entirely. Enforcing near-orthogonality inside each expert also cuts redundant, overlapping features and prevents the representation collapse that plagues shared-gate designs. A medical variant, ERMoE-ba, carries the idea into a 3D vision transformer for brain-age prediction from MRI.
Why it matters
Routing by alignment rather than by a fragile learned gate keeps expert loads flat and specializations meaningful without any balancing crutch, so the whole expert pool stays in use. It reaches state-of-the-art on ImageNet and image-text retrieval and lifts brain-age prediction by over 7% with anatomically interpretable experts.

CVPR'26Teacher-Guided Routing for Sparse Vision Mixture-of-Experts
Problem
In a sparse vision MoE, the router only receives an informative gradient through the experts it happened to select, so every route it did not pick gets almost no feedback. This gradient blocking makes routing decisions jitter from step to step, lets experts collapse, and leaves the router unable to learn a stable, well-spread assignment.
Approach
TGR-MoE brings in a pretrained dense teacher model — one where every expert-equivalent is always active — to supply the global guidance the sparse router cannot get on its own. A teacher router is built from the frozen teacher's intermediate features and trained with load-balancing and entropy losses so it produces a smooth, well-balanced routing distribution. The student's sparse router then learns by distilling that teacher distribution through a KL-divergence loss, effectively getting a full-coverage target instead of the partial signal gradient blocking allows. Crucially the student router is not frozen, so it still adapts while being pulled toward the teacher's stable pattern, which damps the step-to-step routing fluctuations and keeps expert assignments semantically consistent.
Why it matters
By borrowing a dense model's complete view of routing, the sparse router escapes the trap where unselected experts never get feedback, so training stays stable as more experts are added. It consistently beats standard V-MoE across model sizes and keeps improving where V-MoE saturates, with far less routing fluctuation.

CVPR'26MoE-GRPO: Optimizing Mixture-of-Experts via Reinforcement Learning in Vision-Language Models
Problem
Vision-language MoEs normally route with deterministic top-K selection, which greedily hands each token to its current best-scoring experts every time. That greediness overlooks better expert combinations and causes the same few experts to overfit, so the diverse pool is never really explored or used.
Approach
MoE-GRPO reframes expert selection as a sequential decision-making problem and optimizes it with reinforcement learning, specifically Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Rather than always taking the top scorers, the router becomes a policy that explores different expert choices and is shaped by reward-based feedback tied to how well the model actually performs. This lets it discover strong expert combinations that deterministic top-K would never try, breaking the overfitting loop. To keep RL exploration from destabilizing training, a modality-aware router guidance mechanism discourages wandering into experts that a given modality rarely activates. The result is a routing policy tuned for genuine multimodal understanding instead of a fixed greedy rule.
Why it matters
Treating routing as a learnable policy lets the model reach past the top-K favorites and actually recruit its full set of experts, spreading load and specialization. It consistently outperforms top-K routing and its variants across image and video benchmarks while mitigating expert overfitting.

CVPR'26On Token's Dilemma: Dynamic MoE with Drift-Aware Token Assignment for Continual Learning of Large Vision Language Models
Problem
When a large vision-language MoE keeps learning new tasks, it adds fresh experts but suffers routing-drift: tokens from old tasks get wrongly pulled toward the newly added experts, so the model forgets what it already knew. The hidden cause is at the token level — some tokens are genuinely new, but others are ambiguous or clearly old and should never have been sent to a new expert.
Approach
LLaVA-DyMoE diagnoses each token by how ambiguous its routing scores are, using a relative-difference measure between its affinity for new versus old experts. Token Assignment Guidance (TAG) then sorts tokens into three groups — clearly new, clearly old, and ambiguous — and routes the ambiguous and old ones back to the old experts by masking out the wrong choices, so only truly new tokens reach the new experts. A second piece, Routing Score Regularization (RSR), adds an exclusivity loss that pushes old-expert and new-expert activations apart for clean separation and a specialization loss that still encourages healthy use of the new experts. The whole scheme sits on MoE layers built from LoRA experts inside LLaVA, so it stays lightweight.
Why it matters
By catching the ambiguous drift tokens and steering them away from new experts, the router stops leaking old knowledge into fresh capacity, keeping each task's experts stable. It delivers a 7% gain in mean final accuracy and cuts forgetting by 12% across eight VQA tasks on the CoIN benchmark.

3 · Keep every expert working, cheaply

A Mixture-of-Experts splits one big network into many small experts and asks a router to send each input to just a few of them, so you get the capacity of a huge model while only paying to run a slice. But left alone, the router misbehaves: it discovers a handful of experts that already work and keeps piling every input onto them, while the rest go untouched and never learn anything useful. That is load-balance collapse — the experts start identical (expert symmetry) and stay identical (homogenization), so the extra capacity you paid for is quietly wasted. It is the exact mirror of codebook collapse in quantizers, where a few codes soak up all the traffic and the rest die. One line of work attacks utilization: seed each expert differently from the start, break the homogenization by binding experts to genuinely different jobs, and drop the crude balance-penalty losses that force uniform routing at the cost of real specialization. The other line attacks cost, because MoE only pays off if you can skip the low-value experts at inference and turn an existing dense model into an MoE cleanly rather than retraining from scratch. Both are the same fight — making the sparse capacity you were promised actually real.
CVPR'26Enhancing Mixture-of-Experts Specialization via Cluster-Aware Upcycling
Problem
Upcycling turns a trained dense model into an MoE by copying its weights into every expert, which is far cheaper than training an MoE from scratch. But this gives every expert an identical starting point, and the router starts from random noise with no reason to prefer one expert over another. So the experts stay symmetric and barely specialize in early training — the collapse problem baked in from the very first step.
Approach
Instead of cloning the dense weights blindly, this work first looks at how the dense model actually uses its inputs: it runs the model, collects the internal activations, and groups them into semantic clusters using spherical k-means. Each expert is then seeded from the subspace of one cluster (via a truncated SVD of that cluster's activations), so every expert begins life already tilted toward a different slice of the input world. The router is not left random either — its weights are set to the cluster centroids, so from step one it knows which expert owns which kind of input. An expert-ensemble self-distillation loss then keeps training stable by having the ensemble of experts teach the individuals on tokens the router is unsure about. The whole point is to break expert symmetry at initialization by borrowing the structure the dense model already learned.
Why it matters
Measured against standard upcycling, this produces much lower inter-expert similarity and more confident routing — meaning the experts genuinely differ and the router genuinely uses them, so the MoE's promised capacity is not wasted on redundant clones. It shows that fighting collapse can start before training even begins, in how you seed the experts.

CVPR'26VidPrism: Heterogeneous Mixture of Experts for Image-to-Video Transfer
Problem
When you adapt an image-trained vision-language model to video, you need experts that handle motion over time, not just static pictures. But a conventional MoE fails here through expert homogenization: every expert drifts into being the same generalist, all chewing on the same undifferentiated video stream and learning heavily overlapping features. The capacity is there but nobody carves out a distinct job, so the temporal modeling stays weak.
Approach
VidPrism refuses to let the experts be interchangeable and instead makes the MoE heterogeneous by design — each expert is bound to a different temporal scale, one pathway for slow static content and another for fast motion, echoing the brain's two-stream split between what a scene contains and how it moves. To make that binding real, a content-aware multi-rate sampling module feeds each pathway a different input: a semantically rich low-rate stream of keyframes for the spatial expert and a motion-dense high-rate stream for the motion expert. Because the experts literally see different data at different rates, they cannot collapse into the same generalist. A bidirectional fusion mechanism then lets the two pathways trade information so the specialization does not fragment the model. The result is experts with clear, distinct roles rather than a homogeneous blur.
Why it matters
By tying each expert to a genuinely different temporal job, VidPrism gets clear expert specialization — attention sharpens onto key action moments — and reaches state-of-the-art video recognition, showing that structural heterogeneity, not a balance-penalty loss, is what actually defeats homogenization.

CVPR'26MoDES: Accelerating Mixture-of-Experts Multimodal Large Language Models via Dynamic Expert Skipping
Problem
MoE multimodal models are supposed to be cheap because only a few experts fire per token, yet in practice they still run heavy and slow. The obvious fix — skip the least useful experts — badly hurts accuracy when applied naively, because it ignores two facts: experts matter very differently from layer to layer, and text tokens behave nothing like vision tokens. A crude uniform skip throws away important experts in one layer while keeping useless ones in another.
Approach
MoDES is a training-free method that decides, per layer and per modality, which experts are safe to skip at inference — no retraining, just smarter gating. Its globally-modulated local gating folds a global sense of each layer's overall importance into the local routing probabilities, so an expert in a low-value layer is judged more skeptically than the same-looking expert in a critical one. Its dual-modality thresholding sets separate skipping thresholds for text and vision tokens, respecting that the two modalities lean on experts differently. Choosing all these thresholds could take days of search, so a frontier search algorithm exploits a monotonicity property to cut the search from over two days to about two hours. The whole design targets the low-value experts specifically, rather than skipping a fixed fraction everywhere.
Why it matters
This is the cost side made real: at extreme skipping ratios above 80% MoDES still improves accuracy while delivering a 2.16x prefill and 1.26x decode speedup, proving the sparse-inference savings MoE promises only materialize when you skip the right experts, not just any experts.

CVPR'26OneSparse: A Unified Framework for Sparse Activation Layers in Vision Models
Problem
Two different tricks both fire only part of the network per input: MoE, which routes tokens to dynamic experts, and memory modules, which look answers up in a key-value bank. They grew up separately, so nobody could compare them fairly or combine their strengths — MoE is expressive but expensive, memory is cheap but rigid.
Approach
OneSparse shows the two are the same recipe under the hood — dispatch, process, combine — where you send tokens to sparse units, transform them, and merge the results, differing only in whether the processing is dynamic (experts) or a static lookup (memory). Casting both into this one abstraction turns them into endpoints of a single continuous design space rather than rival architectures. It then builds the Nexus Layer inside that space: a unified router uses cross-attention to send tokens to a mix of heterogeneous units — some experts, some memory groups — while explicitly producing balanced utilization so no unit is starved. An adaptive strategy lets cheap memory units give every token a coarse answer while costly experts refine only the semantically important regions. The layer thus spends expensive computation exactly where it earns its keep and falls back to cheap lookup elsewhere.
Why it matters
Because routing is balanced and cost is matched to importance, Nexus beats the strongest MoE baseline with 20% less computation and outperforms memory models using only 70% of the parameters — a concrete demonstration that unifying the sparse-layer design space, rather than picking one flavor, is what makes the efficiency promise real.

4 · One model, many jobs

The single biggest use of Mixture-of-Experts in vision is not saving compute — it is specialization, giving one model a way to cover jobs that pull in different directions. A 3D model has to handle indoor rooms, outdoor streets, single objects, and moving humans; a tracker has to read color, depth, thermal, and event cameras; a lifelong learner has to absorb new tasks without erasing the old ones; a unified image model has to both invent new pictures and make small edits to existing ones. When you force all of that through one shared backbone, the shared weights become a compromise that is mediocre at everything, because tuning for one sub-problem quietly hurts another — the technical name for this is interference. MoE attacks that interference head-on: instead of one set of weights doing every job, you keep several small expert modules, and a lightweight router looks at each input and sends it to the experts that fit it, so specialists coexist inside one model instead of being averaged into a blur. The same split rescues image generation too, where the fight is between generating fresh content and faithfully preserving what should stay — separate experts let each goal be served without one overwriting the other.

CVPR'26MoRE: 3D Visual Geometry Reconstruction Meets Mixture-of-Experts
Problem

A single feed-forward model that reconstructs 3D geometry from images has to handle wildly different scenes — cramped indoor rooms, open outdoor streets, a single object on a table, moving people, and dynamic scenes. When all of that is squeezed through one shared dense network, the shared weights become a compromise that captures none of these domains well, which caps how far the model can scale.

Approach

MoRE takes a strong dense 3D transformer backbone (VGGT, already pretrained on large-scale 3D data) and replaces its plain feed-forward blocks with Mixture-of-Experts layers: each layer holds several expert modules, and a tiny linear router scores every image token and sends it to the top few experts, whose outputs are blended by those scores. Different experts effectively become tuned to different kinds of scene, so scaling up the model adds capacity for the diverse domains without a proportional jump in compute per token, and a load-balancing penalty stops the router from lazily piling everything onto one expert. On top of the MoE, MoRE cleans up its supervision — it uses confidence masks to ignore corrupted depth labels, and it fuses DINOv2 semantic features with the 3D backbone to sharpen surface normals. Training runs in two stages: first learn all the geometry tasks with a plain dense network, then switch on the experts for a robust refinement pass.

Why it matters

By letting each scene type lean on its own experts, one 3D foundation model stops flattening indoor, outdoor, object, and human scenes into a single compromise — and it posts state-of-the-art numbers across pointmap, depth, camera pose, and normal benchmarks at once.

CVPR'26UETrack: A Unified and Efficient Framework for Single Object Tracking
Problem

Most fast single-object trackers only understand ordinary color video and fall apart when the input is depth, thermal, or event-camera data, while the trackers that do handle those extra modalities are too heavy and slow to run on modest hardware. That leaves a real gap: no single tracker that is both multi-modal and efficient enough to deploy widely.

Approach

UETrack packs all the modalities into one model and uses a Token-Pooling-based Mixture-of-Experts block to aggregate features, so that different experts can specialize in the quirks of different signal types instead of one shared network trying to cover them all. Color, depth, thermal, and event streams are folded into a compact 6-channel input by concatenation, while language cues are encoded with CLIP, and the MoE routing lets the appropriate experts light up for whichever modality is present. To keep it fast and small, UETrack adds Target-aware Adaptive Distillation, which only distills knowledge on the samples that actually need the extra supervision rather than on every frame, cutting redundant training signal. The result is a modular design that avoids the bulky, domain-specific machinery earlier multi-modal trackers relied on.

Why it matters

One tracker can then serve color, depth, thermal, event, and language inputs without a bespoke network per modality — hitting 69.2% AUC on LaSOT while still running in real time on GPU, CPU, and an embedded Jetson AGX.

CVPR'26Spectral Mixture-of-Experts for Continual Learning
Problem

A model that must learn a stream of tasks one after another tends to overwrite what it already knew each time it learns something new — the classic problem of catastrophic forgetting. A single shared set of weights has nowhere to keep the old skill safe while it bends to fit the new one.

Approach

This work builds a spectral Mixture-of-Experts, where the routing splits inputs along their spectral components (their frequency structure) and hands different frequency bands, or task subspaces, to different experts. Because each incoming task tends to occupy its own spectral region, the routing naturally steers it toward its own experts, so a new task is written into a fresh slice of the model rather than on top of the parameters an earlier task depends on. Each expert thus specializes on the portion of the signal it owns, and the previously learned experts are left largely undisturbed as new ones take up the new work. That per-task separation is exactly what protects earlier knowledge across a long sequence of tasks.

Why it matters

Giving each sequential task its own specialized experts means the lifelong learner adds new skills without erasing the old ones, directly reducing catastrophic forgetting.

CVPR'26TAG-MoE: Task-Aware Gating for Unified Generative Mixture-of-Experts
Problem

Unified image models that both generate new pictures and edit existing ones suffer badly from task interference: one shared parameter space is pulled between conflicting goals, like making a small local edit while preserving everything else versus inventing a whole new subject-driven image. Ordinary MoE routing does not fix this, because it is task-agnostic — it routes on local pixel-level features alone and never knows which of these opposed jobs it is doing.

Approach

TAG-MoE makes the router aware of the task itself. A Hierarchical Task Semantic Annotation scheme breaks each request into structured descriptors — its scope, its type, and what must be preserved — giving the model an explicit, high-level label for what kind of generation-or-editing job this is. Then Predictive Alignment Regularization ties the router's internal, per-token decisions to those task-level descriptors, training the gating network to shift from blind local routing toward dispatch that reflects the global intent. In effect, generate-style requests and edit-style requests get steered to different experts, so the parameters that invent new content stop colliding with the parameters that preserve existing content. Experts end up specialized per task rather than forced into one compromise.

Why it matters

By tying local routing to what the task actually is, a single unified model stops fighting itself between generating and editing — letting each goal have its own experts and reaching state-of-the-art results across five generation-and-editing benchmarks.

5 · Stairs and gravel without forgetting

A legged robot has to do the same job — stay upright and move forward — on ground that behaves completely differently from one step to the next: flat pavement, stairs, slopes, loose gravel, and it must switch gaits on the fly. The trouble is that if you train one dense network to handle all of it, the behaviors interfere with each other. What the network learns to keep its footing on stairs partly un-learns what it knew about flat ground, because both skills are written into the same shared weights and pull them in opposite directions. This tug-of-war has a name: gradient conflict — the update that helps one terrain actively hurts another, so training stalls and the robot ends up mediocre everywhere. The fix that keeps showing up in robotics is Mixture-of-Experts: give each terrain or gait its own expert sub-policy, and route to the right one at runtime — often by inferring the terrain from the robot's own joint and actuator history, with no cameras at all. Because each expert keeps its own capacity, the stair skill and the flat-ground skill stop overwriting each other, and you can even compose or migrate between them. This is where robotics MoE is most convincing and most explicitly tied to gradient conflict; the real win isn't a leaderboard number, it's a robot that can switch skills at all without one destroying the last.
ROBOTICS'25MoE-Loco: Mixture of Experts for Multitask Locomotion
Problem
Legged robots need one controller that handles many terrains and many gaits, but training a single policy for all of them at once causes gradient conflicts in multitask reinforcement learning. The learning signal for one behavior fights the signal for another because they share the same weights, which drags down both final performance and training efficiency.
Approach
MoE-Loco drops a Mixture-of-Experts architecture inside the reinforcement-learning policy itself, so that individual experts specialize in distinct locomotion behaviors instead of everything piling into one shared network. Because each expert carries its own capacity, the conflicting gradients from different terrains and gaits no longer overwrite each other — the update for a bipedal gait lands on a different expert than the update for a quadrupedal one. This specialization is not hand-assigned; the mixture naturally sorts behaviors into separate experts as it trains. The result is a policy that spans both quadrupedal and bipedal gaits across diverse terrain, and that supports task migration and skill composition. It was validated in simulation and deployed on real hardware.
Why it matters
This is the cleanest statement of the whole theme: MoE works in locomotion precisely because it mitigates gradient conflict, letting one robot hold many gaits at once without any single skill destroying the others.

ROBOTICS'25VMTS: Vision-Assisted Teacher-Student Reinforcement Learning for Multi-Terrain Locomotion in Bipedal Robots
Problem

By giving each terrain its own teacher and only then merging into a vision policy, VMTS sidesteps the conflict of training one dense network on every surface — the specialization survives into a robot that can walk on many terrains from what it sees.

Problem

ROBOTICS'25MOVE: Multi-Skill Omnidirectional Legged Locomotion With Limited View in 3D Environments
Problem

6 · Skills and scenarios that stop fighting

Picture one manipulation policy that must pick, place, insert and pour — or a big vision-language-action model fine-tuned on a pile of mixed-quality demonstrations. Cram all of that into one dense network and you get task interference: the gradients from learning to pour tug the weights away from what made picking work, so getting better at one skill quietly makes another worse. A learned driving planner has the same disease in a different costume — it faces wildly different scenarios and sensor modalities, and a single dense model collapses onto the easy, common cases while the rare emergencies get averaged into mush. The fix in all four of these papers is the same move: give each skill, scenario, or data domain its own expert, and route to the right one per situation, so rare scenarios and distinct skills get their own capacity and stop stepping on each other. The honest framing is gradient conflict and negative transfer — the very same enemy that Mixture-of-Experts fights in legged locomotion. To be straight about it: MoE for robot policies is still a small but real trend, with about one flagship each in driving and in multi-skill VLA. The win here is not a cheaper score on a leaderboard — it is a policy that can switch.
ROBOTICS'25MoRE: Unlocking Scalability in Reinforcement Learning for Quadruped Vision-Language-Action Models
Problem
People want to fine-tune one big vision-language-action model so a quadruped robot can follow instructions and do many skills. But the training data is a messy pile of mixed quality — some great, some sloppy — and stuffing it all into one dense model causes task interference, where getting better at one skill drags down another, and a lot of the data is wasted.
Approach
MoRE plants several small LoRA adapters inside a dense multimodal language model and treats them as a sparse-activated mixture of experts — for any given situation only a few adapters switch on, so different skills lean on different experts instead of all sharing the same crowded weights. The whole thing is then trained not as a plain imitator but as a reinforcement-learning Q-function, meaning it learns to score how good an action is rather than just copy the demonstration. That Q-function training is what lets it swallow the mixed-quality interaction data: good and bad examples both teach it, because it is learning which actions actually pay off. The sparse routing keeps the skills from fighting; the RL objective keeps the low-quality data from being dead weight. The result covers six quadruped skills and still generalizes to situations it never saw in training.
Why it matters
The sparse experts are exactly the tool against task interference: separate skills get separate capacity instead of one dense net where learning to do one job overwrites another. It turns a brittle single-skill policy into one that can switch across many skills and hold up on out-of-distribution scenes.

ROBOTICS'25MuST: Multi-Head Skill Transformer for Long-Horizon Dexterous Manipulation with Skill Progress
Problem
Long-horizon dexterous tasks like pick-and-pack are really several skills strung together — grasp, move, insert, release — and one policy has to both learn each skill and know when to hand off to the next. A single dense network struggles to hold all these motion primitives at once and to sequence them cleanly.
Approach
MuST is a transformer with several parallel heads, one per motion primitive, so each skill is learned in its own head rather than being blended into shared weights where they would interfere. The clever part is the switching signal: each skill head also learns a progress value that reports how close it is to finishing its piece of the task. When a skill's progress says it is done, that value triggers a smooth transition to the next skill, so the model chains primitives in order instead of guessing hard boundaries. This gives an explicit, learned answer to the hard question of when to switch, which is usually left implicit and fragile. One transformer can then execute multiple skills in the right sequence for a long-horizon task.
Why it matters
Parallel per-skill heads keep the primitives from bleeding into each other — the multi-skill version of avoiding negative transfer — while the progress value is the routing signal that lets the policy actually switch between skills at the right moment instead of getting stuck in one.

ROBOTICS'25Generalizing Motion Planners with Mixture of Experts for Autonomous Driving
Problem

ROBOTICS'25Expert-Enhanced Masked Point Modeling for Point Cloud Self-Supervised Learning
Problem
Self-supervised pre-training on 3D point clouds — masking parts and predicting them — is supposed to transfer to downstream tasks, but it suffers negative transfer when the pre-training data has a different semantic mix than the downstream data. Learning across mismatched domains in one shared model means one domain's features actively hurt another's.
Approach
After each backbone block the method inserts a Sparse Mixture of Experts layer with token routers that assign each point token to a domain-specific expert during pre-training. Because tokens from different domains flow to different experts, the model stops forcing conflicting domains through the same weights — the core move against negative transfer. At fine-tuning time the backbone is frozen and only these lightweight expert layers are updated, so adapting to a new downstream task touches very few parameters. This makes specialization cheap: the shared backbone stays stable while the routed experts absorb the domain differences. It beats the Point-MAE baseline by up to 5.86% on ScanObjectNN while training only about 12% as many parameters.
Why it matters
The token routers and domain experts are a direct strike at negative transfer — mismatched domains get their own capacity instead of fighting inside one shared network. It is the same principle as the policy papers, applied to representation learning: route to the right expert so domains stop degrading each other.

Reading it whole. Top to bottom, the themes walk the same router outward: first keep the senses separate in a multimodal model, then fix the fragile router itself, then stop the collapse and pay less, then hand each of many jobs its own expert — and finally give a robot a body whose skills and terrains each get their own slice so they stop fighting.

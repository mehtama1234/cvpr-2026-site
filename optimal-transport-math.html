Optimal transport — why it works, mathematically

You have two piles of sand in different shapes, and you want to morph one into the other as cheaply as possible. The answer depends on finding the right assignment of where each grain goes — but checking all possible assignments is hopeless. This is the trick that finds the best one anyway, and uses the answer to measure how far apart two distributions truly sit.

The key turn — the principle at work

The stuck point. Two probability shapes may contain the same total mass but place it in different locations. Matching totals alone ignores the work needed to move one shape into the other.

The mathematical move. The principle is minimum-cost mass movement. Optimal transport searches for a plan that moves all source mass to target mass while minimizing amount moved times distance moved.

What changes. Distribution comparison stops being blind to geometry. The distance between shapes reflects how hard one must be moved to become the other.

The problem, as a quantity

Imagine you want to move a pile of sand from one place to another, and the new place needs a different shape. Every grain has to go somewhere, and moving a grain costs something — maybe the distance it travels, or the energy it takes. The total cost depends entirely on where each grain ends up; move them smartly and the cost drops. Move them badly and it climbs.

Now add a hard part: the cost is not the same for every grain. Some grains are cheap to move far, others cost a lot to move even a little. The problem becomes finding the one assignment of grains to destinations that gives the smallest total bill. With a million grains, there are roughly a million-factorial possible assignments — a number so astronomically large that checking them all one by one is not just slow, it is impossible.

But here is the real question: suppose you solve this problem for two specific piles, and you find the cheapest cost to morph one into the other. Does that cost mean anything? Can you use it to ask 'how different are these two distributions?' — and get an answer that works like a real distance, one that always obeys the triangle inequality and never lies about which piles are close?

The core ideas — and why they work

The cheapest way to move one pile into the shape of another

min_π Σ π(x,y)·cost(x,y), π moves pile p into pile q
Imagine laying out all possible routes a single grain could take, tagged with the cost of that route, and then asking: which pairing of grains to routes makes the total cost smallest? That is the assignment problem, and it is exactly what the equation solves. The variable π represents an assignment — it records, for each grain at location x, how much of it goes to each destination y. The cost function gives a price per grain for that route. Multiply cost times the amount moved on that route, add them all up, and you get the total bill. The equation finds the assignment π that makes that sum as small as possible.

Why this specific setup and not another? Because the assignment must obey a hard constraint: the total amount leaving each source has to equal the amount arriving at the source pile, and every grain has to land somewhere. Those constraints make the problem have a unique, sensible answer — there is always one best pairing, and it is the same no matter how you solve it. The pile morphs into the other pile completely, with nothing lost or gained.

This cheapest cost is a true distance between whole distributions

W(p,q) = minimum total cost to morph p into q
Once you have found the best assignment for two piles, the cost it produces has a magic property: it acts like a true distance between the two distributions. Call it the Wasserstein distance. Just like the distance between two cities, it is zero only when the two piles are identical, grows smoothly as they move farther apart, and obeys the triangle inequality — if pile A is close to pile B and pile B is close to pile C, then pile A must be reasonably close to pile C.

Why is this true? Because the assignment problem has a deep dual structure: finding the cheapest way to move one pile into another is exactly equivalent to finding the highest-paying way to run a game where you assign prices to every location, then pay based on those prices for moving each grain. The one equals the negative of the other. This duality — which appears in mathematics over and over in disguise — guarantees that the cost of the best assignment really does satisfy all the rules a distance must follow. It is not just a useful number; it is a geometry.

It stays meaningful even when the two piles don't overlap

W(p,q) grows smoothly with how far apart the piles sit
The hardest test comes when the two piles do not overlap at all — one sits on one side of a wall and the other sits on the far side. The Wasserstein distance still works. As the piles drift farther from each other, the cheapest assignment routes more grain over longer distances, and the cost climbs smoothly. The moment two piles touch, the cost does not suddenly jump or collapse; it shifts continuously.

This continuity is crucial. Many other measures of difference between distributions — for instance, ones based on sharp yes-or-no rules — go haywire when the distributions barely overlap, jumping erratically or hitting zero when they should not. The Wasserstein distance never does. It sees smooth motion where other measures see noise, which is exactly why it works so well in real applications: when you are iteratively improving a machine-learning model and the piles of predictions shift a little, the Wasserstein distance changes a little too, letting you steer toward better predictions.

A smoothed version makes it fast enough to actually compute

add a spreading term → fast repeated re-balancing (Sinkhorn)
Computing the true best assignment is hard — too hard for real work. The Sinkhorn algorithm solves a smoothed version by adding an extra term that penalizes assignments being too concentrated in one place, spreading the grain out instead of packing it. Then instead of solving once, you iterate: start with a guess at the assignment, adjust it to lower the bill a little, then adjust again. After enough rounds, the iterates converge to a good answer.

Why does adding a spreading term help? Because it turns a sharp all-or-nothing problem — 'send all the grain this way or send it that way' — into a smooth one where every grain has a little incentive to split across multiple routes. That smoothness means the re-balancing steps feed you direction to improve in, and the iteration can run efficiently on any computer. You trade a small amount of exactness for a massive gain in speed: instead of giving up entirely because the true solution is unreachable, Sinkhorn gives you a good-enough answer in the time it takes to iterate a few dozen times.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Every solution to optimal transport is the same machine with a few dials.

Dial one — how you measure cost. The plain version uses physical distance: moving a grain one mile costs one mile. Other versions might measure cost as a learned distance, or as a mismatch in some feature space. The assignment problem stays identical; only the price tag changes.

Dial two — whether you want the true answer or a fast approximation. Linear programming solvers give the exact best assignment but only for small problems. Sinkhorn and related methods give a smooth, slightly-approximate answer but scale to millions of grains. A third family uses neural networks to learn a good assignment without computing any single optimal path explicitly.

Dial three — whether the piles are at fixed locations or whether you are learning how to morph them. The plain version treats the source and destination piles as given and finds the cheapest assignment between them. Generative models reverse it: they start with random noise and learn a path that lands on a target spread, using the assignment problem as the measure of how well they are doing.

The thread never changes: define what it costs to move each grain, find or approximate the assignment that makes the total cost smallest, and treat that cost as a true distance between distributions.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

One notch deeper: each of the theme's 4 approach-families and the specific mathematical mechanism that makes its papers work — the exact knob turned on the core relations above. Under each, the specific mathematical note for every paper in that family.

1Monge maps vs probabilistic couplings
T (deterministic) vs π(x,y) (probabilistic transport plan)
When you move one pile into the shape of another, you can either create a one-to-one map where each piece has exactly one destination, or you can allow splitting and mixing where pieces can blend together. The transport T is strict and direct; the coupling π allows flow to split. Monge maps are harder to find but give cleaner answers when they exist. Couplings always exist, even when the piles have nothing in common, which is why most algorithms use them.
2Entropy regularization
λH(π) added to the cost: min ⟨c, π⟩ + λH(π)
The raw optimal transport problem has sharp corners and singular solutions that make it hard to solve. Adding a spreading penalty—proportional to λ, the strength of smoothing—rounds off those corners and lets you solve the problem by iterating instead of solving a giant system at once. Higher λ makes the problem smoother and faster to compute, but the answer moves farther from the true optimal cost. This is the smoothing trick that makes OT actually runnable.
3Cost functions and distance metrics
Cost function c(x,y): e.g., ||x−y||^p for different p values
The cheapest move depends on what you measure as expensive. Using the squared distance (p=2) preserves geometric structure and penalizes large moves heavily. Using absolute distance (p=1) is more robust to outliers and doesn't care about direction. Custom costs encode domain knowledge: in images, you might use perceptual distance instead of raw pixel difference. The cost function fundamentally changes what the algorithm considers optimal.
4Computational algorithms and trade-offs
Algorithm choice: linear programming (exact), Sinkhorn (fast iterative), or neural network learning
Finding the true cheapest transport can be solved exactly by setting up a large linear program, but that takes time cubic in the number of pieces. The Sinkhorn algorithm iterates instead and is much faster, but only works with that spreading term. Neural networks can learn an approximation to the transport map once, then reuse it for millions of problems. Each approach trades off between exactness, speed, and how many times you need to solve the problem.

What breaks without this

What goes wrong. Without transport, matching two probability shapes can ignore how far the mass has to move. Two changes with the same before-and-after totals can have very different costs.

Why it goes wrong. A probability shape is not just a list of amounts. The positions matter. Moving a little mass nearby is not the same as moving it across the whole space.

What this idea gives you. Optimal transport asks for the cheapest movement plan. It keeps both the amount moved and the distance moved in view.

The through-line. The core problem is assigning each grain in one pile to a destination in another so the total movement cost is smallest — a problem with factorial-sized complexity that nevertheless has a sensible, unique answer that behaves like a real distance. The key insight is that the assignment problem has a hidden dual structure: finding the cheapest paths is mathematically the same as finding the highest-paying pricing scheme, and that duality guarantees the cost satisfies all the rules a distance must obey.

Two plain ideas carry the whole thing: the assignment problem always has a unique best solution because the constraints force everything to balance perfectly, and that solution is robust enough to be a true distance even when piles do not overlap. The Sinkhorn algorithm then trades exactness for speed by softening the problem — spreading grain across routes instead of forcing all-or-nothing picks — which turns the hard combinatorial problem into one that can be solved by gentle iteration.

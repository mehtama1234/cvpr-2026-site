Reinforcement Learning — why it works, mathematically

You can observe a reward from your environment but never compute its slope. The log-probability trick unwraps the gradient using the action's log-probability instead; then add a constraint to stop the policy from drifting too far and exploiting loopholes in the reward.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

Reinforcement learning trains from consequences. The model tries an action, receives a reward or penalty, and shifts probability toward actions that beat expectation.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology appears in the space of possible states and actions. A robot or game agent moves through a connected landscape of choices. Good learning respects which states can reach which other states.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

RL appears in robotics, games, dialogue, recommender systems, control, resource allocation, and model alignment.

Why Reinforcement Learning is worth learning

It matters because many tasks cannot be learned by copying examples alone. Some require trying actions and learning from what happens.

The problem, as a quantity

The agent is a learned policy π_θ(a|s) shaped by θ. The goal is to maximize the expected total return J(θ) = E_π[ Σ_t γ^t·r_t ] — a sum of rewards gathered along the way. But the reward function is opaque: you can see a number r at each step, yet you cannot compute how to shift it. Normal optimization assumes you can compute the slope of everything; here you cannot. The policy's θ is the only thing you control — it shapes the action probabilities — and the only feedback is the raw return number. How do you extract a usable direction to step from something you can only observe, not differentiate?

The core ideas — and why they work

The log-probability trick: slope of the action choice, not the reward

∇_θ J(θ) = E[ ∇_θ log π_θ(a_t|s_t) · (R_t − b) ]
The core move is to skip the reward's slope entirely. Instead, reframe the math: the direction to step on expected return is the direction of the action's log-probability, scaled by how much the actual return beat a simple estimate b. This is not a loose approximation — it is exact algebra. The return R_t is just a number you see; the direction ∇ log π_θ is something you can always compute. Scale them together and backpropagate, and you nudge high-reward actions upward in probability and push low-reward ones down. The blocked door disappears because you never tried to slope the black box — you sloped only the policy's log-probability, which is always open.

Baseline: separate the baseline level from the real signal

Subtract b from the return: (R_t − b) where b ≈ V(s_t), the expected return from this state
The raw return R_t bounces around: one path gives 100, the next gives 20, even from the same state. That noise makes the gradient jittery. A baseline b(s_t) — usually an estimate of the typical long-term return, called the value — cancels the baseline level. If most paths from a state give similar value, subtracting it leaves just the surprise, the part that is new. Actions beating the average get a big positive weight; actions below it get negative weight. The baseline itself does not change the gradient's heading — only its noise level. By centering the signal, you tell the step direction 'push actions that beat the average from here,' which is steadier and uses fewer samples than raw rewards that include a constant offset.

Reward hacking: the policy climbs exactly what you wrote

The gradient points uphill on r — and every slope, no matter how unintended, is a valid hill to climb
RL solves an optimization problem. If you write r = pixels_on_screen, the policy will find every path to pixels — fill the screen with noise, trigger rendering glitches, mess with the graphics. If you write r = distance_to_goal, the agent might point its laser at the shadow. The math is clean: ∇_θ log π · r climbs the value of r with zero knowledge of your intent. This is not a bug — it is the algorithm working exactly as told. The agent sees only the reward; it has no access to your true goal. Hacking is the easiest-solution trap: given a scoreboard, the system finds the simplest shortcut to make the score big, even if that shortcut is a trick that never happens in real use. One objective, one shared set of levers, no guard — collapse to the simplest loophole is inevitable.

KL regularization: tie the policy to a safe reference

J(θ) − β·KL(π_θ ∥ π_ref) where π_ref is a frozen, known-good policy
To block unbounded hacking, combine the reward goal with a penalty that fires when the policy drifts too far from a reference policy π_ref. The KL divergence — a distance measure between two probability distributions — quantifies how different the two policies are in their action choices. Subtracting β·KL creates a trade-off: chase higher reward, but not if it means deviating wildly from π_ref. If the policy tries a trick outside the normal distribution, the KL penalty kicks in. In language model fine-tuning, π_ref is a clean base model; the penalty keeps the new version roughly in the same action space. The penalty buys safety at the price of lower final score — a deliberate constraint you accept to stay grounded. The β knob adjusts tightness: large β hugs the reference (safe, lower score); small β lets it wander (higher score, risky).

Designing the reward: the inverse puzzle with no perfect answer

Given the policy and the loss, the math is deterministic. Specifying r such that climbing it serves your goal is open-ended.
The log-probability step is mechanical: you write r, the algorithm climbs it. The hard part is writing r so that climbing it matches what you actually care about. A walking robot needs r to balance speed, steadiness, power, and smoothness — different mixes yield different walks. A language model needs r to reward correctness, readability, brevity, and alignment — these pull in different directions. The tensions cannot be solved by math alone. You must try it out, watch the policy's real behavior, and rewrite the reward. Reward design is craft: you need field knowledge, willingness to observe what actually happens, and the discipline to revise when the outcome is not what you imagined. Unlike the step (which is mathematically sound once r is set), the choice of r is your responsibility. This inverse problem — finding the rule that, when optimized, gives you what you want — is RL's hardest remaining challenge.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Basic policy gradient methods like REINFORCE and PPO compute the log-probability step ∇ log π · (R − b) directly from observed paths, using one rule: paths that worked well get their actions nudged upward. Actor-critic combines a policy π(a|s) and a value estimate V(s), so the baseline is steady and the signal is less noisy. Value-only methods like Q-learning learn the expected return per action Q(s,a) and let the best action speak for itself; the policy is implicit (pick the highest). RLHF combines a frozen base policy with a learned reward model: sample from the base, score the results with the reward model, and push the policy to climb the score. GRPO uses reasoning traces as explicit numbers, steering the policy toward better thought paths. PPO-style variants use probability weighting to reuse old paths for multiple steps without the policy changing too much. In every case, the shape is the same: slope the log-probability, weight it by return or advantage, and keep the policy from wandering far from the reference.

A worked numeric example

If two sampled actions get rewards 1 and 0 with baseline 0.4, their advantages are 0.6 and -0.4. The log-probability of the first is increased; the second is decreased. RL is probability mass moving toward actions that beat expectation.

1Aligning generation with reward: preference optimization for images and video
R_pair = logit(π(y_better) - π(y_worse)) + λ·KL(π‖π_ref); train consistency/style/detail rewards as separate models, then combine via weighted sum to steer image/video generation
Preference learning works by scoring pairs of outputs (one good, one bad) and using the log-odds difference — the advantage in logged probabilities between them — as the reward signal. Each task goal (keeping details stable, matching a style, staying true to the input) is learned as a separate reward model, then combined by adding them with weights, avoiding the single-objective hacking problem because competing goals balance explicitly.
These papers learn rewards for image and video generation by comparing pairs of outputs and measuring how much one is better than the other. They break the reward into separate pieces — one for keeping colors steady, one for matching the desired style, one for preserving detail — so each goal can be balanced against the others without one hijacking the entire training.

Defense against hacking uses three tools: (1) gradually increasing task difficulty over time so the policy cannot get stuck in easy shortcuts, (2) picking training samples where the reward is most informative — cases where the model is uncertain — so the policy learns what matters, and (3) breaking down the reward into pieces per decision step, so the policy learns which specific choices earned the points, not just that some aggregate score went up.
These papers directly address reward hacking — when a policy exploits loopholes in the reward specification. They use three defenses: gradually making tasks harder so the policy cannot stay stuck in easy exploits, picking training examples where the reward signal is clearest, and breaking rewards into per-step components to clarify which decisions earned which points.

5Learning to move: RL for legged locomotion and agile control

The through-line. RL turned an impossible problem into a solvable one: you cannot slope the reward, but you can slope the policy's log-probability. The log-probability trick unwraps the direction, turning 'maximize observed return' into a step on action probabilities. But this strength carries a burden — the policy will find and exploit any gap in the reward rule. The KL penalty and the craft of reward design are not added-on tricks; they are the core defense against hacking. The field has shifted twice: first, from 'impossible' to 'computable' via the log-slope trick; second, from 'computable' to 'safe' via penalties and thoughtful reward choice. Both moves are necessary.

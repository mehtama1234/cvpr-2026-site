Graph neural networks — when the answer is in the connections

Some data is not a grid of pixels or a string of words — it is a web of relationships: molecules are atoms bonded together, a scene is objects with relations, a road network is intersections joined by streets. For these, what a thing is depends on what it is connected to. A graph neural network is built for exactly that: each node figures itself out by listening to its neighbours, over and over. This page is the plain story of that idea — the message passing at its heart, and the honest limit on how far you can push it — on a real run.

1 · The problem — a thing defined by its neighbours

Hand a normal network a node and it sees only that node's own features. But in relational data the meaning lives in the structure: a person's community is revealed by their friends, an atom's role by its bonds, a paper's field by its citations. Flatten the graph into independent rows and you throw away the very thing that carries the signal — the edges. Worse, a graph has no natural order, so a model must give the same answer no matter how you happen to number the nodes.

The problem, stated once: for data that is a graph of related entities, a node's meaning comes from its connections — how do you learn on the structure itself, and stay indifferent to how the nodes are labelled?

2 · The reframe — let every node hear its neighbours

The idea: each node updates itself by mixing in messages from its neighbours, and repeats. One round of message passing: every node gathers its neighbours' feature vectors, averages them in, and transforms the result. After one round a node knows about its immediate neighbours; after two, about neighbours-of-neighbours; and so on, information spreading outward along the edges. Because the update only ever sums over neighbours, relabelling the nodes changes nothing — it is permutation-invariant by construction, the graph-shaped cousin of a convolution.

h_v ← update( h_v , aggregate{ h_u : u is a neighbour of v } ) # repeat L times     # each node mixes in its neighbours; after L rounds it has heard from L hops away

It is a simple, powerful move — and it comes with one built-in danger the run makes vivid.

3 · Seen on a real run — connections carry the signal

1 · Structure beats features

We built a graph of 160 nodes in 4 communities (dense links inside a community, sparse across). Here is the catch: each node's own feature vector is pure noise — there is nothing in a node alone that says which community it belongs to. The only signal is who it connects to. We asked two models to classify the nodes:

plain MLP (node alone) → 19%
(chance is 25%)
GNN (message passing) → 96%

The MLP is stuck at chance (19%) — with noise for features and no view of the edges, it literally cannot tell the communities apart. The GNN, by letting each node mix in its neighbours, reads the community straight off the graph and reaches 96%. The information was never in the nodes; it was in the connections.

2 · The honest catch — over-smoothing

More message passing should mean more context — up to a point. Accuracy climbs to 94% at 2 layers, then collapses to 18% by 16. Why? Because every round averages each node with its neighbours, and after too many rounds every node has averaged with everyone — the features all converge to the same blur. The dashed line is how similar the node features have become: it shoots up to 0.998 (nearly identical). This is over-smoothing, and it is the central limit on how deep a plain GNN can go.

4 · Why it matters — the model for relational worlds

Whenever the data is genuinely a graph, a GNN is the natural fit: predicting a molecule's properties from its bonds, reasoning over a scene graph of objects and relations, learning on meshes and CAD, forecasting traffic on a road network, recommending across a social graph. The message-passing template bends to all of them — the only thing that changes is what the nodes and edges mean. Its standing limit is the over-smoothing you saw, which is why much GNN research is about going deeper without collapsing.

5 · The surprise — the robot is a graph

A robot's body is a graph — joints connected by links — so a GNN whose structure matches the robot's morphology is a natural controller, and it transfers across robots that share the pattern. Zoom out and a fleet of robots is a graph too (agents joined by who-can-see-whom), and the scene a robot works in becomes a graph of objects and relations it builds, updates, and plans over.

Same message passing, but the graph is physical. Morphology-aware GNN controllers, multi-agent coordination over interaction graphs, and 3D scene graphs a robot maintains during a task are a real thread in robotics — because the robot and its world are literally nodes and edges. Vision reasons over a graph it extracts; a robot acts over a graph it inhabits. The in-the-wild page lays out both sides.

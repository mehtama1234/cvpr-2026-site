1 · Straighten the path

Standard diffusion samples slowly, creeping along a curved path from pure noise to a finished image, because every step has to be tiny to stay on that winding route. Flow matching reframes the whole thing: instead of a noise schedule, it trains a velocity field that tells any noise point which way to move and how fast in order to become a real data point. The trick that unlocks speed is making that path as straight as possible, because a straight line can be crossed in a few big jumps instead of hundreds of little ones. The papers in this theme each attack that goal from a different angle: a better training objective that rewards straight trajectories, a smarter loss that keeps fine detail alive when you jump fast, a staged architecture that grows the model as the image sharpens, and a fix for the mismatch between how a model is trained and how it actually generates. The common thread is high-quality images in a handful of forward passes rather than a long, patient crawl. Flow matching has quietly become the default generative backbone precisely because these straightening ideas keep paying off.
CVPR'26Learning Straight Flows: Variational Flow Matching for Efficient Generation
Problem
Even flow matching models can be slow, because the paths they learn from noise to image often bend and curve rather than run straight. A curved path forces you to take many small steps to follow it faithfully, which drives up the number of times the network has to run.
Approach
This work builds a variational flow matching framework whose whole purpose is to reward straight-line trajectories. Rather than letting the model wander along whatever route it finds, it adds geometric constraints that pull each path toward a direct line between a noise point and its target image. The training objective explicitly trades off keeping quality high against shortening the trip, so the learned velocity field points as directly as possible. Because the resulting routes are nearly straight, they can be traversed in just a few big jumps instead of a long sequence of tiny corrections.
Why it matters
Straighter paths mean single-digit sampling steps while holding image quality, so generation gets dramatically faster without switching to a heavier model.
CVPR'26Frequency-Aware Flow Matching for High-Quality Image Generation
Problem
Flow matching is fast, but when it takes big jumps it tends to smear the fine stuff, textures, edges, and crisp detail, especially at higher resolutions. The usual training treats every part of the image the same, so the delicate high-frequency detail gets shortchanged and artifacts creep in.
Approach
The method, called Frequency-Aware Flow Matching, brings frequency analysis into the training loop. It uses a Fourier view to split the image into low-frequency structure and high-frequency detail, then weights the flow matching loss so the model spends extra effort learning to transport the high-frequency parts correctly. The objective is hybrid, working in both the spatial and the frequency domain at once, so the velocity field learns to carry coarse layout and fine texture appropriately. In effect the model is told where the fragile detail lives and to protect it as the path is crossed.
Why it matters
By defending high-frequency detail during training, it keeps textures sharp even at aggressive step counts, so you get high-fidelity images without giving back the speed advantage.
CVPR'26NAMI: Efficient Image Generation via Bridged Progressive Rectified Flow Transformers
Problem
Big flow-based Transformers like FLUX make gorgeous images but are slow and expensive because every step runs the full heavyweight network. They also ignore a natural fact: early in generation the model is just roughing out layout, while only later does it need full power for fine detail.
Approach
NAMI splits the rectified flow into several resolution stages, for example 256 then 512 then 1024, each getting its own time window along the path. Early low-resolution stages use only a few Transformer blocks to sketch the coarse layout, and more blocks are progressively added as the resolution and the detail demand climb. A small learnable BridgeFlow module, essentially a linear transform, stitches adjacent stages together by aligning their distributions at the hand-off points so the path stays coherent as it jumps up in resolution. Training feeds multiple resolutions at once with dynamic loss weighting, and inference walks the staged flow with upsampling and the bridge at each stage boundary.
Why it matters
Spending cheap capacity early and full capacity only late cuts 1024 generation time by about 64 percent versus a same-size FLUX baseline while staying competitive on quality.
CVPR'26MixFlow Training: Alleviating Exposure Bias with Slowed Interpolation Mixture
Problem
During training the model only ever sees clean, ground-truth noisy images, but at generation time it feeds on its own imperfect outputs. That mismatch, called exposure bias, lets small errors pile up step after step and drag the sample off course.
Approach
MixFlow starts from an observation the authors name the Slow Flow phenomenon: the noisy data a model actually produces during generation sits closer to the higher-noise ground-truth than the standard schedule assumes. To close the gap, each training timestep is fed a mixture of slowed interpolations, pushing the training samples toward those higher-noise points that better match what inference really encounters. The timestep itself is drawn from a Beta(2,1) distribution so more attention lands where drift accumulates. The whole change is only a few lines of code on top of an existing trainer.
Why it matters
By training on samples that look like what the model will actually see, it curbs error accumulation and reaches state-of-the-art ImageNet FID, so the few-step path stays on track instead of drifting.

2 · One shot instead of fifty

This is the largest sub-theme, and it starts from a frustrating fact: a many-step teacher makes beautiful images but it is slow, because it has to nudge the noise toward a picture dozens of times before it is done. The papers here collapse that long walk into just one to four steps, and they do it two ways. The first way is to distill a slow teacher into a fast student, where the student watches the teacher and learns to leap across many of the teacher's little steps at once. The second way is to train a consistency-style or MeanFlow model that maps any point along the path straight back to the clean output in a single shot, so no teacher is needed at run time. The recurring trap is the teacher-student mismatch: the student practices on made-up in-between states the teacher never actually visits, so at test time it stumbles, and several papers fix this by distilling on the teacher's real trajectories or without any dataset at all. The payoff is the same across all of them: the quality of a full diffusion model delivered in one or a few forward passes.
CVPR'26Flash-DMD: Towards High-Fidelity Few-Step Image Generation with Efficient Distillation and Joint Reinforcement Learning
Problem
Turning a slow diffusion model into a few-step generator normally costs a huge amount of training, and the shortcut often blurs the image. The two usual tools each misbehave: distribution-matching is unstable and slow to settle, while reinforcement learning tacked on afterward starts gaming its own reward and collapses.
Approach
Flash-DMD splits training into two phases and, crucially, decouples the objectives by timestep. Early on, at high-noise timesteps it uses pure distribution-matching to get the overall structure right, and at low-noise timesteps it switches to a pixel-level adversarial loss to sharpen fine detail. In the later phase it folds reinforcement learning directly into the distillation loop rather than running it as a separate stage, which is what stops the reward-hacking and policy collapse. The RL is built specifically for a few-step model, so it can polish details without the student learning to cheat the reward. The result converges at only 2.1 percent of the training cost of DMD2.
Why it matters
It shows you can hand a few-step student the structure-then-detail knowledge of a full teacher and keep the quality, while paying a tiny fraction of the usual training bill.
CVPR'26Flow Map Distillation Without Data
Problem
The usual way to compress a flow model into a one-step generator draws practice examples from an external dataset. But if the teacher has learned to generate beyond that original data, or was fine-tuned afterward, the student practices on the wrong distribution, a gap the authors call Teacher-Data Mismatch.
Approach
FreeFlow throws the dataset away entirely and samples only from the prior, the pure-noise starting point the teacher is guaranteed by construction to begin from. From that single trustworthy anchor, the student learns to predict the teacher's whole sampling path in one leap. Because a one-jump student can drift, it actively corrects its own compounding errors along the way using velocity-based corrections. Nothing about the process depends on an outside data distribution, so the mismatch risk is removed rather than merely reduced. This is the first fully data-free flow-map distillation that actually beats the data-based versions.
Why it matters
It collapses the teacher's many steps into a single forward pass, and by anchoring only on noise it hits a 1.45 FID on ImageNet in one step, better than methods that used real data.
CVPR'26Transition Models: Rethinking the Generative Learning Objective
Problem
Generative models are stuck in a trade-off: iterative diffusion is gorgeous but expensive, while few-step models hit a hard quality ceiling. The authors trace this to the training objective itself, which teaches a model either the infinitesimal moment-to-moment dynamics or a direct jump to the endpoint, but never both.
Approach
Transition Models (TiM) reframe the target as a state-to-state transition for any finite time interval, not just an instant and not just the final endpoint. The model learns the exact continuous-time equation describing how to move from any point on the path to any later point across an arbitrary gap. This means the very same model can jump the whole way in one step or take many careful steps, unifying fast generation and accurate refinement under one objective. It learns the solution manifold of the generative process directly instead of approximating a differential equation. Trained from scratch at only 865M parameters, it beats models an order of magnitude larger.
Why it matters
One trained model supports any step count from one to many, delivering few-step speed and, uniquely, quality that keeps climbing monotonically as you spend more steps.
CVPR'26FlowSteer: Guiding Few-Step Image Synthesis with Authentic Trajectories
Problem
Rectified-flow distillation methods like PeRFlow lag behind consistency and score distillation, and the reason is a mismatch: the student trains on straight-line interpolated starting points that the teacher never actually produces. That fake in-between input at training time, versus the real path at inference time, quietly wrecks few-step quality.
Approach
FlowSteer introduces Online Trajectory Alignment, which trains the student on the teacher's authentic continuous trajectories instead of the linearly interpolated fakes, keeping the teacher on-path during training. On top of that it applies adversarial distillation directly to the ODE trajectories, forcing the student's path to imitate the teacher's real one step by step. It is the first method to run adversarial distillation on flow-model trajectories in this way. Along the way the authors also uncover and fix a hidden flaw in the standard FlowMatchEulerDiscreteScheduler that had been degrading few-step output. The fixes apply to both distilled and plain pretrained models.
Why it matters
By distilling on the teacher's true path rather than a shortcut approximation, it closes the teacher-student gap and gets clean few-step images out of a rectified-flow model.

3 · Fast where it matters most

A single image is one frame, but a video is hundreds of them and a 3D object is a whole surface of vertices, so these modalities cost far more to sample than a picture, and the few-step win of flow matching pays off most exactly here. The papers in this theme put a flow-matching or rectified-flow generator, usually a diffusion transformer, on a video, mesh, voxel, or point-cloud latent rather than on raw pixels. On top of that straight-path sampler they add distillation or pyramidal multi-resolution schedules to make long clips and high-resolution shapes tractable to generate. Some carve the video into a rolling window of frames and stream it out chunk by chunk in real time; others compress an entire mesh into a compact latent and generate all of its vertices at once. The 3D methods anchor their latents to a coarse voxel grid so a big model can spend its budget on where detail goes, then scale that budget up at test time. The through-line is generating in parallel or in a continuous stream rather than crawling one denoising step at a time.
CVPR'26StreamDiT: Real-Time Streaming Text-to-Video Generation
Problem
Text-to-video models normally produce a short clip all at once, offline, with high latency because the transformer cost grows quadratically with the number of frames. That makes them useless for anything interactive, where you want video to appear as you go rather than after a long wait.
Approach
StreamDiT reshapes flow matching around a moving buffer: a rolling window of frames, each at a different stage of denoising, that slides forward so finished frames stream out one end while fresh noisy frames enter the other. It mixes several partitioning schemes during training, so different frames in the buffer can follow uniform or diagonal noise schedules, which improves consistency and quality. The backbone is an adaLN diffusion transformer with time embeddings that vary across the buffer and window attention to keep cost down. A multistep distillation tailored to the buffer then collapses the number of network evaluations down to the number of buffer chunks. The result runs at 16 frames per second for 512p video on a single GPU, fast enough for live streaming and video-to-video editing.
Why it matters
Video is the most expensive modality to sample because every clip is many frames, so a straight, few-step sampler is what turns generation from a long offline wait into something that streams in real time.
CVPR'26MeshFlow: Efficient Artistic Mesh Generation via MeshVAE and Flow-based Diffusion Transformer
Problem
The usual way to generate an artist-grade mesh is autoregressively, placing one vertex after another, which costs quadratically more as the mesh grows and forces coordinates onto a discrete grid that introduces quantization error. Implicit alternatives that fit a smooth surface tend to spew out too many vertices or round off the sharp edges that artists need.
Approach
MeshFlow first trains a MeshVAE that squeezes a whole mesh into a compact, fully continuous latent space, encoding connectivity as continuous feature vectors learned with contrastive learning and using vertex normals to order edges. Because the latent is continuous, there is no coordinate discretization and no quantization error. A rectified flow transformer then generates all vertices and edges of the mesh in parallel from that latent, rather than one at a time. The compact latent is roughly 72 times smaller than a naive tokenizer, so the model has far fewer things to produce. Together this gives an 18 times speedup over the fastest autoregressive generators at higher accuracy.
Why it matters
A mesh has thousands of vertices, so generating them one by one is the bottleneck; a rectified-flow sampler over a compact latent lets the whole shape emerge in parallel along a nearly straight path instead of crawling vertex by vertex.
CVPR'26LATTICE: Democratize High-Fidelity 3D Generation at Scale
Problem
3D generation trails 2D image synthesis because a model has to invent both the overall spatial structure of an object and its detailed surface geometry from scratch. Existing 3D latents are unstructured and expensive, so it is hard to scale up either the model or the fidelity.
Approach
LATTICE introduces VoxSet, a semi-structured latent that compresses a 3D asset into latent vectors anchored to a coarse voxel grid, so each latent knows roughly where in space it lives while still holding what the surface looks like. Generation runs in two stages: an off-the-shelf model first lays down a sparse voxel geometry anchor, then a large rectified flow transformer fills in detailed geometry conditioned on that anchor. Trained progressively up to 4.5 billion parameters, it stays cheap to train, with a 2B model finishing in under a day on 64 GPUs. Because the latents are localizable, the model supports arbitrary-resolution decoding and strong test-time scaling: pushing tokens from about 6,000 up to 30,000 keeps improving quality without retraining. Decoupling where-to-place from what-to-place is what makes the rectified-flow sampler scale to high fidelity.
Why it matters
Detailed 3D surfaces are far more costly to sample than an image, so a straight-path rectified-flow generator over structured voxel-anchored latents is what lets fidelity scale with model size and test-time token budget instead of stalling.
CVPR'26MotionHiFlow: Text-to-Motion via Hierarchical Flow Matching
Problem
Generating human motion from text at a single temporal scale forces one representation to capture both the high-level structure of a movement and its fine frame-by-frame detail, and it struggles to do both. The result is motion that either drifts from the text or looks jerky in the details.
Approach
MotionHiFlow runs flow matching hierarchically, generating motion coarse-to-fine across temporal scales the way a person first blocks out key poses and then refines them. It starts by producing a sparse low-scale motion that nails the overall structure, then progressively upsamples to finer temporal detail. A cross-scale transition of denoising, upsampling, and renoising carries noise consistently from one scale to the next so the refinement stays coherent. Driving this is a Text-Motion Diffusion Transformer that models the skeleton through joint-aware positional encoding, paired with a topology-aware Motion VAE. Semantic alignment holds up even when the coarse motion keeps only 20 percent of its frames.
Why it matters
Long motion sequences are expensive to sample densely, so a coarse-to-fine flow-matching schedule pins down structure cheaply at low resolution before spending steps on fine detail, giving both alignment and quality without a full-resolution crawl.

4 · Make the transport smarter

A single flow-matching pass learns velocities whose marginal trajectories curve, so a naive one-step jump follows the tangent and overshoots the real target. Two lines of work fix this. The first redesigns the source coupling so that the paths connecting noise to data do not cross and stay nearly straight, using optimal transport or mode-aware pairing instead of random noise. The second trains the average velocity over a whole time interval rather than the instantaneous one, so a single big step lands correctly for genuine one-step generation; this is the MeanFlow idea. MeanFlow is a fast-rising family, but it carries its own training challenges — numerical instability near the boundary and a tricky target design — that several of these papers tackle head-on. The shared goal is to reach one step without giving up the fidelity of the slow many-step sampler.
CVPR'26COT-FM: Cluster-wise Optimal Transport Flow Matching
Problem
Standard flow matching pairs each data point with a random noise sample, so the learned trajectories bend and cross. Those curved paths make every discretization step wrong by a little, which piles up into blurry samples and forces many sampling steps.
Approach
COT-FM straightens the field by fixing the source coupling instead of touching the network. It clusters the target samples, then gives each cluster its own dedicated noise distribution obtained by reversing a pre-trained flow-matching model back to the noise side. Training then alternates between refining the target vector field and re-solving a per-cluster optimal coupling, so within each cluster noise and data are matched in a near-transport-optimal way. Because the paths inside a cluster no longer tangle, the learned velocities come out with much lower curvature. It is fully plug-and-play, working with existing FM architectures and with both supervised and unsupervised clustering.
Why it matters
Straighter, non-crossing paths mean a big step lands close to the true target, so far fewer steps are needed — it lifts Rectified Flow from 12.6 to 8.23 FID at 10 steps and reaches 96.1% on LIBERO with a single function evaluation.
CVPR'26MeanFlow Transformers with Representation Autoencoders
Problem
MeanFlow can generate an image in one step, but it is expensive: it operates in full-resolution latent space and needs enormously long training schedules, hundreds of GPU-days even for the single-step version. Compressing the latents with a representation autoencoder would cut compute, but naively bolting it onto MeanFlow makes training unstable and degrades quality.
Approach
MF-RAE stabilizes the pairing with a staged curriculum that eases the model into the one-step regime rather than forcing it there. Stage one pre-trains an ordinary flow-matching model in the compact autoencoder latent space, building a stable starting point without the mean-velocity constraint. Stage two warms the model up with a conditional-mean-trajectory objective that enforces trajectory-mean consistency, and stage three fine-tunes with the full mean-flow distillation loss for single-step generation. The backbone is a dual-head diffusion transformer that predicts noise and clean latent together, and the average velocity is approximated with a cheap finite-difference Jacobian-vector product instead of an exact Jacobian.
Why it matters
The compact latents plus the gentle FM to CMT to MFD ramp deliver a true one-step model that is drastically cheaper — 1-step FID 2.03 on ImageNet 256 versus 3.43 for vanilla MeanFlow, with 38% fewer GFLOPS and 83% less training cost.
CVPR'26Improved Mean Flows: On the Challenges of Fastforward Generative Models
Problem
MeanFlow reaches one-step generation by learning a mean velocity field, but its original recipe is fragile. The training target blows up numerically near time zero, and conditioning on both the current noisy sample and the expected clean sample sends the network contradictory gradient signals.
Approach
iMF reparameterizes the target so the one-step objective behaves. It swaps the unstable u-loss for a v-loss that expresses the mean velocity through the instantaneous velocity, which removes the exploding denominator near time zero, and it pins the boundary condition at time zero to the clean sample. A flexible guidance mechanism folds the guidance scale and interval in as conditioning variables, giving inference-time control without a separate unconditional training run. Finally it replaces adaLN-zero modulation with in-context token conditioning — class labels as 8 tokens, other conditions as 4 — which also trims the model by a third. Together these stabilize training while shrinking the network.
Why it matters
A cleaner target and boundary make the single big step land right, improving one-step quality by 50% — iMF-XL/2 reaches FID 1.72 at 1-NFE on ImageNet 256, down from MeanFlow's 3.43.
CVPR'26Stable Mean Flow: Lyapunov-Inspired One-Step Flow Matching
Problem
Flow matching for generation needs both stable convergence and efficient one-step inference. Existing one-step approaches can be numerically unstable, and unstable dynamics either drift away from the target or force the model back to multi-step sampling.
Approach
Stable Mean Flow borrows Lyapunov stability theory from dynamical systems to keep the one-step mean-flow numerically well-behaved. The idea is to learn flows whose dynamics carry a Lyapunov-style guarantee, so the trajectory provably settles toward the data manifold instead of overshooting or diverging. That stability property is what lets a single step be taken safely, turning the mean-flow field into a reliable one-shot map from noise to data.
Why it matters
Building in provable stability makes the one big step trustworthy, giving more stable and efficient one-step generation than mean-flow objectives that can wander.

5 · Same input, faithful output, fast

Restoration and editing are not asking for a fresh sample; they want this input mapped to a faithful output, and they want it quickly. Flow matching fits that job because its velocity field is deterministic — the model learns a fixed path from noise to image rather than a random walk, so that path is easy to steer, easy to invert, and easy to run in a single step. Two lines of work follow from this. One builds one-step restorers — super-resolution and HDR-style enhancement that keep fidelity to the low-resolution or degraded input instead of hallucinating new content. The other edits by manipulating the flow trajectory itself — velocity differencing, orthogonal decomposition, and inversion-free steering — rather than slowly regenerating the whole picture from scratch. The deterministic straight path is exactly what makes precise, fast, controllable editing possible.
CVPR'26FiDeSR: High-Fidelity and Detail-Preserving One-Step Diffusion Super-Resolution
Problem
Diffusion super-resolution normally needs many slow denoising steps, which is too expensive to deploy on real photos. Squeezing it down to a single step usually breaks in two ways at once: the coarse structure drifts and looks inconsistent (because the model conditions through a compressed latent), and the fine high-frequency detail gets washed out. So one-step methods tend to be either faithful but blurry, or sharp but distorted.
Approach
FiDeSR is a frequency-aware, one-step super-resolver that tries to win both fidelity and perceptual sharpness at the same time. During training it uses a detail-aware weighting strategy that leans harder on the regions where the model is making the biggest prediction errors, so difficult textures get more attention. At inference it adds separate low- and high-frequency adaptive enhancers that refine the output without any retraining, letting you dial the two frequency bands independently. On top of that a latent residual refinement step corrects the diffusion model's noise prediction to recover fine detail that the single pass would otherwise lose. All of this runs in one forward step rather than an iterative sampling loop.
Why it matters
Because a flow/diffusion model that runs in a single deterministic step is fast enough to actually deploy, the remaining problem is only fidelity — and FiDeSR shows you can restore that fidelity with frequency-aware correction bolted onto the one-step path rather than paying for slow multi-step generation.
CVPR'26Delta Rectified Flow Sampling for Text-to-Image Editing
Problem
Rectified-flow models like Stable Diffusion 3 are fast because they follow near straight-line paths, but bending them toward an edit is hard. Standard editing objectives over-smooth the result — the image loses structure and the background regions that should stay put drift away from the original. And the usual fix, inverting the image back to noise first, adds its own errors from approximating that inversion path.
Approach
Delta Rectified Flow Sampling (DRFS) is an inversion-free way to edit that works directly on the flow trajectory. The core trick is differencing: it subtracts the source image's flow residual from the target's, so whatever drift both trajectories share cancels out and only the actual edit signal survives — this is what keeps the untouched background from moving. To line up the two trajectories in time it adds a time-dependent shift term whose schedule rises then falls back toward zero, concentrating the edit signal in the middle timesteps where it matters most. Optimization runs coarse-to-fine, handling large timesteps before small ones. It needs no architectural change to SD3 or SD3.5, and it cleanly generalizes prior methods: turn the shift off and you recover DDS, set it to a specific form and you recover FlowEdit as a special case.
Why it matters
This is editing by manipulating the deterministic flow trajectory rather than regenerating the image — because the velocity field is fixed and shared between source and target, simply subtracting the two residuals isolates the edit and preserves everything else, no inversion required.
CVPR'26FlowDirector: Training-Free Flow Steering for Precise Text-to-Video Editing
Problem
Text-driven video editing usually starts by inverting the video back to noise, but inaccurate inversion corrupts appearance fidelity and, worse for video, breaks motion consistency across frames. That makes complex edits especially fragile. The field needs a way to edit without inversion that still keeps temporal coherence.
Approach
FlowDirector reframes editing as steering an ODE directly in data space — it never inverts the video, it just guides the deterministic flow toward the edited result. It layers on three flow-correction strategies that each handle a separate failure mode. Direction-Aware Flow Correction amplifies the component of the velocity that opposes the current content, pushing a genuine semantic change instead of a timid one. Motion-Appearance Decoupling orthogonalizes the motion control so you can change appearance without disturbing how things move. And Differential Averaging Guidance smooths the trajectory over time to keep frames stable. Together these steer the flow precisely while leaving the background and the original motion intact.
Why it matters
Because the flow is a deterministic ODE, you can edit a video by steering that path frame-to-frame — decomposing the velocity into motion and appearance directions — rather than paying the fidelity cost of inverting and re-generating.
CVPR'26BiFM: Bidirectional Flow Matching for Few-Step Image Editing and Generation
Problem
Real-time interactive editing needs few-step models, but editing a few-step model means inverting it — running it backwards from image to noise — and that inversion accumulates approximation errors fast. Existing few-step inversion methods paper over this with pretrained generators and extra auxiliary modules, which don't scale or transfer across architectures.
Approach
BiFM makes inversion a native ability of the model by learning the flow in both directions at once. Instead of only learning the noise-to-image velocity, it directly estimates the average velocity field going image-to-noise as well, tying the two together through a shared instantaneous velocity field. A bidirectional consistency objective plus a lightweight time-interval embedding keep the forward and backward passes coherent under continuous-time supervision. The result is a single flow-matching model that can invert in one step with no auxiliary network, and it drops into existing backbones like SiT and MMDiT — or into large pretrained text-to-image models via cheap fine-tuning.
Why it matters
Because the velocity field defines one deterministic path, integrating that same ODE the other way is inversion — so BiFM simply learns both directions together, getting one-step invertibility for free and turning few-step editing into a native capability rather than a bolted-on approximation.

6 · An action in time

A generative policy is a wonderful thing: instead of committing to one guess about what to do next, it can hold many good moves at once and pick a coherent one, which is exactly what you want when a task can be solved several ways. But the standard recipe for building one, a diffusion policy, pays for that richness by starting from pure noise and cleaning it up over many small passes, and each action can cost twenty to fifty of those passes. On a robot that is fatal, because while the policy is still denoising the world keeps moving, so by the time a good action finally emerges it is already answering a question about a scene that no longer exists. Flow matching attacks the root cause: instead of a long noisy walk, it learns a nearly straight path from noise directly to a good action, so one pass, or a small handful, lands you where diffusion needed dozens of steps to arrive. Separately, teams who already have a diffusion policy that works simply want it to run faster without paying to retrain it, so they lean on training-free tricks that reuse computation across the steps that barely change the answer, or warm-start each control tick from the last one, or fully clean up only the action they must emit right now and leave future actions half-finished to be refined later. And the same idea reaches past low-level actions all the way up to planning, where flow matching can lay down a whole smooth, feasible trajectory in a single pass rather than grinding it out. The punchline separates the two worlds: in vision, taking fewer steps is a nice saving on cost, but in robotics fewer steps is the difference between acting in time and being too late to act at all.
ROBOTICS'25FlowNav: Combining Flow Matching and Depth Priors for Efficient Navigation
Problem
Robots that navigate toward a goal image using a diffusion policy are slow, because generating each move means running many denoising passes. On top of that, their sense of the scene is thin, so they read the space around them poorly. Together this keeps them from reacting in real time on real hardware.
Approach
FlowNav swaps the diffusion generator for conditional flow matching, which learns a near-straight path from noise to a navigation action so a good move comes out in far fewer passes than diffusion needs. To fix the weak perception, it feeds in monocular depth priors lifted from an off-the-shelf foundation model, giving the policy a real sense of how far away things are without training a depth system from scratch. The action generator is conditioned on the goal image and this richer perceptual signal. The result is a policy that decides where to go quickly and sees the environment more accurately at the same time. It is validated on real robots across several different environments.
Why it matters
Navigation is a closed control loop where the robot must keep issuing moves as the world scrolls past, so cutting the many diffusion passes down to a few is what lets a generative navigator actually run on the robot instead of stalling.
ROBOTICS'25Fast Policy: Accelerating Visuomotor Policies without Re-training
Problem
Diffusion visuomotor policies are slow because every action is produced by iterating a denoising network many times over. The usual fix, distilling the policy into a faster one, means an expensive retraining step. Teams who already have a working policy want the speed without paying that cost.
Approach
Fast Policy is a training-free accelerator that speeds up an existing diffusion policy at inference, untouched. It observes that the network is doing heavy new work only on certain denoising steps and coasting on the rest, and it uses a Fourier energy analysis to tell the two apart. On those non-critical steps it simply reuses the UNet encoder features computed earlier instead of recomputing them, skipping most of the work. Because reusing stale features would normally erode quality, it adds a noise correction step to offset that drift. The outcome matches state-of-the-art task success while doing far less computation per action.
Why it matters
This is the deployment gate for a policy you cannot afford to retrain: by spending real compute only on the denoising steps that matter, it makes an already-trained diffusion policy fast enough to hold a control loop.
ROBOTICS'25Fast Policy Synthesis with Variable Noise Diffusion Models
Problem
Diffusion imitation policies need many denoising steps to synthesize each action, which makes them too slow for fast, reactive control. The robot cannot afford to wait for a full clean-up cycle every time it must move.
Approach
Streaming Diffusion Policy reorganizes when the denoising work happens instead of trying to do less of it. At each control tick it emits a whole trajectory of upcoming actions, but only the immediate action is fully denoised and ready to execute; the actions further out are left deliberately partially denoised. On the next tick those partly-cleaned future actions are rolled over and pushed a few steps further along, so the near-term action is always nearly finished by the time it is needed. This spreads, or amortizes, the cost of denoising across many control ticks rather than paying it all at once. It reaches this speed without distillation, keeping the original policy intact.
Why it matters
By fully finishing only the one action it has to emit right now and carrying the rest forward half-done, the policy pays a tiny per-tick cost and can react at control speed instead of pausing to synthesize from scratch.
ROBOTICS'25FlowMP: Learning Motion Fields for Robot Planning with Conditional Flow Matching
Problem
Robot motion planning has to produce paths that are smooth and dynamically feasible, not just geometrically valid. Plain first-order flow matching ignores acceleration, so the trajectories it generates come out jerky and hard for a real robot to follow.
Approach
FlowMP extends conditional flow matching to second order, meaning it accounts for acceleration and not merely position and velocity. It learns a smooth, continuous transformation that carries a simple prior distribution straight into the distribution of good target trajectories, generating a whole plan in a single pass with no iterative denoising loop. The acceleration behavior is captured either explicitly, by modeling it in the network, or implicitly, through the training objective. Because the acceleration is respected, the resulting paths are smooth enough for a robot to actually execute. It beats both first-order flow and diffusion-based planners on trajectory smoothness and planning success rate.
Why it matters
Here flow matching lays down an entire feasible trajectory in one pass rather than grinding through many denoising steps, so the planner is both fast enough to sit inside a real-time system and produces motion a robot can follow without smoothing it after the fact.

1 · What happened, and when

A video is time passing, not a stack of pictures. So a model that answers questions about a video has to know two things a single photo can never tell it: when something happened and in what order. Did she pour the batter before or after she lit the stove? Point to the exact second the ball first bounces. The honest failure mode of most video models is that they peek at one lucky frame, see a stove and a bowl, and simply guess the order — and because they are guessing, they will happily contradict themselves if you ask the same question two different ways. The papers here stop the guessing by making the model commit to a moment: they localize the segment where the answer actually lives, index the frames so the model can point at a timestamp, sample its attention toward the frames that matter instead of spreading it thin, and force the reasoning to name the frames it used. Once a model has to say which frame it looked at, it can no longer hide a guess behind a confident sentence.

CVPR'26Chain-of-Frames: Advancing Video Understanding in Multimodal LLMs via Frame-Aware Reasoning
Problem

When a video model explains its answer step by step, the explanation usually floats free of the actual footage — it never says which moment it is talking about, so its reasoning can drift out of order without anyone noticing. The alternative has been to bolt on a separate key-frame-finder model first, which is slow and clumsy. What is missing is reasoning that stays anchored to specific frames in a single pass.

Approach

Chain-of-Frames teaches the model to write its reasoning with the frame numbers baked right in — literally saying Frame 5 shows the pouring, Frame 12 shows the stove lit as it thinks. To learn this habit the authors build a training set of 164,186 examples where every reasoning step is tagged with the frame it relies on, drawn both from real annotated videos and from a synthetic physics world (CLEVRER) where appearance-order and counting questions can be generated automatically at zero cost. They fine-tune off-the-shelf video models (InternVL2.5-4B and InternVL3-8B) on this frame-grounded data, with no extra key-frame model needed at inference. Strikingly, the cheap synthetic data alone — with no real footage — still teaches the frame-pointing habit well enough to help on real-world benchmarks.

Why it matters

Forcing the model to name the frame behind each step means it can no longer smuggle in a guess: the improvement (for example, VSI-Bench jumping from 41.0 to 51.3) comes precisely from reasoning that knows when, not just what.

CVPR'26GroundVTS: Visual Token Sampling in Multimodal Large Language Models for Video Temporal Grounding
Problem

To answer when did this happen, a model has to place events on a timeline with real precision. But standard video models chop the video into evenly spaced frames and treat every one the same, so the few frames that actually contain the answer get drowned out and the fine timing is lost. Bolting on an outside frame-picker helps only coarsely.

Approach

GroundVTS keeps the whole video but then spends the model's attention unevenly, concentrating it where the answer lives. After the video is encoded into tokens, it scores each token against the text of the question and keeps the ones that best match — so a query about a specific action pulls in more tokens from that stretch of time and thins out the rest. This produces a deliberately non-uniform distribution across both time and space, unlike the flat, one-size-fits-all sampling it replaces, and it happens inside the model rather than in a separate module. Because feeding a model such lopsided input can destabilize it, they ramp it up with a progressive training schedule so the model learns to trust the concentrated tokens.

Why it matters

By pouring its limited attention onto the moment the question is really about, the model can pin down a timestamp instead of averaging over the whole clip — worth a 7.7-point jump in moment-retrieval accuracy.

CVPR'26ViKey: Enhancing Temporal Understanding in Videos via Visual Prompting
Problem

To run fast, video models throw away most frames and keep only a sparse few — but ripping out the in-between frames snaps the thread of time, and the model loses track of what came before what. It ends up misreading the order of events, which is exactly what a temporal question hinges on.

Approach

ViKey restores the sense of order with a trick that needs no retraining: it simply stamps a small label like frame #01 onto each surviving frame, so the picture itself carries its place in the sequence. It then adds a Keyword-Frame Mapping step that treats those stamped indices like the keys of a dictionary, letting a word in the question look up the specific frame it refers to. Together these give the model a lightweight way to anchor its reasoning to a timestamp at inference time, with no change to the model's architecture. Remarkably, the authors show the visual stamps alone are enough to bring back the model's sense of order after aggressive frame-dropping had scrambled it.

Why it matters

A visible frame number is a cheap handle on when: with only a fifth of the frames, ViKey holds onto dense-video accuracy because the model can still say which frame — and therefore which moment — it is pointing at.

CVPR'26Understanding Temporal Logic Consistency in Video-Language Models through Cross-Modal Attention Discriminability
Problem

Ask a video model the same timing question two different ways and it will often give you two contradictory answers — a sign it never really understood the order, it just guessed. This paper asks why, and traces the flaw to a specific mechanism inside the model rather than treating it as bad luck.

Approach

The authors open up the model and look at its cross-modal attention — the part that links words to video frames — and find the culprit: those attention heads cannot tell frames at different times apart, so the model's picture of the timeline is essentially blurry. Everything runs together, which is exactly why it flips its answer when a temporal question is reworded. To fix it, they add a training objective called Temporally Conditioned Attention Sharpening (TCAS) that pushes those heads to draw crisper distinctions between one timestamp and the next, sharpening the blur into a clear ordering. This is a targeted intervention on the diagnosed cause, not a general retraining.

Why it matters

When the model can actually tell moments apart, it stops contradicting itself — and the same sharpening that fixes the inconsistency also lifts its accuracy on ordinary when-did-it-happen grounding tasks.

2 · What happens next

Anticipation is what turns a passive observer into an agent. A driver does not merely see a pedestrian at the curb; the driver guesses where that person is about to step, and a robot foresees where a block will slide before it ever pushes it. The papers here all try to build that foresight into a machine: from the frames already seen, predict the next frames, the next action, or the path ahead. But the crucial difficulty is that the future is genuinely multi-modal — the pedestrian might cross, wait, or turn back, and each is a real possibility. Committing to a single averaged guess is therefore wrong-headed, since the average of two valid futures is often a third future that makes no physical sense, so many of these methods instead learn a whole distribution of plausible futures and draw samples from it. Increasingly this is framed as a generative rollout: unspool a range of coherent continuations conditioned on the observed past, rather than regress one deterministic answer.
CVPR'26Gaussian-Mixture Latent Flow for Stochastic 3D Human Motion Prediction
Problem
Where will a body move next? Given a few seconds of someone walking, reaching, or turning, we want to predict how their skeleton continues. The trouble is that human motion is inherently uncertain — from the same starting pose, many different continuations are all plausible — and a method that predicts a single deterministic future, or that models this uncertainty too crudely, simply cannot capture that spread of possibilities.
Approach
The method embraces the many-futures problem head-on by predicting a mixture of plausible motions rather than one. It works in a compressed latent space, where instead of a single blob of probability it places a Gaussian mixture — several bell-curves that between them can describe a genuinely multi-peaked set of outcomes (turn left, turn right, keep straight). On top of that it layers a continuous normalizing flow, a generative trick that gradually warps a simple starting distribution into the complicated real one, so the model can bend those mixture components into the true shape of future motion. Predicting a new future then becomes sampling from this learned distribution, and each draw is a different but self-consistent continuation of the body's movement. The result is a better balance between diversity (covering the range of what could happen) and accuracy (each sample being a realistic motion).
Why it matters
This paper is a clean statement of the theme's core idea: because the next moments of motion are truly multi-modal, the honest thing to predict is a distribution you can sample from, not a single averaged pose that would blur several real futures into an unnatural one.
CVPR'26Perceiving the Near, Reasoning the Distant: Coherent Long-Horizon Trajectory Prediction
Problem
Where is that car going over the next eight seconds? For self-driving, predicting a long stretch of future path is essential, but the near future and the distant future are different animals: the next two seconds are tightly constrained by current speed and heading, while five-plus seconds out is dominated by open-ended choices. Forcing one predictor to handle both at once produces kinematically incoherent paths — impossible accelerations and jitter — and errors that snowball the further out you look.
Approach
The system, NDPNet, splits the future into a near stage and a distant stage and treats them differently. Stage one predicts the immediate 0–2 second path using learnable query slots that attend over the surrounding agents and the map, producing several candidate short-term trajectories. A Near-to-Distant Temporal Bridging step then compresses each of those short trajectories into a compact context feature — via a small recurrent encoder over frequency-embedded trajectory points — so the near future becomes the explicit springboard for the far future. Stage two predicts the 2–8 second continuation by attending over that bridge together with the scene, conditioned on each near-future hypothesis, so the distant guess is always grounded in a concrete beginning. A Motion-Aware Coherence loss ties both stages together with physics-style constraints on position and direction, keeping the whole eight-second path physically plausible. It is the first method to break minFDE6 of 1.75 on the Waymo benchmark while staying lightweight and real-time.
Why it matters
It shows anticipation is not one uniform task: the near future is perceived from momentum while the distant future must be reasoned out among many branches, and bridging the two is what keeps a long-horizon prediction coherent instead of dissolving into nonsense.
CVPR'26Prototypical Action Reasoning Facilitated by Vision-Language Alignment for Egocentric Action Anticipation
Problem
What is this person about to do next? From first-person (head-mounted-camera) video of someone cooking or working, we want to anticipate the coming action before it happens. This is hard because future actions are stochastic and open-ended, and because an action is compositional — a verb plus a noun, like cut tomato or pour water — so a system with no explicit sense of meaning struggles to reason about which combination comes next.
Approach
The framework, PAR-VLA, brings in language as a scaffold for meaning by aligning vision with text to learn separate prototypes for verbs and for nouns — reusable semantic anchors like a generic cutting concept or a generic tomato concept, distilled from a vision-language model. As new video arrives, a reasoning module dynamically retrieves the relevant verb and noun prototypes and models how they interact, so the messy open-ended prediction problem is reshaped into a more constrained conditional forecast guided by well-defined concepts. A pair of coupled decoders then predicts the verb and the noun together while respecting their interdependence — certain actions only pair with certain objects — which sharpens both halves of the guess. By anchoring prediction to named concepts rather than raw pixels, the model handles the ambiguity between visually similar fine-grained actions and copes better with the sheer uncertainty of what comes next.
Why it matters
It reframes anticipation as reasoning over a small set of meaningful building blocks (verbs and nouns) rather than blindly extrapolating pixels, taming a genuinely uncertain, combinatorial future by giving the model semantic anchors to bet on.
CVPR'26Generative Point Tracking and Forecasting
Problem
Follow a point through a video — then keep going past the end. Tracking where a physical point on an object travels across frames, and forecasting where it will go next, are usually built as two separate systems. Solving them apart misses the shared structure between them and tends to produce trajectories that are not temporally coherent when tracking hands off to prediction.
Approach
This work unifies tracking and forecasting inside a single generative model, so the same machinery that infers a point's past path also rolls it forward into the future. Rather than emit one deterministic trajectory, it uses a probabilistic generative approach — in the diffusion or flow family — that can represent a range of possible motions and sample coherent point paths from it. Because tracking and forecasting share one framework, the consistency learned while following a point observed in the video carries directly into the motion it predicts beyond the video. This joint formulation improves both sides at once: cleaner tracking and better-grounded forecasts, with smoother temporal coherence across the seam between what was seen and what is predicted.
Why it matters
It is the clearest expression of the emerging pattern in this theme — treating seeing the past and predicting the future as one generative rollout, where a probabilistic model naturally represents the several futures a point might take rather than one brittle guess.

3 · Stop the flicker, remember the hour

Run an image model on every frame independently and the result shimmers — depth jitters from one frame to the next, a segmented object flickers in and out, an edit wobbles as the camera moves. The fix is temporal consistency: add glue that propagates information across frames and anchors each new frame to what came before, so the video stays stable instead of being reinvented sixty times a second. Separately, video is expensive to remember: a one-minute clip is already tens of thousands of tokens, and an hour is impossible to hold whole. So long-video methods learn to forget well — compress the old frames that add little, select the few frames that actually matter, and stream causally so the model can answer at any moment without re-watching everything. The recurring idea across both halves is a bounded, adaptive memory: a summary of the past that stays small yet stays responsive to whatever the newest frame brings.
CVPR'26STCDiT: Spatio-Temporally Consistent Diffusion Transformer for High-Quality Video Super-Resolution
Problem
Diffusion models are great at hallucinating realistic detail, which is exactly what you want when upscaling a blurry video — but run that generative power frame by frame and it invents different details each time, so the sharpened video shimmers and drifts, especially under complex camera motion. The other risk is that the model, in its enthusiasm to add texture, changes the underlying structure and stops being faithful to the original.
Approach
STCDiT keeps the video stable in two ways. First, a motion-aware reconstruction step chops the video into short clips that each contain one uniform kind of motion, so the compressor never has to handle a pan and a zoom in the same breath. Second, and most important, it uses anchor-frame guidance: the first frame’s latent, which survives temporal compression cleanly, is treated as a structural anchor that every other frame is constrained to match. An anchor-corresponding feature-modulation step then decides which spatial information to borrow from that anchor. The whole thing is bolted on with a small LoRA adapter, adding only about 7% extra parameters.
Why it matters
By tying every generated frame back to a shared anchor, the detail the model invents stays consistent across time instead of flickering — you get sharp, realistic upscaling that still faithfully tracks the real structure of the scene.
CVPR'26MoCha: End-to-End Video Character Replacement without Structural Guidance
Problem
Swapping a character into a video — for film, virtual try-on, or avatars — normally demands a per-frame segmentation mask plus explicit structural scaffolding like a skeleton or depth map for every single frame. That scaffolding is expensive to produce and brittle: it falls apart under occlusions, unusual poses, characters interacting, and tricky lighting.
Approach
MoCha throws out the per-frame masks and the structural guidance entirely, and instead leans on something the video diffusion model already knows how to do: track a subject through time on its own. You give it a single mask on one arbitrary frame, and the model’s own temporal tracking carries the replacement forward through the clip. A condition-aware position encoding (RoPE) fuses the different inputs coherently, and a reinforcement-learning post-training stage with a differentiable facial reward keeps the swapped identity crisp and consistent. Training data is stitched together from UE5 renders, animated portrait videos, and existing video-mask pairs.
Why it matters
Trusting the model’s built-in frame-to-frame tracking, rather than re-specifying structure on every frame, keeps the replaced character temporally stable through occlusions and motion instead of jittering wherever the per-frame guidance would have broken.
CVPR'26FluxMem: Adaptive Hierarchical Memory for Streaming Video Understanding
Problem
A model watching a live video stream — for robotics, driving, or smart glasses — has to answer questions the moment they come, while a never-ending flood of frames piles up. Cramming all of it into the language model is impossible, but the usual fix of one fixed compression rule either keeps too much stale long-ago context or throws away the fine recent detail that a causal answer depends on.
Approach
FluxMem is a training-free memory with three tiers: recent frames are kept in full, mid-range frames are trimmed for temporal redundancy (a lightweight module compares neighboring frames and keeps only tokens that actually changed), and old frames are trimmed for spatial redundancy (repeated regions are merged into a few representative anchors). The clever part is that it never uses a hand-set cutoff: it borrows Otsu’s method from image thresholding to read each frame’s own score distribution and split keep-versus-drop automatically, so it compresses harder or softer as the scene changes. The same statistics also flag scene changes, letting the model decide when it’s worth speaking up.
Why it matters
This adaptive, tiered forgetting keeps memory bounded over an unbounded stream — cutting latency by about 70% and peak GPU memory by a third — while preserving exactly the fresh detail that answering in the moment requires.
CVPR'26Wavelet-based Frame Selection by Detecting Semantic Boundary for Long Video Understanding
Problem
A vision-language model can only look at a handful of frames from a long video, so which frames you feed it decides everything. Picking the frames that merely score highest for the question tends to grab a cluster of near-duplicate moments, missing the transitions — so the model loses the storyline and can’t reason about what caused what.
Approach
WFS-SB reframes frame selection as a signal-processing problem. It takes the noisy, jittery signal of how relevant each frame is to the query and runs a wavelet transform to decompose it into multiple scales, stripping out the noise to leave a clean signal of where the video’s meaning actually shifts. Peaks and valleys in that coarse signal mark the semantic boundaries — the cuts between scenes or steps — and the video is segmented into coherent clips there. It then hands each clip its own share of the tiny frame budget and, within each clip, picks a diverse spread using Maximal Marginal Relevance. The whole method is training-free.
Why it matters
By spending the frame budget on genuine narrative turning points rather than clustered relevance peaks, it preserves the procedural, cause-and-effect thread of an hour-long video — the coherence that isolated high-relevance frames throw away.

4 · Learn from the arrow, spend compute where time is dense

Time hands you free labels. The correct order of frames, the fact that the future follows the past and not the reverse, and the ability to hide part of a clip and ask a model to fill it back in are all supervision you get for nothing, no human annotation required. This is where the arrow of time shows up most explicitly: the smartest papers deliberately break the symmetry between past and future, so a model is forced to learn how the world changes rather than what a single frozen frame looks like. When you reconstruct a hidden future from a visible past, or reverse a pair of images and demand the answer flip too, you are teaching dynamics instead of static appearance. Separately, there is a brutal cost problem: attention over long video grows quadratically, so a forty-minute clip is hopeless to attend over directly. Two efficiency families dominate the response: state-space models (Mamba) that scan the sequence in linear time and generalize cleanly from short clips to long ones, and cross-frame token reduction that throws away redundant frames before they ever reach the expensive transformer. The shared idea running through all of it is to treat time as an asymmetric axis and to spend compute only where the video actually changes.
CVPR'26Recurrent Video Masked Autoencoders
Problem
The leading video self-supervised methods encode space and time together in one early-fusion block, which treats time as if the two directions were interchangeable. That symmetry blocks them from online and streaming use, and their features drift and degrade over long clips, so they do well on whole-clip labels but poorly on dense per-pixel tasks that need strong geometry.
Approach
This work processes a video one frame at a time with a recurrent core, and it bakes the arrow of time directly into the learning signal through asymmetric masking: a handful of past source frames are left fully visible, and a future target frame is hidden at a punishing 95 percent mask ratio. A shared vision-transformer encoder reads each frame on its own, and a hybrid gated recurrent core carries a running state forward through time, using reset and update gates to decide how much old state to keep versus how much new evidence to fold in. A decoder then reconstructs the masked future frame from the accumulated past, trained with a plain pixel-level loss. Because the past is unmasked and the future is masked, the model cannot cheat by copying appearance and is forced to predict how the scene evolves. The recurrence gives stable, causal, directional memory at linear cost, so features stay coherent over eighty-plus frames instead of decaying.
Why it matters
By making the future the thing you must reconstruct from the past, the arrow of time becomes free supervision that teaches dynamics rather than static texture, and the recurrent state lets a small 34M model beat encoders four times its size while staying stable over long horizons.
CVPR'26Temporal Inversion for Learning Interval Change in Chest X-Rays
Problem
Most medical vision-language models read a chest X-ray in isolation, but the real clinical question is how a finding has changed between a prior scan and the current one. Existing evaluations barely probe this directional temporal reasoning, so models never learn which way time actually ran.
Approach
The method turns the direction of time itself into a supervision signal by feeding the model both the correct prior-then-current ordering and its temporal inversion, the reversed pair. A change-aware sigmoid loss pulls unchanged cases together and pushes changed cases apart, while a bidirectional cross-entropy term demands that when you flip the image order the predicted change label flips with it. A temporal consistency loss further requires the probability distributions to align coherently under reversal. This inversion is woven through pretraining, fine-tuning, and inference, not bolted on at the end. The result is an order-aware representation that genuinely encodes progression rather than just averaging two appearances.
Why it matters
Reversing the pair and insisting the answer reverse too is a free label straight from the arrow of time, forcing the model to represent interval change, the actual clinical quantity, instead of static per-image appearance.
CVPR'26MS-Temba: Multi-Scale Temporal Mamba for Understanding Long Untrimmed Videos
Problem
Detecting actions in long untrimmed videos, up to forty minutes with densely overlapping events, defeats transformers because attention cost grows quadratically with length. Yet a naive state-space model runs cheaply but smears together the fine-grained temporal structure that action boundaries depend on.
Approach
MS-Temba keeps the linear-time scan of Mamba, which sweeps the whole sequence in a single pass whose cost grows only linearly with length, and fixes the resolution problem with dilated state-space models at several temporal scales at once. Its Temba Blocks run multiple dilated branches in parallel, some watching tight short-term dynamics and others reaching across long-range structure, tied together by projection-based alignment and scale-aware supervision. A lightweight multi-scale fuser then aggregates those branches, again through a state-space scan rather than attention, into boundary-accurate predictions. This lets one efficient model span both the split-second motion and the minutes-long context of a long clip. It reaches state-of-the-art accuracy with just 17M parameters.
Why it matters
The linear-time Mamba scan is what makes forty-minute video tractable at all, and the multi-scale dilation is how you spend that cheap compute densely enough to keep fine temporal precision, beating transformers by eight points at roughly a fifth the parameters.
CVPR'26StreamingTOM: Streaming Token Compression for Efficient Video Understanding
Problem
In a live video stream, tokens pile up without bound and you cannot peek at future frames, so cost grows as the clip runs on. Prior efficiency work only trims the key-value cache after the language model, leaving the costly pre-model prefill stage untouched.
Approach
StreamingTOM is a training-free, strictly causal pipeline that cuts tokens before they ever reach the expensive transformer, respecting the arrow of time by never looking ahead. Its Causal Temporal Reduction stage keeps a fixed per-frame budget of tokens, choosing which to keep from how much each frame changed relative to the frame just before it plus a saliency score, so redundant near-duplicate frames are dropped where the video barely moves. Because the budget per frame is fixed and forward-only, latency stays predictable no matter how long the stream runs. A second stage stores the surviving tokens in compact 4-bit quantized memory and retrieves them on demand, bounding cache growth. Together these attack both the pre-model and post-model bottlenecks under strict causality, with no retraining.
Why it matters
A fixed forward-only budget spends compute only where adjacent frames actually differ, so a streaming model runs in bounded memory with predictable latency, delivering roughly 16x cache compression and 2x faster first response.

5 · Predicting the future so a robot can act

A robot does not watch time to understand what already happened; it models time to guess what happens next, so it can move before it is too late. To plan a safe path it must forecast where each person and car will be, not where they are right now, because by the time the robot reacts the scene has already changed. And the future is never a single line — a pedestrian at a corner might cross, wait, or turn back — so good methods are multi-modal: they sample a whole spread of plausible paths and hand that spread to the planner instead of one brittle guess. For choosing its own action, the robot learns a world model, a predictor of how the scene evolves given what the robot does, then plans by rolling that model forward in imagination and picking the action whose imagined future looks best. Here the forward arrow of time is not a nice-to-have; it is literally the planning substrate the robot computes on. And being wrong has a physical cost — a collision, a dropped handover, a missed grasp — not a low benchmark score. The punchline: vision remembers time, robotics bets on it.
ROBOTICS'25Curb Your Attention: Causal Attention Gating for Robust Trajectory Prediction in Autonomous Driving
Problem
A self-driving car has to predict where every nearby agent is heading, but most prediction models pay attention to all surrounding agents equally — including ones that have nothing to do with what the target will do next. A parked car or a pedestrian on the far sidewalk can perturb the forecast, and a wrong forecast quietly poisons the driving decision that depends on it.
Approach
CRiTIC first runs a causal discovery network over a short window of past motion to figure out which agents actually influence which — who is genuinely reacting to whom, versus who just happens to be nearby. It then feeds that judgment into a Causal Attention Gating mechanism inside a Transformer, which turns down the attention paid to non-causal agents so their motion cannot leak into the prediction. In effect the model learns to ignore the crowd and watch only the agents that matter for what happens next. This makes the forecast lean on real cause-and-effect rather than spurious correlations that happen to appear in one dataset. The reported gains are large: up to 54% better robustness to non-causal perturbations and up to 29% better generalization across domains.
Why it matters
The whole point of predicting other agents is so the car can plan around them, and a forecast corrupted by an irrelevant agent is exactly the kind of error that turns into an unnecessary brake or a real collision. Filtering the prediction down to true causes is filtering the plan down to reality.
ROBOTICS'25GDTS: Goal-Guided Diffusion Model with Tree Sampling for Multi-Modal Pedestrian Trajectory Prediction
Problem
To move safely through people, a robot must predict pedestrian paths — and pedestrians are genuinely unpredictable, so one path is not enough; the model needs a spread of plausible futures. Diffusion models can produce that spread, but they tend to collapse toward a few similar paths and they are slow, needing many denoising steps, which is a problem when the robot must decide right now.
Approach
GDTS conditions a denoising diffusion model on an estimated goal for each pedestrian — where the person seems to be headed — which anchors the generated paths and keeps the different modes from collapsing onto each other. On top of this it adds a two-stage tree sampling algorithm: the early denoising steps, which are shared across all the candidate futures, are computed once at the trunk of a tree, and only the later steps branch out into the distinct paths. Because the common work is not repeated per path, the model produces many diverse futures in real time. It does this without needing an extra initialization network, simply by reusing the shared intermediate diffusion states. The result is a fast, genuinely multi-modal forecast of where each person might go.
Why it matters
A planner that receives a rich spread of pedestrian futures can leave room for the crossing that might happen, not just the one it guessed; deliver that spread too slowly, or too narrowly, and the robot is planning against a future that has already moved on.
ROBOTICS'25Imagine-2-Drive: Leveraging High-Fidelity World Models via Multi-Modal Diffusion Policies
Problem
One way to learn to drive is to learn a world model — a predictor of how the scene will look after each action — and practice inside that imagined world. But if the world model predicts one step at a time, tiny errors compound into nonsense a few steps out, and a policy that can only pick one kind of maneuver cannot capture the many reasonable ways to drive a situation.
Approach
Imagine-2-Drive pairs two diffusion-based pieces. DiffDreamer is a diffusion world model that generates an entire future observation sequence in parallel rather than rolling forward one step at a time, so a single early mistake does not snowball down the whole imagined rollout. Alongside it, a Diffusion Policy Actor is trained inside that imagined world and can represent a multi-modal distribution over trajectories — letting it hold several distinct driving options at once instead of averaging them into one bland move. The policy improves by planning against these high-fidelity imagined futures and keeping the actions that lead to good ones. On CARLA this yielded 15% better Route Completion and 20% better Success Rate.
Why it matters
The robot commits to actions based on a future it imagined, so if that imagination drifts or collapses to one option, the car practices against a fantasy and fails in the real world — a crash, not a bad number.
ROBOTICS'25X-MOBILITY: End-to-End Generalizable Navigation via World Modeling
Problem
Learned navigation policies tend to work only in the environments they trained on and fall apart in new, cluttered, out-of-distribution scenes, while classical planners need heavy hand-tuning and still struggle with clutter. The gap is that a policy which only memorizes what-to-do never really learns how the world moves.
Approach
X-Mobility trains an auto-regressive world model in a compact latent state space — it repeatedly predicts the next state of the world, step after step, learning the dynamics of how a scene unfolds as the robot travels. Several auxiliary decoder heads force that latent state to actually capture the meaningful structure of the environment. Crucially it decouples world modeling from the action policy, so the dynamics can be learned from cheap off-policy data while the control is learned from on-policy demonstrations. Because the robot has internalized how the world evolves rather than a fixed set of reactions, the same model transfers zero-shot from simulation to reality and even across different robot bodies.
Why it matters
By rolling its learned model of the world forward, the robot can steer through a place it has never seen without retraining — and when the model of the future is sound, the navigation stays safe on real hardware instead of only in the simulator it was born in.

6 · Act a beat early, keep perception stable

A single snapshot cannot tell you whether the mug is being lifted or set down, or whether the person reaching across the table is mid-reach or already done, so a policy that sees only the present is guessing at half the story. To close that gap, robots need temporal context — several frames of history — and, better still, they need to anticipate the human's next move so the robot can start helping before it is asked. One thread here learns skills straight from video: human and egocentric footage becomes a signal the robot reads, and by predicting the next action the robot acts proactively instead of scrambling to react. Another thread keeps perception stable from one frame to the next — the same car keeps its identity, the space it occupies is tracked rather than re-guessed from scratch every instant. This matters because flickering perception makes flickering plans, and flickering plans around people are unsafe. The payoff is a robot that acts a beat early and hands its controller a state estimate it can actually trust.
ROBOTICS'25PACE: Proactive Assistance in Human-Robot Collaboration Through Action-Completion Estimation
Problem
A robot helping a person with a shared task tends to either sit idle or get in the way, because it has no sense of how close the person is to finishing their current step. Without that timing sense, it either waits for an explicit signal or jumps in at the wrong moment. The result is stop-and-go teamwork that feels clumsy rather than fluid.
Approach
PACE estimates how far along the person is in their subtask by watching their hand motion and comparing it, moment by moment, against a reference motion. It uses a time-warping technique (Dynamic Time Warping) with correlation analysis so the progress estimate holds up even when the person moves faster or slower than the reference. That live completion estimate then feeds a reinforcement-learning policy, trained from only a handful of demonstrations, whose whole job is to decide when to step in. Because the robot can see that the human is, say, eighty percent through reaching for a part, it can begin its own contribution just before the help is needed. Measured against reactive baselines, this cuts the robot's idle time and makes the collaboration feel smoother.
Why it matters
Estimating a human's progress turns the robot from a follower into a partner that acts a beat early — timing help to the moment it lands rather than waiting for a request. That is proactivity built directly on a read of the human's ongoing action.
ROBOTICS'25Ego-A3: Adaptive Fusion-Based Disentangled Transformer for Egocentric Action Anticipation
Problem
Predicting what a person is about to do next from a head-worn camera is hard because the video is cluttered with background content that has nothing to do with the coming action. It is made harder still because most models mash together two very different questions — what the person will do and which object they will do it to — into one tangled prediction. When those two are conflated, an error in one drags down the other.
Approach
Ego-A3 first uses adaptive fusion to filter action-irrelevant content out of the incoming video and sensor streams, so the model stays focused on the cues that actually foreshadow the next move. It then feeds what remains into a transformer that carries separate learnable query tokens — one set for the verb (the action) and one set for the noun (the object). Because verb and noun are predicted through their own disentangled slots, a mistake about the object no longer contaminates the guess about the action, and vice versa. The two predictions are produced independently and then combined into a full anticipation of the next verb-plus-noun action. On EpicKitchens-100 and EGTEA Gaze+ this disentangled design reaches state-of-the-art top-1 accuracy.
Why it matters
Anticipating the next action from egocentric video is exactly the signal a robot needs to help before being asked rather than after. Turning human footage into a clean, forward-looking prediction is what lets a policy act proactively instead of reactively.
ROBOTICS'25Failure Forecasting Boosts Robustness of Sim2Real Rhythmic Insertion Tasks
Problem
When a robot repeats a high-precision fitting task many times — think screwing a nut with a wrench, cycle after cycle — tiny pose errors and friction accumulate a little more each round. Left unchecked, those small errors stack up until the whole task fails partway through. The danger is not any single motion but the slow drift across a long horizon.
Approach
The core is a sim-to-real reinforcement-learning policy that tracks the part's exact 6D pose in the nut's own coordinate frame, which keeps the control precise even as the hardware wanders from the simulator. Running alongside it is a small neural network whose only job is to forecast failure — to watch for the tell-tale signs that this cycle is heading toward a jam or a slip. The moment it predicts trouble, it triggers a lift-and-retry recovery: the robot backs off and re-approaches before the error can compound into a breakdown. Anticipating the failure mid-task, rather than discovering it after the part is stuck, is what keeps the long repetitive sequence on track. The nut-frame pose tracking plus this forecasting module together deliver high success rates across many repeated trials.
Why it matters
Seeing a failure coming a beat early and resetting is far cheaper than reacting a beat late, once the errors have already locked in. Forecasting turns a brittle long-horizon loop into a robust one by acting before the breakdown, not after.
ROBOTICS'25TrackOcc: Camera-Based 4D Panoptic Occupancy Tracking
Problem
Camera-based driving perception has historically forced a choice: either build a full 3D picture of what fills every region of space (occupancy) or follow individual objects consistently over time (tracking), but not both at once. Pick occupancy and each frame is re-guessed from scratch, so identities flicker; pick tracking and you lose the dense sense of what space is occupied. Neither alone gives a planner a stable, complete world to trust.
Approach
TrackOcc processes streaming camera images end-to-end using a set of 4D panoptic queries — learned vectors, one per region of space-time. Each query jointly predicts two things at once: which category fills that voxel, and which specific object it belongs to across successive frames. Because segmentation and tracking flow through the same shared queries in a single pass, the model is forced to keep the two consistent — the same car keeps its identity and its occupied space frame to frame, rather than being re-labeled every instant. A localization-aware loss trains the queries to place things correctly in space. It is the first camera-only 4D panoptic occupancy tracking formulation, and it reaches state-of-the-art performance on the Waymo dataset.
Why it matters
Tracking occupancy instead of re-guessing it means identity and space hold steady over time, so the perception the controller reads does not flicker. A stable state estimate is exactly what keeps downstream plans from flickering into unsafe behavior.
Reading it whole. Top to bottom, the themes walk time outward: first understand what happened and when, then predict what happens next, then stop the flicker and remember the hour, then mine the arrow of time for free labels and spend compute only where it changes — and finally hand time to a robot that must predict the future to act early. The tasks differ wildly; the fact that a video is time passing, running one way, never changes.

NeRF / neural fields — why it works, mathematically

Think of a whole scene as one big function: hand it a point in space and a direction to look from, and it tells you the color there and how solid it is. A photo is just what you get by running that function along a lot of sightlines. Fold the photo-making math into the training goal, and the scene rebuilds itself from a handful of pictures.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

NeRF represents a scene as a function. Give it a 3D point and a viewing direction, and it predicts color and density. Rendering follows camera rays through that field.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology matters because density should form coherent surfaces and empty spaces. A good scene model should preserve rooms, openings, object boundaries, and connected parts, not just match the training views.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

It appears in 3D capture, graphics, robotics, archaeology, medical imaging, and simulation.

Why NeRF / neural fields is worth learning

It matters because it showed that ordinary photos can teach a hidden 3D world, as long as the picture-making process is built from smooth steps you can trace a mistake back through.

The problem, as a quantity

A photograph is flat. In squashing the world onto a rectangle it throws away depth, throws away what is hidden behind what, and offers nothing at all for the viewpoints the camera never stood at. From a few such photos you want the full 3D scene back, so you can look at it from anywhere.

The long-standing way is to build an explicit shape first — a surface stitched from tiny triangles, or a stack of little 3D boxes — and then draw a picture from that shape. The trouble is the drawing step. The usual way of turning a shape into pixels works by hard decisions — this triangle covers this pixel, that one does not — and a hard decision has no gentle slope to follow. So when a drawn pixel comes out the wrong color, there is no trail leading backward from that mistake to say which part of the shape to move. The thing doing the fitting is blind to the very step that made the picture.

That is the real bottleneck: unless the drawing step is built out of smooth math that you can trace a mistake back through, photos can never teach a fitter about 3D shape. NeRF turns the usual order around. Instead of building a shape and then drawing it, it represents the entire scene as a single smooth function — ask it about any point in space, from any viewing direction, and it answers with a color and a how-solid number. Making a picture is then defined as a smooth sum along each sightline, which you can trace a mistake back through. Nobody puts the geometry in by hand; it emerges because that is what makes the pictures come out right.

The core ideas — and why they work

The whole scene is one function, not a box of pieces

F_θ : (x,y,z,θ,φ) → (c, σ) where σ ≥ 0 is density and c ∈ [0,1]³ is view-dependent colour
A surface of triangles or a grid of little boxes is chopped into fixed pieces, and you have to choose how fine to chop before you have learned anything from the photos — too coarse and detail is lost forever, too fine and it is impossibly heavy. A learned function takes a different route. It is a formula with a few million adjustable numbers θ inside it, and you can ask it about any point in space at all: it answers with how solid the scene is there (σ) and what color of light leaves that point toward you (c).

Because you can query any point and the function fills in smoothly between the points you check, the effective detail has no fixed grid — it is as fine as you care to look. Feeding in the viewing direction as well lets the color change with where you stand: a bright glint on a glass ball slides across its surface as you walk around it, and the function can learn that the color at one point depends on the angle you see it from. Nothing is stored out in 3D space at all — the entire scene lives inside those few million numbers.

Adding up color along a sightline, in a way you can trace backward

C(r) = ∫₀^∞ T(t)·σ(t)·c(t) dt, T(t) = exp(−∫₀^t σ(s) ds)
Follow a straight sightline out from the camera through one pixel. Along the way it passes through the scene, and at each point two things happen: some light is blocked or absorbed, by an amount set by how solid the scene is there (σ), and the color of that point (c) is added to what the pixel will show. How much light makes it to a given distance without being blocked earlier is T(t), and it steadily shrinks the more solid stuff sits in front. The pixel's final color is the sum of every point's color along the line, front to back, each one weighed down by how little light survived to reach it.

In practice you do not add up a true continuous line — you take a few dozen sample points along it, spaced a small step apart, and add their weighted colors: C ≈ Σᵢ Tᵢ·(1 − exp(−σᵢ·δᵢ))·cᵢ. Near, solid points dominate the color; distant points behind them fade toward nothing.

The point that makes everything else possible: every piece of this sum is smooth. Nudge the how-solid number or the color at any point by a hair and the pixel's color changes by a matching hair. That smoothness is exactly the trail that was missing before — a wrong pixel's error can be traced back, step by step through the sum, all the way to the numbers inside the function, telling each of them which way to move.

Turning the position into a bundle of waves wakes up fine detail

γ(x) = [sin(2⁰πx), cos(2⁰πx), sin(2¹πx), cos(2¹πx), …, sin(2^L πx), cos(2^L πx)]
A plain learned function of this kind has a built-in leaning toward smooth, gradual answers. Broad, blurry shapes it picks up quickly; sharp edges and fine texture it learns painfully slowly or never, because reproducing tiny fast variations would need its internal numbers tuned in a delicate way it does not naturally drift into. This is a real, measurable effect: in a direct test, feeding the raw position gave a far worse picture than feeding waves — a pixel error roughly fourteen times larger (a match score of 23.25 against 34.31, higher being better).

The fix does not touch the function at all — it changes what you feed in. Instead of handing over the bare position, you first turn that position into a whole bundle of sine and cosine waves at many scales, from slow gentle ripples up to fast tight wiggles. Now the fine detail the function struggled to invent is already present in its input, and all it has to do is mix these ready-made waves together — something it does easily. The leaning toward smoothness is not beaten by making the function bigger; it is sidestepped by giving the function a richer thing to look at.

It learns from the photos alone — no depth, no labels

L(θ) = Σ_rays ‖C_θ(r) − C_gt(r)‖², ∂L/∂θ = Σ_rays (C_θ − C_gt) · ∂C_θ/∂θ
The training loop is plain. Pick a sightline through a pixel whose true color you know from a real photo, run the sum above to get the function's predicted color, compare it to the real color, and nudge every one of the function's numbers a little in whichever direction shrinks that gap. Do this across many pixels and many photos, over and over.

Nobody ever marks up depth, or labels what anything is, or supplies a point cloud. The function ends up putting solid stuff exactly where the edges in the photos demand it, because placing it anywhere else would make some photo come out wrong. The different camera angles police each other: a made-up piece of geometry that happens to look right from one view betrays itself from another, so the only way to satisfy all the photos at once is to get the real 3D shape right. Because shape and color are pushed toward the same target together, a function that reproduces the training photos almost always has the hidden 3D shape right as well. The price is speed: getting one pixel's color means asking the function about many points along its sightline, every round, which is slow.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Every faster version of NeRF answers the same two questions: what carries the scene instead of one slow function, and how do you sample each sightline with less work?

The first version asked its function about a hundred-odd points on every pixel's sightline, and took hours per scene. The best-known speedup keeps most of the scene in a set of lookup tables at several scales — a quick read-off blended by only a tiny function — which cuts training from hours to seconds. Another family adds time as an extra input, or learns how shapes bend and move from frame to frame, which lets the same idea capture a moving scene rather than a frozen one. Underneath, nothing changes: the scene is a function you can query anywhere, a picture is a smooth sum along each sightline, and the photos are the only teacher.

A worked numeric example

If a ray samples densities [0.2,0.5] and colors [1.0,0.4], each sample contributes color weighted by accumulated transmittance. Raising early density both adds its own color and blocks later color. NeRF learns geometry because opacity controls what light reaches the camera.

The through-line. NeRF stands on one formula and one decision. The formula adds up color along a sightline, weighed by how much light survives to each point — and it is smooth, so a mistake in the final pixel can be traced back through it. The decision is to place that formula inside the training goal, so that ordinary photos, with nothing labelled, end up teaching 3D shape. Two hurdles had to be cleared along the way: the function's natural pull toward blurry answers, sidestepped by feeding it a bundle of waves, and the sheer cost of asking it about so many points per pixel. What carries the whole method is not the function itself but the choice to build the entire picture-making process out of smooth, traceable steps — because once a mistake in a photo can be traced all the way back to the scene, the photo itself becomes the teacher.

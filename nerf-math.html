Turn photos into a continuous 3D scene by learning to render light paths

The problem, as a quantity

A photograph captures light rays bouncing through space, encoding full 3D structure in 2D pixels. Classical methods reconstruct geometry (meshes, voxels) then render; NeRF reverses this: learn the rendering process directly from photos, so the 3D scene emerges as a side effect. The core insight: if we can predict what colour and density exist at any point in space, differentiable ray-casting becomes a supervisor — images teach the field by comparing rendered pixels to real ones.

The core ideas — and why they work

Volume rendering as differentiation

The mathematical engine
Light travels along a ray and accumulates colour as it scatters and absorbs. Formally: C = ∫ T(t)·σ(t)·c(t) dt, where σ(t) is density at distance t, c(t) is the emitted/scattered colour, and T(t) = exp(−∫_0^t σ ds) is transmittance — how much light survives to reach point t. In discrete form (Σ_i T_i·(1 − exp(−σ_i·δ_i))·c_i), this is front-to-back alpha blending: near-surface voxels dominate, distant ones fade. The magic: this integral is fully differentiable. Gradient descent can compute ∂C/∂σ and ∂C/∂c, so pixels directly teach the density and colour field. No mesh, no hand-tuned shaders — just backprop through the render equation.

Coordinate MLP as a scene parameterization

The learnable field
A neural network maps spatial location (x, y, z) and viewing direction → (density σ, colour c). This is a radical simplification: instead of storing voxels on a grid or vertices on a mesh, we store a single function approximator. One forward pass predicts the entire scene at any coordinate. The network has no inductive bias toward geometry — it learns to fit whatever density and colour fields match the photos. During training, the MLP gets called millions of times along ray-casts, each call compared to real pixel values via squared error loss. Gradients flow back, and weights shift to reduce the error. The result: a smooth, memory-efficient implicit representation that generalizes across unobserved viewpoints because it learned the underlying 3D structure, not just a lookup table.

Positional encoding defeats spectral bias

High-frequency geometry emerges
Raw MLPs are notoriously smooth — they struggle to represent sharp edges or fine details (spectral bias). NeRF sidesteps this with Fourier features: γ(x) = [sin(2^0·x), cos(2^0·x), sin(2^1·x), cos(2^1·x), ..., sin(2^k·x), cos(2^k·x)]. The input to the MLP is no longer bare coordinates but this sinusoidal embedding across multiple frequencies. High powers of 2 oscillate fast — they carry high-frequency information. Low powers carry coarse structure. The MLP, given this frequency decomposition, can mix and weight components to reconstruct arbitrarily fine detail. Mathematically: Fourier bases span a much richer function space than the MLP's natural weights can learn directly, so encoding 'lifts' the problem into a domain where gradient descent converges faster and reaches higher fidelity.

Differentiable rendering as a supervision loop

Photos train the field
Training is pure supervision: (1) sample a ray through a real pixel, (2) cast it through the learned field, accumulating colour and density, (3) compare rendered pixel to ground-truth photo, (4) backprop ∂loss/∂σ and ∂loss/∂c through the compositing chain and the MLP. No masks, no pre-segmented objects — just raw images. The MLP is incentivized to place high density where surfaces should be (to match silhouettes and edges) and high colour where textures should be. Because the volume-rendering integral is differentiable end-to-end, geometry and appearance co-optimize. Views unseen during training are predicted using the learned 3D field, recovering novel view synthesis nearly for free.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Original NeRF used dense MLPs queried per ray — slow but highly accurate. Instant-NeRF introduced hash grids (coarse-to-fine spatial hashing) + shallow MLPs, cutting train time from hours to minutes. Gaussian Splatting abandons volume rendering entirely, storing local Gaussians instead, for real-time rasterization (trading accuracy for speed). SDF variants (like neural unsigned distance functions) replace density with signed distance, enabling cleaner surfaces and watertight reconstruction. 3D-GS (Gaussian Splatting in 3D, not 2D projection) improved quality. In dynamic scenes, time-conditioning extends the MLP to handle motion, or separate layers capture geometry vs. deformation. Medical and physics-driven fields add symmetry, physics priors, or constraints (e.g., smoothness) to the loss. Robotics applications train fields as the robot moves, building occupancy maps for collision avoidance or path planning; occupancy itself becomes a learned implicit field.

1The original job, and the cost fight
C_mesh = ∫ σ(s) exp(−∫ σ(u)du) c(s) ds over tetrahedra or triangles; mesh storage O(V) vs neural O(1) (weights)
Explicit surface meshes trade memory for rendering speed: Delaunay tetrahedra or triangle soups fix geometry upfront, eliminating the need to query a coordinate network at dense sample points. Conversely, neural fields amortize geometric detail across learnable weights via the volume-rendering integral, paying a query cost per ray but storing the entire scene in a single compact MLP.

The through-line. A scene is a continuous function from space to appearance (colour and density). Photos are a rendering of that function. By making rendering differentiable and comparing rendered images to real ones, we teach the function to match the world. High-frequency positional encoding and neural networks together learn functions rich enough to capture fine geometry and texture at arbitrary resolution.

NeRF / neural fields — why it works, mathematically

A scene is a continuous function from space to appearance. Photos are a rendering of that function. Put the rendering equation inside the loss, and gradient descent reconstructs the world.

The problem, as a quantity

A photograph is not a scene — it is a projection of a scene. The projection destroys depth, occlusion, and all viewing angles that were not captured. Classical pipelines try to invert this: fit a mesh or a voxel grid to match the photos, then render from it. The trouble is that a mesh must be explicitly defined and rasterised, and rasterisation is not differentiable — there is no path from 'the rendered pixel is wrong' back to 'move this vertex'. So the optimizer cannot see through the renderer. The real obstacle is this: the rendering step must be part of the computation graph, or photos cannot teach geometry. NeRF reframes the problem entirely: instead of reconstructing a geometric object and then rendering it, represent the scene as a continuous field F_θ(x,d) → (c, σ) — colour and density at every point in space — and define rendering as an integral that is differentiable by construction. Geometry is then not fitted; it emerges as a side effect of minimising the difference between rendered and real pixels.

The core ideas — and why they work

The scene as a continuous field

F_θ : (x,y,z,θ,φ) → (c, σ) where σ ≥ 0 is density and c ∈ [0,1]³ is view-dependent colour
A mesh has faces, a voxel grid has cells — both are discrete, and any discrete representation forces you to commit to a resolution before you have seen the data. A neural network parameterised by weights θ makes a different bet: use a function instead of a container. Query it at any point in continuous space and it returns how solid space is there (σ) and what colour that solid emits toward your eye (c). The resolution is effectively infinite, because the MLP interpolates smoothly between sampled points. The viewing direction input (θ,φ) lets the field represent view-dependent effects — the specular highlight on a glass sphere moves as you orbit it, and the MLP learns that shift. Nothing is stored in space; everything lives in 4 MB of weights. Same primitive elsewhere: this is the Low rank and compression move — instead of storing a volumetric grid (millions of numbers), you compress the entire scene into the low-dimensional manifold of an MLP's weight space.

Volume rendering — the differentiable integral

C(r) = ∫₀^∞ T(t)·σ(t)·c(t) dt, T(t) = exp(−∫₀^t σ(s) ds)
Light travels a ray, and at each point along it two things happen: some fraction of the surviving light is absorbed or scattered (controlled by σ), and what remains carries the colour c at that point. Transmittance T(t) is how much light reaches distance t without being stopped — it decays exponentially with accumulated density. The rendering equation integrates colour contributions front-to-back, each weighted by both T(t) (how much light survived to reach this point) and σ(t) (how much of it this point absorbs). In practice the integral is discretised: for N samples at distances t_i with spacings δ_i, C ≈ Σᵢ Tᵢ·(1 − exp(−σᵢ·δᵢ))·cᵢ where Tᵢ = exp(−Σⱼ＜ᵢ σⱼ·δⱼ). This is exactly front-to-back alpha compositing — near surfaces dominate, distant ones fade. The crucial property: every operation is smooth in σ and c, so ∂C/∂σ and ∂C/∂c exist everywhere. Gradient descent can now flow from pixel error all the way back into the field. Same primitive elsewhere: this is the Differentiable rendering primitive — put the physics of image formation inside the computation graph so that observations can supervise geometry. Gaussian Splatting uses the same philosophical move, replacing the continuous integral with a projected alpha-blend over Gaussians to gain speed.

Positional encoding defeats spectral bias

γ(x) = [sin(2⁰πx), cos(2⁰πx), sin(2¹πx), cos(2¹πx), …, sin(2^L πx), cos(2^L πx)]
A plain MLP with smooth activations is a low-pass filter by default. This is spectral bias: the network's gradient dynamics during training preferentially reduce error at low spatial frequencies first — smooth, blurry predictions arrive quickly; sharp edges and fine textures arrive extremely slowly or never, because the high-frequency components of the target function require the weights to cooperate in a finely tuned way the optimiser does not naturally reach. The gap is not aesthetic — our real run measured it at 11 dB (23.25 dB without encoding vs. 34.31 dB with it), a factor of roughly 14x in mean-squared error. Fourier features lift the input into a high-dimensional sinusoidal space: the same spatial coordinate x is presented simultaneously at many octaves, from coarse (2⁰) to fine (2^L). The MLP then only needs to linearly combine these precomputed high-frequency signals — a task that gradient descent handles easily. Spectral bias is not defeated by making the MLP bigger; it is defeated by changing what the MLP sees. Same primitive elsewhere: transformer positional encodings use the same Fourier construction — sinusoids at multiple frequencies encode position so that the attention mechanism can distinguish near from far tokens. The spectral-bias insight is the shared root.

Differentiable rendering closes the supervision loop

L(θ) = Σ_rays ‖C_θ(r) − C_gt(r)‖², ∂L/∂θ = Σ_rays (C_θ − C_gt) · ∂C_θ/∂θ
Training is a loop with no special-purpose supervision: sample a ray through a known pixel, evaluate the volume-rendering integral using the current field F_θ, compare the rendered colour C_θ(r) to the ground-truth pixel C_gt(r), and backpropagate through the entire chain — compositing, sampling, and the MLP — to update θ. No depth labels, no segmentation masks, no point clouds. The field is incentivised to place high density exactly where surfaces must be (otherwise silhouettes do not match) and to assign the right colour at each view direction (otherwise textures do not match). Because density and colour co-optimise through the same loss, a NeRF that fits the training views almost always fits the held-out geometry too — the 3D constraint is implicit in the multi-view consistency of the image collection. The honest cost: this supervision loop requires hundreds of MLP queries per pixel per training step, making NeRF slow. Gaussian Splatting later beat it on speed by making each primitive a rasterisable blob rather than a per-ray integral, trading the elegant continuous formulation for real-time throughput.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Every variant of NeRF is a different answer to the same two questions: what replaces the MLP, and how do you sample the ray more efficiently? The original paper used a deep fully-connected MLP queried at 64–128 points per ray — hours of GPU time per scene. Instant-NGP replaced most of the MLP with a multi-resolution hash grid: a lookup table of learned feature vectors at each resolution level, combined by a tiny MLP; training drops from hours to seconds. 3D Gaussian Splatting abandons volume rendering entirely, storing the scene as a cloud of 3D Gaussians projected onto the image plane by rasterisation — orders of magnitude faster but losing the clean continuous-field interpretation. TensoRF and K-Planes factor the 3D volume into products of 2D planes, compressing storage while keeping differentiability. Dynamic NeRFs add a time coordinate to the field or separate a deformation field from a canonical field, enabling video reconstruction. All share the core principle: scene-as-function, rendering-as-integral, photos-as-supervision.

A worked numeric example

If a ray samples densities [0.2,0.5] and colors [1.0,0.4], each sample contributes color weighted by accumulated transmittance. Raising early density both adds its own color and blocks later color. NeRF learns geometry because opacity controls what light reaches the camera.

1The original job, and the cost fight

Explicit surface meshes trade memory for rendering speed: Delaunay tetrahedra or triangle soups fix geometry upfront, eliminating the need to query a coordinate network at dense sample points. Conversely, neural fields amortize geometric detail across learnable weights via the volume-rendering integral, paying a query cost per ray but storing the entire scene in a single compact MLP.

The through-line. NeRF is built on one equation and one insight. The equation is the volume-rendering integral — a weighted sum of colour along a ray that is differentiable in the scene parameters. The insight is that if you put that equation inside the loss, photographs become supervisors for 3D structure. The mathematical obstacles were spectral bias (solved by Fourier lifting) and per-ray query cost (still an open trade-off). What makes NeRF a landmark is not the MLP — it is the move of making rendering differentiable end-to-end, the same move that Gaussian Splatting, differentiable rasterisers, and physics-based inverse rendering all repeat. The scene-as-function idea, compression into weight space rather than voxel grids, and the spectral-bias fix by Fourier encoding each recur throughout the field: in transformer positional encodings, in neural radiance caching, in implicit neural representations of audio and medical volumes. The deep principle is differentiable rendering — once you can backpropagate through the image-formation process, the physical world becomes a loss function.

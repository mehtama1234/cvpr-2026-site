1 · Distilling a slow generator into a fast one: one-step and few-step synthesis

Large diffusion models produce high-quality images and videos but require many sequential denoising steps—slow for real-time use. Knowledge distillation transfers what a multi-step teacher knows into a much faster student: instead of learning from one-hot labels, the student sees the teacher's soft outputs over its entire generation path, capturing which classes are close competitors and how confident to be at each step. This dark knowledge teaches the student in ways labels alone cannot. These papers tackle one frontier: compressing image and video generators from dozens of steps down to one or a few, either by preserving weight geometry during optimization, routing information along the generation flow, learning to self-evaluate without a teacher, or intelligently matching transition probabilities across the compressed trajectory.
CVPR'26WaDi: Weight Direction-aware Distillation for One-step Image Synthesis
Problem
Diffusion models need many denoising steps to produce good images. Squeezing a multi-step model into a one-step generator directly causes mode collapse—the student forgets diverse samples and quality drops.
Approach
WaDi distills by watching not just the student's output features but also the geometric direction of weight updates during training. By enforcing that weight parameters move in the right direction (not just toward the right place), the distillation process becomes more stable and the student learns to produce both good and diverse images.
Why it matters
Standard distillation only cares about matching final outputs; WaDi adds geometric constraints on the learning trajectory itself, giving the student a richer signal about how the teacher organizes its parameters. This extra information helps the student compress dozens of steps into one without losing the diversity that made the teacher strong.
CVPR'26RecTok: Reconstruction Distillation along Rectified Flow
Problem
Visual tokenizers—learned representations that feed into diffusion models—face a hard trade-off: high-dimensional latent spaces give better reconstruction and semantic richness but break the distillation process, while low-dimensional spaces stay stable but lose information.
Approach
RecTok distills vision foundation model knowledge directly into the entire denoising trajectory (each noisy intermediate state), not just the final clean image. It adds a semantic decoder at each flow step supervised by foundation model features, and reconstructs masked regions to align with the full-image representation.
Why it matters
By routing semantic information along every step of the generation path—rather than only at the end—RecTok teaches the student tokenizer what features matter throughout the diffusion process. This lets the student maintain high-dimensional, semantically rich representations without instability, compressing the need for foundation model supervision at inference time.
CVPR'26Self-Evaluation Unlocks Any-Step Text-to-Image Generation
Problem
Flow matching models learn from local velocity signals (short-range guidance), requiring many steps. Distillation methods need a pretrained teacher, so you cannot train a few-step model from scratch.
Approach
The student model learns in two ways: from real data via local velocity supervision, and through self-evaluation. At each training step, the student generates samples and scores them using its own current estimates, treating itself as a dynamic teacher. This combines local data-driven learning with global distribution matching without needing an external teacher.
Why it matters
Self-teaching eliminates the pretrained-teacher bottleneck. The student learns from data labels (local) and simultaneously refines its generation quality by evaluating itself (global), compressing the multi-step generation path into flexible any-step inference. The model naturally improves across few-step, medium-step, and long-step budgets monotonically.
CVPR'26Transition Matching Distillation for Fast Video Generation
Problem
Video diffusion models produce high-fidelity results but require hundreds of sampling steps, making real-time generation infeasible. Simple distillation struggles to preserve both motion coherence and visual quality in the compressed video sequence.
Approach
Transition Matching Distillation (TMD) uses a decoupled student architecture: a main backbone extracts semantic features at each transition, and a recurrent flow head refines details within each step. Training occurs in two stages—first matching the multi-step denoising trajectory via flow matching, then matching output distributions with the flow head rolling out to add fine details.
Why it matters
By splitting semantic extraction from fine refinement, TMD teaches the student to compress the teacher's many-step trajectory into a few key semantic transitions, then let a lightweight recurrent module add polish. This hierarchical distillation preserves motion and fidelity in far fewer steps than the teacher requires.

2 · Dataset distillation: squeezing a whole dataset into a few synthetic images

Dataset distillation tackles one of training's hardest inverse problems: how do you squeeze the knowledge locked inside thousands of images into a tiny synthetic set that trains a model almost as well as the full dataset? Rather than distilling knowledge from a teacher network into a student, distillation here compresses an entire training set—the patterns, distributions, and gradients—into a handful of carefully optimized proxy images. Recent work tackles distribution shifts introduced by diffusion-based generation, handles multimodal vision-language alignment in synthetic pairs, applies retrieval-augmented generation to filter high-quality distilled samples, and uses orthogonal gradient matching to avoid expensive bilevel optimization. The theme unifies these approaches as rigorous attempts to extract a dataset's essential learning signal into compact form.

CVPR'26Multimodal Distribution Matching for Vision-Language Dataset Distillation
Problem

Vision-language dataset distillation requires matching complex multimodal distributions where visual and textual modalities are interdependent. Existing methods focus on single modalities, failing to capture the joint structure that makes vision-language datasets unique.

Approach

The method proposes multimodal distribution matching that simultaneously captures visual and linguistic distributions in the distilled synthetic pairs. Contrastive objectives maintain multimodal alignment between the synthetic dataset and the original vision-language relationships.

Why it matters

By matching multimodal distributions, the synthetic dataset preserves not just visual patterns or text independently, but the intricate coupling between image and caption—the core knowledge that vision-language models learn from. This is dataset distillation at the intersection of sight and language.

CVPR'26Mitigating The Distribution Shift of Diffusion-based Dataset Distillation
Problem

Diffusion-based dataset distillation suffers from two critical distribution shifts: at training time, the diffusion model replicates the original distribution rather than learning a simplified manifold; at sampling time, low-capacity estimation causes diversity collapse and drift away from the target distribution.

Approach

A two-stage approach addresses each shift: Restricted Score Matching applies L1 sparsity during training to learn semantically sparse manifolds; Collaborative Guided Sampling uses Determinantal Point Process loss and distribution matching during sampling to prevent diversity collapse and correct drift.

Why it matters

By decoupling and targeting training-time and sampling-time shifts separately, the method enables diffusion models to generate synthetic datasets that genuinely compress the original distribution's essence—the semantic signal without the noise—into a tiny set of images that train models nearly as well.

CVPR'26UniRain: Unified Image Deraining with RAG-based Dataset Distillation and Multi-objective Reweighted Optimization
Problem

Real-world rainy scenes exhibit multiple degradation patterns simultaneously (rain streaks, raindrops, daytime and nighttime conditions), yet public deraining datasets are fragmented across tens of thousands of uneven-quality pairs. Merging them directly causes training imbalance and forces models to favor easier degradations.

Approach

UniRain distills the merged deraining dataset using retrieval-augmented generation: a hierarchical retrieval pipeline (semantic similarity, visual features, structure) selects relevant references, and an ensemble of three vision-language models votes on data quality, reducing the dataset to ~2.6% of its original size (52,869 high-quality pairs). Multi-objective optimization dynamically reweights loss contributions across rain types.

Why it matters

By distilling a large, noisy deraining dataset into a curated synthetic set via language-guided retrieval and quality voting, the model captures the essential patterns of each rain degradation type—the knowledge needed to handle diverse real-world rain—without the imbalance and noise of the merged original.

CVPR'26Beyond Soft Label: Dataset Distillation via Orthogonal Gradient Matching
Problem

Existing gradient-matching dataset distillation methods rely on gradient magnitude and scale rather than direction, producing over-smoothed synthetic data and instability from expensive bilevel optimization (backpropagating through gradient computation).

Approach

Orthogonal Gradient Matching isolates gradient direction via SVD: decompose real-data gradients into singular vectors and singular values, set all singular values to 1 to remove scale, and minimize the MSE between synthetic and real singular vectors. A forward-pass pseudo-gradient avoids costly backpropagation through the gradient computation.

Why it matters

Gradient direction encodes the intrinsic optimization geometry—the true learning trajectory. By matching singular vectors alone, the synthetic dataset captures the essential optimization signal and learning geometry of the original data without noisy scale information, enabling stable distillation at scale (ImageNet-1K).

3 · Distilling across time: knowledge distillation against forgetting

When models learn continuously—absorbing new tasks, new data, new domains—they face a fundamental problem: the new washes away the old. This is catastrophic forgetting, and distillation is a powerful shield. By treating the model's own past outputs as a teacher, and the updated model as the student, distillation anchors what the model already knew, preventing old skills from erasing. These four papers show distillation deployed across vision's hardest continual-learning problems: dense-prediction tasks with heterogeneous outputs, incremental object detection, person re-identification across new identities, and even the reverse problem—deliberately distilling knowledge out of vision-language models to erase sensitive content. Each grounds the same primitive—soft targets carrying knowledge forward—in radically different settings.
CVPR'26HAD: Heterogeneity-Aware Distillation for Lifelong Heterogeneous Learning
Problem
Lifelong learning typically assumes homogeneous tasks (e.g., a stream of classification problems). But real scenarios demand learning across heterogeneous task structures—semantic segmentation, depth estimation, pose estimation, object detection—all producing outputs with different spatial and semantic organizations. The learner must retain knowledge from all of them without access to past task data.
Approach
HAD distills the model's own past outputs forward using self-distillation during each new task phase. Two complementary losses preserve heterogeneous knowledge: a distribution-balanced distillation loss combats the global imbalance of predictions across diverse output spaces, and a salience-guided loss focuses learning on informative edge pixels (extracted via Sobel), ensuring that structural knowledge in dense predictions carries through.
Why it matters
By anchoring the model's heterogeneous predictions—its learned representations of different task structures—through soft targets, HAD prevents the new task from overwriting old geometric and semantic knowledge. Distillation preserves the full spectrum of prior learning even as the model adapts to fundamentally different output distributions.
CVPR'26Incremental Object Detection via Future-Aware Decoupled Cross-Head Distillation
Problem
Incremental object detection must learn new classes while preserving detection of old classes, all without replay memory. Conflicts arise between supervising new classes and distilling old ones, and the detection head becomes tightly coupled to the backbone, so head-specific biases contaminate the shared features, amplifying forgetting.
Approach
FaCHD decouples the detection head from the backbone and uses cross-head distillation: ROI features pass through both a frozen historical teacher head and an intermediate teacher head, transferring knowledge from both temporal perspectives. A Prototype Semantic Drift Compensation module recalibrates old-class prototypes at multiple granularities to correct the semantic drift caused by the new task, restoring accuracy of forgotten knowledge.
Why it matters
By distilling through multiple detection heads across time—past and near-present—the model anchors old knowledge while mitigating prediction conflicts between new and old classes. Decoupling ensures features remain generalizable, and prototype recalibration ensures that distilled old-class knowledge reflects the model's true learned prototypes, not corrupted versions.
CVPR'26Dynamic Magic: Unleashing Restricted Knowledge for Lifelong Person Re-Identification
Problem
Person re-identification systems must adapt continuously to new identities and visual domains without forgetting previously learned people. Class imbalance, limited labeled data for new domains, and catastrophic forgetting when identities shift make lifelong ReID a fundamentally hard continual-learning problem.
Approach
Dynamic Magic harnesses restricted auxiliary knowledge—weakly-labeled or unlabeled data—through dynamic knowledge distillation, enabling the model to adapt efficiently without replaying old identity data. The dynamic distillation framework adjusts the distillation schedule and intensity as new classes arrive, preserving the model's internal representation of old identities while incorporating new appearance variations.
Why it matters
By distilling from the model's own knowledge of old identities into a new model adapted to new identities, Dynamic Magic keeps the learned identity features stable and transferable. The use of weak auxiliary data reduces label requirements while distillation ensures the learner anchors what it already knew about previous people, preventing identity confusion.
CVPR'26VL-Eraser: Vacuum Distillation for Machine Unlearning in Vision-Language Models
Problem
Vision-language models must sometimes forget sensitive data (faces, copyrighted content) to respect privacy rights or regulatory mandates. Traditional reverse-training unlearning methods degrade model performance and, critically, disrupt cross-modal alignment between vision and language encoders, leaving knowledge residual in the language model and risking incomplete forgetting.
Approach
VL-Eraser reformulates unlearning as distillation-then-deletion: First, vacuum distillation isolates undesired knowledge into low-rank LoRA adapters constrained to a vacuum space (the left null space orthogonal to retain knowledge), preserving cross-modal alignment by design. Then, arithmetic deletion simply subtracts these LoRA parameters from the base model, removing undesired knowledge while the orthogonality constraint guarantees retained knowledge survives.
Why it matters
Unlike traditional reverse-training, VL-Eraser uses distillation not to preserve knowledge but to isolate and concentrate undesired knowledge into a separable component, then removes it cleanly. The vacuum space constraint ensures distillation never touches what should be retained, protecting the vision-language alignment and guaranteeing that unlearning is complete and irreversible.

4 · The privileged teacher: sim-to-real teacher-student distillation for control

Knowledge distillation transfers a capable teacher's understanding into a deployable student. In robotics, the canonical pattern is privileged-teacher distillation: a teacher trained in simulation or with sensors the real robot cannot access learns a capability; a student trained only from onboard observations distills that knowledge into a deployable policy. The papers below show four variants: a humanoid learning multiple command modes from full-body motion imitation; a biped reading terrain from cameras while mimicking specialists trained on each surface; a small manipulator refined from a foundation model during live RL; and a sensor-limited learner guided through hard moments by an all-seeing teacher. In each case, the teacher sees or knows what the student cannot, and distillation is how the student inherits that advantage.
ROBOTICS'25HOVER: Versatile Neural Whole-Body Controller for Humanoid Robots
Problem
A humanoid robot performing walking, manipulation, and tabletop tasks faces a problem: each task mode requires different movement commands (root velocity, joint positions, or individual angles), forcing separate controllers.
Approach
HOVER trains a teacher policy on a rich library of full-body motions, then distills that knowledge into a single student policy that learns to accept all command types at once through a shared distillation step, letting the student interpret every command mode through the same underlying motion knowledge.
Why it matters
Teacher-student distillation unifies diverse command interfaces by anchoring them in the teacher's learned motion patterns, so the robot runs one policy instead of retraining for each task mode.
ROBOTICS'25VMTS: Vision-Assisted Teacher-Student Reinforcement Learning for Multi-Terrain Locomotion in Bipedal Robots
Problem
A bipedal robot needs to walk on multiple surfaces (stairs, gravel, slopes) using only camera images, but it is unclear how to fuse visual input with terrain-specific control without losing the ability to handle each surface.
Approach
Specialist teachers are trained first—one per terrain type with privileged knowledge of the surface. A student policy then learns to read camera images and match the right specialist's behavior on each terrain, guided by a loss that pushes the student to mimic the specialist's internal representations rather than just copy its actions.
Why it matters
Distilling internal representations from multiple specialists gives the student a richer learning signal, enabling cross-terrain generalization with only camera input where the teacher sees the actual ground.
ROBOTICS'25Refined Policy Distillation: From VLA Generalists to RL Experts
Problem
Large vision-language-action foundation models generalize across many tasks but fail too often on specific objectives and are brittle to camera angle and setup changes.
Approach
A large generalist VLA model acts as a teacher guiding a smaller student model during reinforcement learning; the teacher initializes sensible exploration, and the student learns through trial and error to exceed the teacher's performance on the specific task.
Why it matters
The generalist teacher transfers its broad task understanding into an expert student without requiring either model to see privileged information—the student learns real task expertise by refining the teacher's initial guidance through RL.
ROBOTICS'25Distilling Realizable Students from Unrealizable Teachers
Problem
A robot with only onboard sensors cannot simply copy a teacher policy that uses privileged information (perfect state, terrain, future), because the student cannot reproduce what the teacher can do.
Approach
Two distillation strategies close the gap: querying the all-seeing teacher for corrections only at moments the student is most likely to fail, and starting the student's learning from the specific situations where the teacher would recover from mistakes.
Why it matters
Focusing distillation on high-risk moments teaches the sensor-limited student to handle hard cases without requiring it to match the teacher everywhere—the student inherits the teacher's recovery knowledge for the situations it actually encounters.

5 · Cross-modal and real-time: distilling perception to run onboard

Knowledge distillation transfers the accuracy of large, slow perceptual models into smaller, faster ones that can run on-board a robot. In robotics, this has two key applications: squeezing heavy perception models into real-time inference on resource-constrained platforms, and carrying knowledge across sensor modalities so a model trained on one camera type (thermal, RGB, LiDAR) can teach a model for a different one. The papers here show distillation working across the thermal-to-RGB boundary for stereo depth, camera-to-LiDAR for 3D segmentation, camera-to-overhead for motion detection, and label-space unification across camera datasets.
ROBOTICS'25ThermoStereoRT: Thermal Stereo Matching in Real Time via Knowledge Distillation and Attention-Based Refinement
Problem
Estimating depth from two thermal cameras in real time is too slow for small computers, and there is not enough labeled thermal data to train large models directly.
Approach
A large trained network (the teacher) passes its learned representations to a small fast network (the student) through knowledge distillation, and attention modules help the student sharpen its depth estimates before output.
Why it matters
The student network inherits the accuracy of the large thermal model without its compute cost, enabling real-time depth sensing on resource-constrained platforms while attention refinement recovers fine depth detail that small networks would otherwise miss.
ROBOTICS'25Domain Adaptation-Based Crossmodal Knowledge Distillation for 3D Semantic Segmentation
Problem
Labeling 3D laser scan data to teach robots what objects they are seeing is very time-consuming and expensive.
Approach
They use a model already trained on ordinary 2D camera images to teach a 3D laser scan model, without needing any 3D labels. The 2D model's outputs are used as targets for the 3D model, and a special convolution layer filters out features that only make sense for one sensor type so the two models can align.
Why it matters
Because 2D images and 3D scans of the same scene are captured together, the 2D model's rich learned knowledge transfers to the 3D model directly—no manual 3D labeling needed—demonstrating how distillation bridges camera and LiDAR modalities.
ROBOTICS'25KDMOS:Knowledge Distillation for Motion Segmentation
Problem
Detecting which objects are moving in a laser scan in real time is hard because accurate models are too slow and fast models miss too many moving objects.
Approach
A large, accurate 3D model (teacher) trains a smaller, faster model that works on flat overhead images (student) by passing its predictions as extra training signal; separate training strategies handle the rare moving-object class and the common static class differently to avoid missing either.
Why it matters
The smaller model learns from the more accurate model's outputs rather than only raw labels, achieving real-time motion segmentation on overhead representations while preserving the 3D teacher's fine distinctions between moving and static points.
ROBOTICS'25Knowledge Distillation for Semantic Segmentation: A Label Space Unification Approach
Problem
Different camera datasets for self-driving cars each use their own set of object categories, so you cannot simply combine them to train a bigger, more accurate model.
Approach
A teacher model trained on one dataset automatically labels other datasets by mapping their category names to the teacher's categories. A student model then trains on all this combined data, learning from the teacher's outputs rather than inconsistent original labels (knowledge distillation).
Why it matters
Translating every dataset into the same label space through distillation lets the student learn from diverse camera data unified under one semantic scheme, achieving better performance than single-dataset training.

6 · Distilling the giants: foundation and descriptor knowledge onto the robot

Knowledge distillation—compressing a large, slow model's learned representations into a small, deployable student—has become essential for embedding foundation models and descriptor networks directly onto real robots. In 2025 robotics, distillation enables precise manipulation (dexterous grasping from few examples), real-time perception (6D pose estimation, dense visual understanding), and interpretable multi-agent coordination. Foundation models pre-trained on web-scale vision and language capture powerful semantic features; descriptors from dedicated architectures encode spatial geometry and appearance for alignment and tracking. Yet these giants run at the speed of seconds on a GPU. Distillation transfers that knowledge into lightweight modules that run at 30+ Hz on robot hardware, making foundation-scale understanding practical for pick-and-place, navigation, and transparent decision-making without sacrificing the semantic richness that makes robots generalizable.
ROBOTICS'25LensDFF: Language-enhanced Sparse Feature Distillation for Efficient Few-Shot Dexterous Manipulation
Problem
Teaching a dexterous robot hand to grasp new objects from a few examples demands expensive 3D scene reconstruction (e.g., NeRF) every episode, making real-time grasping on-robot infeasible.
Approach
LensDFF distills features from a large pretrained vision model (the teacher) onto a sparse point-cloud representation (the student). Language descriptions guide feature alignment across views, and a small set of learned grasp motion patterns enable generalization without full scene reconstruction.
Why it matters
By projecting foundation-model features directly onto geometry instead of reconstructing the full scene, the system cuts computation by orders of magnitude while language grounding ensures the distilled features transfer to unseen object categories.
ROBOTICS'25Distilling 3D distinctive local descriptors for 6D pose estimation
Problem
The GeDi method can estimate 6D object pose from a single image without prior training on that object, but its slow per-pixel descriptor computation makes real-time industrial use impossible.
Approach
A lightweight student network is trained to replicate GeDi's descriptor behavior via knowledge distillation. A robust training loss handles cases where the teacher's signal is unreliable, and the student is trained on large pose-estimation datasets including partial occlusions.
Why it matters
The distilled model retains GeDi's zero-shot capability while running 10–100x faster, enabling real-time 6D pose tracking on robot manipulators for grasping and assembly tasks.
ROBOTICS'25Efficient Prediction of Dense Visual Embeddings via Distillation and RGB-D Transformers
Problem
Robots need real-time dense scene understanding to answer open-ended natural-language queries about their environment, but large vision-language models run too slowly on onboard hardware.
Approach
DVEFormer, a compact RGB-D Transformer, is trained by distilling knowledge from a large foundational embedding model (the teacher). Every pixel receives a dense vector encoding its semantic meaning, enabling zero-shot pixel-level retrieval via text queries.
Why it matters
Distillation from the foundation model gives DVEFormer rich semantic understanding while keeping inference fast enough for real-time navigation and scene-understanding tasks on robot platforms with limited compute.
ROBOTICS'25Towards Transparent Multi-Agent Autonomous Systems Through Principled Multi-Source Knowledge Distillation
Problem
Deep reinforcement learning policies for multi-robot coordination are black boxes; engineers cannot inspect why robots make each decision, hampering debugging, verification, and operator trust.
Approach
Recordings of learned multi-robot policies are distilled into human-readable behavior trees by extracting and simplifying the logical rules via Boolean algebra. This converts opaque neural policies into explicit if-then rule structures.
Why it matters
Distilling learned coordination into symbolic behavior trees makes multi-robot policies interpretable and verifiable without sacrificing the learned expressiveness, enabling safe deployment in shared human–robot environments.

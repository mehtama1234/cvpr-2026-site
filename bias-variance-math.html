Bias, variance & double descent — why it works, mathematically

A learner can stumble in two opposite ways: by staying too rigid and missing the real pattern, or by bouncing around and memorizing noise. The first suffers from rigidity, the second from jitter. This is the hidden equation governing how many adjustable numbers your learner actually needs — and the surprising discovery that sometimes more numbers, beyond some point, suddenly helps again.

The problem, as a quantity

Imagine you are teaching a learner to predict house prices from square footage. If your learner is too simple — say, just a single straight line through the data — it will never quite hit the real prices, because real prices don't follow a perfect line. But if you make your learner more complex, letting it bend and twist to touch every single training example, it will memorize the noise in the training set and then fail on new houses it has never seen. There is no clear answer to where the sweet spot is.

Even stranger: in recent years, researchers discovered that if you make your learner wildly complex — far more flexible than traditional wisdom said was safe — something unexpected happens. Instead of getting worse and worse as it memorizes junk, the error eventually starts to improve again. This goes against everything the old rules said should happen. Understanding why this happens requires untangling two separate sources of error, and seeing that they move in opposite directions as your learner gets more flexible.

The core ideas — and why they work

Two ways to be wrong: too rigid, or too jumpy

error ≈ bias² + variance + unavoidable noise
Your learner can fail in two distinct ways. The first is rigidity: if it is not flexible enough, it cannot bend to match the true pattern in the world, so it will make a systematic error no matter how much training data you give it. This systematic error is called bias, and it depends only on how limited your learner is, not on luck or randomness. The second is jitter: if your learner is very flexible, it can fit not just the true pattern but also the random noise that appears in any real dataset. On new data without that particular noise, the learner gives different answers — wildly different answers. This scatter in predictions is called variance. The total error you see is the sum of these two sources, plus an irreducible floor of noise baked into the world itself.

The classic trade-off: more flexibility swaps one for the other

↑ flexibility ⇒ ↓ bias, ↑ variance
As your learner becomes more flexible — more able to bend its internal numbers to match the training data — bias shrinks because it can now capture more of the real pattern. But variance grows, because that same flexibility means it also captures more of the random noise in your particular training set. These two move in opposite directions. When your learner is very rigid, bias is huge and variance is tiny. When your learner is very flexible, bias is tiny and variance is huge. In the middle, the total error forms a U-shape: it falls as you add flexibility (bias dropping faster than variance rises), hits a bottom, then rises again as flexibility keeps growing (variance rising faster than bias dropping).

Overfitting is fitting the noise instead of the signal

training error ↓ while test error ↑
Overfitting is the moment when your learner stops learning the true pattern and starts memorizing noise instead. You can see this happen by comparing two error numbers: the error on the training data you showed your learner, and the error on brand-new test data it has never seen. While your learner is learning the true pattern, both errors drop together. But once your learner becomes flexible enough to also memorize the noise in the training set, something breaks. Training error keeps falling — it gets lower and lower because the learner is now fitting the noise — but test error starts to rise, because the noise it memorized is not in the test data.

The modern surprise: past the tipping point, error falls again

test error: down, up, then down again as flexibility grows (double descent)
The surprise discovery is that this U-shaped error curve does not keep rising forever. If you keep adding flexibility — giving your learner way more adjustable numbers than traditional statistics said was wise — the error eventually stops rising and starts falling again. The reason is subtle: with enough flexibility and enough data, the learner can fit all the true patterns plus have so much leftover flexibility that the extra adjustable numbers start to average out the noise rather than memorizing it. This second descent is called double descent because the error goes down, then up, then down again as you steadily make your learner more flexible.

The approaches, as variations on the math

The same fundamental trade-off between rigidity and jitter holds across all learners, but you can move the danger zones by adjusting these dials: model size (how many adjustable numbers), training data (more data reduces variance because your learner has less room to memorize noise), regularization (a penalty on large numbers that forces the learner to stay simpler), and early stopping (pause training before the learner memorizes noise). The thread never changes: there is always a rigidity-versus-jitter trade-off, and modern learning has shown that you can sometimes escape the worst part of it by going to the extreme of very large models with very large datasets.

1Bias-Variance Decomposition
Error = (Bias)² + Variance + Noise
Every prediction error breaks into three parts: how far your model's average guess sits from the truth (Bias squared), how much your guess bounces around when trained on different data (Variance), and the random noise baked into the data itself (Noise). This equation shows why you can't eliminate both bias and variance at once—one shrinks while the other grows as your model gets more flexible.
2Regularization & Trade-offs
Loss = Data-fit + λ × Complexity, where λ ∈ [0, ∞)
Regularization adds a penalty term to your training goal that grows with model complexity. The coefficient lambda λ is the knob: at λ = 0 the model fits data perfectly (high variance, low bias); as λ increases you force the model to stay simple (low variance, high bias). Finding the right lambda balances the two errors in the classical regime before overfitting takes over.
3Model Capacity & the U-Curve
Test Error ~ (1/n) × (# parameters)² − (# parameters)/(n) for large n
As you add more parameters—more knobs for the model to turn—test error drops at first because the model learns the true signal. But past a point (called the interpolation threshold), error rises sharply as the model starts fitting random noise in the data. The U-shaped curve appears because as parameters grow, variance explodes much faster than bias shrinks.
4Benign Overfitting & Double Descent
lim(n→∞) [higher than U-curve minimum] then lim(# params→∞) [error drops again]
Once you add enough parameters to perfectly memorize training data, something surprising happens: error doesn't stay high—it falls again as parameters grow even larger. This is because the high-dimensional geometry of overparameterized models (many more dimensions than data points) acts like automatic regularization: redundant parameters average out noise instead of locking it in. The mechanism depends on implicit bias from how the model learns, not explicit penalties.

What breaks without this

What goes wrong. Without the bias-variance view, you can mistake training success for real success. A model can miss the main pattern because it is too stiff, or chase noise because it is too flexible.

Why it goes wrong. Data is only a sample of the world. Some errors come from a model that cannot bend enough. Other errors come from a model that bends to every accident in the sample.

What this idea gives you. Bias and variance name the two ways a model can fail. The point is not to make the training error smallest; it is to choose a shape that survives new data.

The through-line. The core problem is predicting how well your learner will work on new data, given only that it trained on old data. You cannot just make it arbitrarily complex, because at some point it will memorize noise and fail. But you also cannot make it too simple, or it will miss the pattern entirely. For decades, the answer seemed to be: stop somewhere in the middle, at the bottom of the U-shaped error curve. The modern discovery is that the U-shape has a surprising second act — if you go far enough into the regime of enormous flexibility, error falls again. Two plain ideas carry it: error splits into two sources that move in opposite directions as you change flexibility, and fitting noise in training data is not the end of the story if you have so much flexibility that the noise-fitting gets averaged out by all the other unused flexibility.

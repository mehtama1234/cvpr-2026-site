Energy-based & score models — why it works, mathematically

Give every possible picture a 'badness' score — low means it looks real. Turning those scores into true probabilities needs an impossible grand total over all pictures. The trick is that you never actually need it: work with the slope of the badness landscape and the impossible total cancels out.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

An energy model gives each possible state a score. Good states get low energy; bad states get high energy. Instead of directly predicting an answer, the model learns a landscape where better answers sit downhill.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology fits naturally because many shapes are better described by allowed and forbidden states. A connected vessel tree, a closed surface, or a valid mesh should sit in a low-energy region; broken or self-crossing versions should sit higher.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

Energy ideas appear in physics, chemistry, optimization, image repair, planning, and anomaly detection. They are useful when we can judge whether a state is plausible even if generating it directly is hard.

Why Energy-based & score models is worth learning

It matters because many problems are easier to check than to build. Energy gives the model a way to repair by moving from bad states toward better ones.

The key turn — the principle at work

The stuck point. Sometimes it is easier to score whether something looks compatible than to directly produce it or normalize its full probability.

The mathematical move. The principle is energy as preference. An energy model assigns low scores to good states and high scores to bad ones. Learning shapes the energy landscape so data sits in low valleys.

What changes. Modeling stops requiring an explicit normalized probability at every point. The hard part moves to sampling: finding low-energy states can take a search or a learned denoising process.

The problem, as a quantity

Here is the most flexible possible way to model data: hand every possible picture a single number — call it a badness score, low for pictures that look real and high for ones that look wrong. Any scoring function at all is allowed, so this can express any pattern you like.

The problem shows up when you try to turn those scores into genuine probabilities. To do that you must divide every score by the grand total of the scores over every possible picture — and in a space of million-number images that total is astronomically large and flatly uncomputable. It seems to block everything: you cannot say how probable a picture is, cannot train by matching probabilities, cannot sample. The whole subject is a set of ways to get everything you want — generating, training, comparing — while never once computing that impossible grand total.

The core ideas — and why they work

Ask only which way is better, and the impossible total drops out

s(x) = ∇ₓ log p(x) = −∇ₓ E(x) [Z drops out: ∂/∂x log(1/Z) = 0]
Give every picture its badness score — low means realistic, high means wrong. To turn that into an actual probability you would divide by the grand total over all pictures, which you cannot compute.

But notice what you usually need: not the probability itself, only the direction that makes a picture more probable — which way to nudge it to lower its badness. And that direction depends only on how the badness changes from one nearby picture to the next, not on the grand total at all. The total is the same fixed number everywhere, so the instant you ask 'which way is downhill?' it contributes nothing and vanishes. This is the key that unlocks the rest: you can work entirely with the slope of the badness landscape — which way is more realistic, at every point — and never touch the impossible total.

Generate by rolling downhill, with a little jitter

x_{t+1} = x_t − (ε/2)·∇ₓ E(x_t) + √ε·z, z ~ N(0,I)
To make a new realistic picture, start from pure random noise and repeatedly step in the downhill direction the last idea gave you — toward lower badness, more realistic.

But pure downhill rolling has a flaw: every start would slide into the single lowest point, so you would get the same one picture every time. The fix is to add a small random jitter at each step. The jitter keeps the points restless, so instead of all collapsing onto the one deepest spot they spread out and settle across all the low-badness regions, spending time in each in proportion to how realistic it is. Roll downhill, jitter, repeat — and the pictures you end up with are spread just like real data: realistic and varied rather than one endlessly-repeated 'best' picture.

Train by pushing real things down and made-up things up

∇θ L = E_data[∇θ E_θ(x)] − E_model[∇θ E_θ(x)] [raise energy on fantasy samples, lower on real ones]
How do you shape the badness landscape without the impossible total? By a tug-of-war. Take real data and push its badness down, carving valleys where real things live. Then let the model dream up its own pictures — using the downhill-with-jitter rolling above — and push their badness up, filling in any valley the model wrongly dug in a place where no real data actually is.

When the model's dreams finally look just like real data, the two pushes land in the same places and cancel, and the landscape stops changing: it now has valleys exactly where real data lives and nowhere else. Nothing here needs the grand total — only a comparison between real examples and the model's current best guesses. The model learns by being repeatedly shown the difference between reality and its own imagination.

Learn the downhill directions straight from data

J(θ) = E_data[½·‖s_θ(x)‖² + tr(∇ₓ s_θ(x))]
There is an even more direct route. Since all you ever need is the downhill direction at each point, why not train the model to output that direction and skip the badness scores entirely?

The obstacle is that you have no pile of true directions to imitate. A clever piece of calculus removes that obstacle: it rewrites the training goal into an equivalent form that uses only the data you actually have, plus how the model's own directions change from point to point — the unknown true directions drop out of the math. Minimising it teaches the model, at every point, which way real data would pull. This is exactly the same downhill direction that diffusion models learn, reached here from the badness-score side of the story rather than the noise-removal side.

Combining two models is just adding their scores

p₁(x) ∝ exp(−E₁(x)), p₂(x) ∝ exp(−E₂(x)) ⟹ p₁(x)·p₂(x) ∝ exp(−E₁(x) − E₂(x))
A quiet bonus of thinking in badness scores: to demand that a picture satisfy two conditions at once — say 'looks like a bird' and 'is red' — you simply add their two badness landscapes together.

A picture scores low on the combined landscape only if it scores low on both, so rolling downhill on the sum yields pictures that meet both demands. No retraining, no delicate juggling of probabilities — because badness scores just add where probabilities would have to be multiplied, and adding is easy. You can stack conditions like building blocks: train separate models for separate properties, then combine them on the fly by summing their landscapes.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Methods here differ mainly in how they dodge the impossible total.

How you generate. Roll downhill with jitter from noise, taking more steps for higher quality; faster variants shape the landscape so fewer steps suffice. How you train. The push-real-down, push-fantasy-up tug-of-war, or the calculus trick that learns the downhill directions straight from data without ever generating fantasies. Scores versus directions. Keep the full badness landscape (handy for combining conditions by addition) or model only its slope (handy for direct training and for sampling). What you combine. Because conditions add, separate models for separate properties can be mixed at will. The thread never changes: ask only which-way-is-better so the impossible total cancels, and add landscapes instead of multiplying probabilities.

A worked numeric example

If two states have energies E=[1,3], their unnormalized probabilities are [e^{-1},e^{-3}], so the lower-energy state is about 7.4× more likely. Langevin sampling follows -∇E downhill but adds noise so it can still explore.

1Energy-based generation and composition
E_combined(x) = E_1(x) + E_2(x) + ... Additive energies = product of distributions. Knob: compose arbitrary behaviors by summing learned energy potentials without retraining.
Energy models encode behaviors as unnormalized densities. The key insight is simplicity: if p(x) = exp(−E_1(x))/Z_1 and p'(x) = exp(−E_2(x))/Z_2, then p(x)p'(x) ∝ exp(−(E_1 + E_2)), so combined behaviors follow by adding energies. This avoids computing the impossible normalization constant — you never touch it — and lets you mix learned motion pieces, arm movements, and part-level rules together without retraining. The energy addition is exact and requires no tuning.
These papers show different ways to break down complex behaviors into parts and recombine them. Each focuses on how energy models let you add separate learned pieces together without retraining, whether for human motion, robot arms, or mixed skills.

2Score matching and distribution matching: learning the gradient of density
s(x,t) = ∇_x log p(x|t) learned via regression on noise-free score matching. Knob: partition the diffusion process into SNR subintervals; train local score experts per phase, chain backward via distribution matching.
Score matching learns the slope of log p directly without computing the normalization constant Z. By training on synthetic noise or matching intermediate distributions in diffusion, you get s(x) = −∇E(x), which you use either for random-walk sampling or direct integration. Breaking the diffusion into phases splits the problem into simpler pieces, each with cleaner training signals, which saves memory and stops few-step models from collapsing.
These papers train score networks — the gradients of log-probability — to handle diverse real-world tasks. They range from multi-reward image generation to fast video restoration and stylized animation.

3Energy for the open world: detecting the unknown
E(x) = distance to in-distribution manifold. High energy ⇒ anomalous. Knob: learn orthogonal energy subspaces (known vs. unknown), contrastive margins, and adaptive class-conditional calibration.
Standard classifiers output probabilities over seen classes and fail on unknowns. Energy-based out-of-distribution detection flips this: train an energy landscape on clean data, where low energy means familiar and high energy means new. By explicitly modeling unknown cases (separate subspace) and pushing unknown embeddings away from the known-class energy landscape using contrastive training, the model learns a representation that actively identifies anomalies rather than just tuning a threshold on classifier uncertainty.
These papers use energy landscapes to spot things the model has never seen during training. They differ in how they structure the energy space and calibrate it for real data.

4Diffusion bridges and inverse problems: the score in action
∇_x log p(x|y) ∝ ∇_x log p(y|x) + ∇_x log p(x). Score-based transport from degraded y to clean x via learned conditional score. Knob: adaptive residual-to-noise ratio, noise alignment, energy refinement at bridge endpoint.
Diffusion bridges pin the endpoint at the target domain (not noise), enabling restoration and inverse problems. The score field (the direction that log-probability rises or falls) guides transport: ∇log p(x|y) combines the likelihood of observations and the prior (the prior learned as a diffusion score). By tuning noise profiles to match input and training trends, and adding energy-based refinement near the endpoint, bridges achieve sharp restoration and constraint-respecting paths without slow random-walk sampling.
These papers solve restoration problems by building learned paths from degraded images to clean ones. They vary in how they design the noise schedule and refine the endpoint.

The through-line. The core obstacle is that scoring how likely a picture is seems to demand an impossible grand total over all pictures. The escape is to work with the slope of the badness landscape, where that total cancels: you can then generate by rolling downhill with a little jitter, train by pushing real pictures down and made-up ones up, or learn the downhill directions straight from data. Two plain ideas carry the whole subject: ask only which-way-is-more-realistic so the impossible total drops out, and combine several demands by adding their landscapes instead of multiplying probabilities.

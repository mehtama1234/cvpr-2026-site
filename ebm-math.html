Energy-based & score models — why it works, mathematically

Any function is a valid density if you never need to normalize it everywhere at once. The gradient of log-probability skips that global normalization, and everything else builds from there.

Plain-English big picture

What this is really about

An energy model gives each possible state a score. Good states get low energy; bad states get high energy. Instead of directly predicting an answer, the model learns a landscape where better answers sit downhill.

Where topology and shape enter

Topology fits naturally because many shapes are better described by allowed and forbidden states. A connected vessel tree, a closed surface, or a valid mesh should sit in a low-energy region; broken or self-crossing versions should sit higher.

Where the same idea appears elsewhere

Energy ideas appear in physics, chemistry, optimization, image repair, planning, and anomaly detection. They are useful when we can judge whether a state is plausible even if generating it directly is hard.

Why Energy-based & score models is worth learning

It matters because many problems are easier to check than to build. Energy gives the model a way to repair by moving from bad states toward better ones.

The problem, as a quantity

The goal is total flexibility in density estimation: pick any score you want for any point x, say high score means high probability, and the model learns whatever shape fits the data — bumpy, twisted, made of separate parts. The catch is normalization: to turn scores into a real probability, you divide by the sum of all scores everywhere: p(x) = exp(−E(x)) / Z, where Z = ∫ exp(−E(x)) dx adds up every point in the entire space. That sum is impossible to compute for any real model. Without Z you cannot measure how well the model fits the data, so learning by comparing to data is blocked. And without samples from p(x) you cannot even write down the training direction. The real blocker is one number — Z — that math says you need but computation says you cannot get. Every idea in this family is a different way around that one wall.

The core ideas — and why they work

The gradient kills Z (the score function)

s(x) = ∇ₓ log p(x) = −∇ₓ E(x) [Z drops out: ∂/∂x log(1/Z) = 0]
The normalization constant Z does not depend on x — it is the same everywhere. So when you take the slope of log p(x) = −E(x) − log Z in the x direction, the log Z part is flat and disappears: ∇ₓ log p = −∇ₓ E(x). This is not a trick — it falls straight out of basic calculus. The score s(x) (the direction that density rises or falls) captures everything you need about where probability rises or falls, and you can compute it from any energy function while completely ignoring the impossible sum. Why this matters: you never experience probability as an absolute number — only as differences between nearby spots, and the score is exactly those differences.

Sampling by noisy gradient descent (Langevin dynamics)

x_{t+1} = x_t − (ε/2)·∇ₓ E(x_t) + √ε·z, z ~ N(0,I) [stationary dist. ∝ exp(−E(x))]
Strip away the noise and you have gradient descent: it slides down to a peak and stops. Strip away the gradient and you have a random walk: it bounces around but ignores the landscape. Put them together: the gradient pulls the path toward high-probability spots while the injected random noise √ε·z stops it from getting stuck at one peak. The steady-state distribution (the long-run pattern that persists) of this noisy walk is exactly p(x) ∝ exp(−E(x)) — you can prove it under normal conditions on step size ε. You never need to know Z because the walk never asks for it; it only looks at ∇ₓ E at the current spot. The real cost: the walk is slow at mixing (exploring across different modes). To jump a deep valley between two peaks, the noise has to be strong enough to push back against the gradient — and strong noise means slow, scattered exploration. This slowness was why old energy models failed in high dimensions, and this is the exact problem that modern diffusion models solved.

Training without Z: contrastive divergence

∇θ L = E_data[∇θ E_θ(x)] − E_model[∇θ E_θ(x)] [raise energy on fantasy samples, lower on real ones]
The training direction is: lower the energy of real data and raise it on made-up samples from the model. Intuitively, the landscape should have valleys where your data lives and hills everywhere else. But getting made-up samples needs a slow random walk (MCMC), which is expensive. Contrastive divergence is a shortcut: run the walk for just a few steps starting from real data instead of running it all the way to settling down. This introduces a real error — the walk has not fully settled — but it makes training much faster. The tradeoff is harsh though: if the walk never leaves the data's neighborhood, the model never learns to push energy high in regions it has not seen — so the energy landscape in unseen places stays jumbled and sampling from scratch breaks. The failure mode to watch: if the model only sees its own predictions as negative examples and never escapes the data's immediate neighborhood, it collapses: the energy surface looks good locally but is meaningless beyond the region it trained in.

Score matching: learn the gradient directly

J(θ) = E_data[½·‖s_θ(x)‖² + tr(∇ₓ s_θ(x))] [integration by parts removes the need for p_data]
Instead of training an energy and hoping its gradient is right, learn the score function s_θ(x) ≈ ∇ₓ log p(x) straight from data. The simple loss would be E_data[‖s_θ(x) − ∇ₓ log p_data(x)‖²], but that needs knowing the true probability — circular. Rearranging the math via integration by parts cancels out the unknown probability: the loss becomes just a trace (sum of diagonal terms) of how much the score function curves plus a squared norm, both things you can compute from samples alone, no normalization constant and no density. The trace cost is steep in high dimensions (costs d backward passes), so instead people add Gaussian noise to x and train the score at that noise level — denoising score matching. At noise strength σ, the score you want is simply (x̃ − x)/σ²: the direction back toward clean data. Training a score network at many noise levels σ₁ > σ₂ > … > σ_T and chaining the steps is a diffusion model. The score IS what diffusion learns — ε_θ(x_t, t) ≈ −σ_t · ∇ₓ log p(x_t). Energy-based models and diffusion are the same thing at different noise levels; diffusion works so well because it learns the score at every noise level rather than just at zero noise.

Composition is free (product of energies)

p₁(x) ∝ exp(−E₁(x)), p₂(x) ∝ exp(−E₂(x)) ⟹ p₁(x)·p₂(x) ∝ exp(−E₁(x) − E₂(x))
Because unnormalized probability is an exponential, multiplying two distributions means adding their energies. Train a content energy and a style energy separately, then sample from both together by running Langevin on E₁ + E₂ — no retraining, no combined model, no blend weights to adjust. This is impossible with normalized models (flows, VAEs, language models) because multiplying two normalized densities breaks the normalization and computing the new constant is the same impossible integral. Unnormalized energy representation turns composition from hard to simple addition. The payoff in practice: classifier-guided or reward-guided generation. Add a classifier's probability as an energy term and Langevin drifts toward high-probability, high-reward corners. This guidance idea in diffusion models is exactly this primitive.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Classical contrastive training directly pushes the model toward data by lowering energy on real points and raising it on short random-walk samples. It works well in small spaces but suffers from slow mixing: if the random walk never escapes the data neighborhood, the energy landscape is only shaped right there. The result: a model that scores real data well but generates badly from random starting points.

Score matching and its denoising variant avoid random walks during training entirely — you train the gradient using only data and their noisy corruptions. Training is stable, scales to high dimensions, and avoids partition-function headaches. The cost: you only learn a function; to sample you still have to run a gradient-guided walk (Langevin or an integrator).

The diffusion connection fixes the mixing problem by learning the score at every noise level. At high noise the distribution is almost Gaussian and mixes instantly; at low noise it is close to data. Sampling goes high to low noise — coarse to fine — which acts like an annealed random walk that never gets trapped. The 'denoising network' in diffusion IS a score network: ε_θ(x_t,t) / σ_t ≈ −∇ₓ log p_t(x_t).

Energy for spotting unknowns and steering sampling uses the fact that high energy by design means low probability. A model trained on clean data assigns low energy there; out-of-distribution points get high energy automatically (no special training needed). For steering, add any differentiable goal — a classifier, a physics rule, a human preference — as extra energy and reshape sampling for free.

A worked numeric example

If two states have energies E=[1,3], their unnormalized probabilities are [e^{-1},e^{-3}], so the lower-energy state is about 7.4× more likely. Langevin sampling follows -∇E downhill but adds noise so it can still explore.

1Energy-based generation and composition
E_combined(x) = E_1(x) + E_2(x) + ... Additive energies = product of distributions. Knob: compose arbitrary behaviors by summing learned energy potentials without retraining.
Energy models encode behaviors as unnormalized densities. The key insight is simplicity: if p(x) = exp(−E_1(x))/Z_1 and p'(x) = exp(−E_2(x))/Z_2, then p(x)p'(x) ∝ exp(−(E_1 + E_2)), so combined behaviors follow by adding energies. This avoids computing the impossible normalization constant — you never touch it — and lets you mix learned motion pieces, arm movements, and part-level rules together without retraining. The energy addition is exact and requires no tuning.
These papers show different ways to break down complex behaviors into parts and recombine them. Each focuses on how energy models let you add separate learned pieces together without retraining, whether for human motion, robot arms, or mixed skills.

2Score matching and distribution matching: learning the gradient of density

Score matching learns the slope of log p directly without computing the normalization constant Z. By training on synthetic noise or matching intermediate distributions in diffusion, you get s(x) = −∇E(x), which you use either for random-walk sampling or direct integration. Breaking the diffusion into phases splits the problem into simpler pieces, each with cleaner training signals, which saves memory and stops few-step models from collapsing.

3Energy for the open world: detecting the unknown
E(x) = distance to in-distribution manifold. High energy ⇒ anomalous. Knob: learn orthogonal energy subspaces (known vs. unknown), contrastive margins, and adaptive class-conditional calibration.
Standard classifiers output probabilities over seen classes and fail on unknowns. Energy-based out-of-distribution detection flips this: train an energy landscape on clean data, where low energy means familiar and high energy means new. By explicitly modeling unknown cases (separate subspace) and pushing unknown embeddings away from the known-class energy landscape using contrastive training, the model learns a representation that actively identifies anomalies rather than just tuning a threshold on classifier uncertainty.

4Diffusion bridges and inverse problems: the score in action

Diffusion bridges pin the endpoint at the target domain (not noise), enabling restoration and inverse problems. The score field (the direction that log-probability rises or falls) guides transport: ∇log p(x|y) combines the likelihood of observations and the prior (the prior learned as a diffusion score). By tuning noise profiles to match input and training trends, and adding energy-based refinement near the endpoint, bridges achieve sharp restoration and constraint-respecting paths without slow random-walk sampling.

The through-line. The whole field is one push to pull apart two things standard models glue together: choosing a distribution shape and normalizing it. Any scalar function of x can shape a distribution — low output = high density — but normalizing means summing that function over all of space, impossible for anything real. The key insight: you almost never need that sum. Sampling only needs the slope of log p (the sum vanishes), training can be rewritten so the sum cancels via math tricks, and composing distributions becomes simple addition. The modern payoff is diffusion models — they are score networks trained at every noise level — they get energy models' flexibility and solve the mixing problem by working coarse to fine. The key math tricks reappear throughout: gradually reshape a simple distribution into data, gradients of black-box quantities, and the trap of models that learn from themselves. Energy models did not lose to diffusion — they became it.

Energy-based & score models — why it works, mathematically

Any function you like is a valid density — as long as you never need to sum it over all of space. The gradient of log-probability dodges that sum entirely, and everything else follows.

The problem, as a quantity

The dream is maximally flexible density estimation: assign any scalar score to any point x, call high-score high-probability, and learn whatever shape the data has — multimodal, non-convex, compositional. The problem is that to turn scores into a real probability, you must divide by their total: p(x) = exp(−E(x)) / Z, where Z = ∫ exp(−E(x)) dx sums over every point in space. That integral is intractable for any interesting model. Without Z you cannot compute the likelihood, so maximum-likelihood training is blocked. And without samples from p(x) you cannot even write the training gradient. The real obstacle is a single number — Z — that is mathematically necessary but physically uncomputable. Everything in this family is a different route around that one impossibility.

The core ideas — and why they work

The gradient kills Z (the score function)

s(x) = ∇ₓ log p(x) = −∇ₓ E(x) [Z drops out: ∂/∂x log(1/Z) = 0]
The partition function Z is a constant — it does not depend on x. So when you differentiate log p(x) = −E(x) − log Z with respect to x, the log Z term vanishes: ∇ₓ log p = −∇ₓ E(x). This is not an approximation or a trick — it is an exact identity that falls out of calculus. The score s(x) encodes everything you need to know about where probability is going up or down, and it is computable from any energy function without ever touching the intractable sum. The deeper reason this matters: probability is not a quantity you perceive directly — you only ever experience relative differences between nearby points, and the score is precisely those differences. Same primitive elsewhere: this is the gradient of an expectation of a black box — the same score-function trick that lets reinforcement learning differentiate through a stochastic reward and that lets adversarial attacks climb a loss surface through a non-differentiable classifier.

Sampling by noisy gradient descent (Langevin dynamics)

x_{t+1} = x_t − (ε/2)·∇ₓ E(x_t) + √ε·z, z ~ N(0,I) [stationary dist. ∝ exp(−E(x))]
If you remove the noise, this is gradient descent: it finds a mode of p(x) and stays there. If you remove the gradient, it is a random walk: it explores space but ignores the energy landscape. Together, the gradient term pulls the chain toward high-probability regions while the injected Gaussian noise √ε·z keeps it from collapsing to a single point. The stationary distribution of this stochastic process is exactly p(x) ∝ exp(−E(x)) — provably, under mild conditions on step size ε. You never need to know Z because the chain never computes it; it only evaluates ∇ₓ E at the current point. The catch is honest: mixing is slow. To cross a low-probability valley between two modes, the noise must be large enough to overcome the gradient pulling back — and large noise means slow, diffuse exploration. This mixing tax is what made classical EBMs brittle in high dimensions, and it is exactly the problem the score-diffusion connection was built to solve. Same primitive elsewhere: this is Reshape a Gaussian into your data — the same transport idea that drives diffusion models, flow-matching, and normalizing-flows, all of which move noise to data along some gradient-guided path.

Training without Z: contrastive divergence

∇θ L = E_data[∇θ E_θ(x)] − E_model[∇θ E_θ(x)] [raise energy on fantasy samples, lower on real ones]
The log-likelihood gradient is −E_data[∇θ E] + E_model[∇θ E]: lower the energy of real data points, raise it on samples from the model. This is intuitive — the landscape should have wells where the data lives and hills everywhere else. But the second term requires samples from p_θ(x), which needs a Markov chain (MCMC), which is slow. Contrastive divergence shortcuts this by running the chain for only a few steps starting from real data rather than running it to convergence. This is an approximation, and it introduces a real bias. More importantly, it exposes the standing failure mode of all contrastive training: if the chain never escapes the data's neighbourhood, the model never learns to assign high energy to the other modes it has not seen — so the energy landscape around unseen points is unconstrained and sampling from scratch fails. The enemy it defeats — and sometimes doesn't: this is the degeneracy trap. The same collapse dynamic that breaks GAN discriminators, VQ codebooks, and MoE routing appears here: any objective that uses your own model's outputs as a negative signal risks a trivial solution where the model only ever sees itself, producing a landscape that looks good locally but is meaningless globally.

Score matching: learn the gradient directly

J(θ) = E_data[½·‖s_θ(x)‖² + tr(∇ₓ s_θ(x))] [integration by parts removes the need for p_data]
Instead of training an energy and hoping its gradient is a good score, learn the score function s_θ(x) ≈ ∇ₓ log p(x) directly. The naive loss would be E_data[‖s_θ(x) − ∇ₓ log p_data(x)‖²], which requires knowing p_data — circular. Integration by parts rewrites the expectation so that the unknown density cancels: the loss reduces to a trace of the Jacobian of s_θ plus a squared-norm term, both computable from samples alone with no partition function and no density. The Jacobian trace is expensive in high dimensions (it costs d backward passes), so in practice one perturbs x with Gaussian noise and regresses the score of the noisy density — denoising score matching. At noise level σ, the target score is simply (x̃ − x)/σ²: the direction back toward the clean point. Same primitive elsewhere: training a score network at many noise levels σ₁ > σ₂ > … > σ_T and chaining the resulting Langevin steps is exactly a diffusion model. The score IS what a diffusion model learns — ε_θ(x_t, t) ≈ −σ_t · ∇ₓ log p(x_t). Energy-based models and diffusion models are the same idea at different noise scales, and diffusion's stability comes from doing score matching at every noise level rather than only at zero.

Composition is free (product of energies)

p₁(x) ∝ exp(−E₁(x)), p₂(x) ∝ exp(−E₂(x)) ⟹ p₁(x)·p₂(x) ∝ exp(−E₁(x) − E₂(x))
Because probability under an energy model is an exponential, the product of two distributions is just the sum of their energies. You can train a content energy and a style energy separately, then sample from their product by running Langevin on E₁ + E₂ — no retraining, no joint model, no blending coefficients to tune. This is structurally impossible with any model that normalises explicitly (flows, VAEs, autoregressive) because the product of two normalised densities is not normalised and computing its new constant requires yet another intractable integral. The unnormalised energy representation turns composition from a hard problem into a sum. The real-world use is classifier-guided or reward-guided generation: add a classifier's log-probability as an energy term and Langevin drifts toward high-probability, high-reward regions. Diffusion guidance is this idea, applied at each denoising step. Same primitive elsewhere: summing energies is the same factorize the joint by the chain rule move that autoregressive models and world-models use — except here the factorization is multiplicative in probability space (additive in energy space) rather than sequential.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Classical contrastive training directly minimises the KL divergence to data by pushing energy down on real points and up on short-chain MCMC samples. It works in low dimensions but suffers the mixing tax: if the negative samples never leave the data neighbourhood, the landscape is only well-shaped there. The result is a model that scores real data correctly but generates badly from random initialisation.

Score matching and its denoising variant sidestep MCMC during training entirely — you regress the gradient of the log-density using only data and their noisy corruptions. Training is stable, scalable, and free of partition-function complications. The cost is that you only learn a function; to sample, you still need to run a gradient-guided chain (Langevin or an ODE integrator).

The diffusion connection resolves the mixing problem by learning the score at every noise level. At high noise, the distribution is nearly Gaussian and trivially mixable; at low noise, it is close to the data. Sampling goes from high to low noise — coarse to fine — which is equivalent to running an annealed Langevin chain without ever getting stuck. The 'denoising network' in a diffusion model is precisely a score network: ε_θ(x_t,t) / σ_t ≈ −∇ₓ log p_t(x_t).

Energy for anomaly detection and guidance exploits the fact that high energy = low probability by design. A model trained on clean data assigns low energy there; out-of-distribution points receive high energy by default (no special negative training needed). For guidance, you add any differentiable criterion — a classifier, a physics constraint, a human preference model — as an additive energy term and steer sampling for free.

A worked numeric example

If two states have energies E=[1,3], their unnormalized probabilities are [e^{-1},e^{-3}], so the lower-energy state is about 7.4× more likely. Langevin sampling follows -∇E downhill but adds noise so it can still explore.

The through-line. The whole field of energy-based and score models is a sustained effort to separate two things that standard probabilistic models entangle: expressing a distribution and normalizing it. Any scalar function of x can express a distribution — low output = high density — but computing the normalizer Z means integrating that function over all of space, which is intractable for interesting functions. The insight is that you almost never actually need Z: sampling only needs the gradient of log p (which kills Z), training can be rewritten to cancel Z via integration by parts (score matching) or by comparing real and fantasy points (contrastive divergence), and composition becomes free because log of a product is a sum of energies. The modern payoff is diffusion models, which are score networks trained at every noise scale — they inherit the flexibility of energy models and solve the mixing problem by working coarse-to-fine. The recurring mathematical moves are all here: Reshape a Gaussian into your data (Langevin transport), the gradient of an expectation of a black box (score function), and the degeneracy trap (contrastive collapse). Energy models did not lose to diffusion models — they became them.

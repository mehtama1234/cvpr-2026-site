Maximum likelihood & cross-entropy — why it works, mathematically

You have data—numbers, labels, patterns—and a tunable model with millions of dials. You want the dials set so your model would have predicted your data as naturally as possible. This is the core machine that finds those dials.

The problem, as a quantity

You have a bag of observations: a coin landed heads 7 times and tails 3 times, or a person clicked an ad or didn't, or a photo showed a cat or didn't. These are the facts. You also have a model—a guessing machine with tunable settings—that assigns a score to how likely each outcome should be under those settings. The problem is: which settings would make the facts you actually saw look least surprising? If you turned the dial one way, the model might say "heads should happen 60% of the time" and your 7 heads out of 10 looks pretty normal. If you turned it another way, the model says "heads should happen 1% of the time" and your 7 heads look almost impossible. You want the dial where the data looks most natural—where the model would say "yes, I would have expected exactly that."

The challenge is that "natural" is slippery. Some settings make one data point natural but others unlikely. And with millions of dials (like in a neural network), checking each combination is hopeless. You need a single, clean rule that points to the best settings without checking them all.

The core ideas — and why they work

Pick the settings that make the data you saw most expected

θ* = argmax_θ Σ_i log p(x_i; θ)
When your model has tunable settings (call them θ), it can give a score for how expected each data point is—call it p(x_i; θ), the expectedness score for point x_i under settings θ. To find the best settings, you ask: which θ makes all your data points look expected at once? The answer is sneaky: add up the logs of all the expectedness scores and pick the settings that make that sum as big as possible. Why logs? Because when you multiply many small numbers (each data point's odds), you get numerical chaos—you get zero or infinity or meaningless overflow. Taking the log of each score before summing turns that multiplication into harmless addition, and the best settings stay the same. It is the same as asking "which settings make the data least surprising overall?"—just in a form that actually works on a computer.

The equation says: settings θ-star equal the choice of θ that makes the sum (over all data points i) of the log of how expected that point is, as large as possible. You are not trying to fit each point perfectly; you are trying to find settings where, on average, the model would naturally produce your data.

Making the data expected is the same as minimizing surprise

−Σ_i log p(x_i) is the cross-entropy — the same objective, flipped
Instead of thinking about "maximizing expectedness," flip it backwards: each data point has a natural cost, which is the negative log of its expectedness score—call it −log p(x_i). This is the surprise. The cross-entropy is the average surprise across all your data points: the average cost. Maximizing the original expectedness sum is exactly the same as minimizing this cost sum—they point to the same best settings. It is just a different lens on the same target.

Why does this matter? Because in practice, you build optimizers (like stepping downhill) that shrink costs, not maximize sums. Flipping the problem into a minimization frame makes it natural to plug into all the standard training machinery. The settings that give you the most expected data are exactly the settings that give you the smallest average surprise—and that reframing lets you use decades of existing tools.

It's really shrinking the gap to the true distribution

max likelihood ⇔ min gap between data and model (KL)
The real action underneath is this: you have the true odds—the actual, unknown chances of what nature does. Your model with tunable settings is trying to get as close as possible to that truth. Maximizing how expected your data points are (under your settings) is equivalent to shrinking the gap between your model's pretend odds and the true odds. That gap is measured by something called KL divergence—a pure geometry metric of how different two distributions are.

Why is this true? Because maximizing likelihood (making your observed data look natural) is the same as minimizing how surprised you would be if you sampled fresh data from the true odds and tried to score it with your model. That surprise—the gap—shrinks exactly when you pick settings that match the true odds as closely as possible. You are not trying to memorize; you are trying to match reality.

Add a prior belief and you get regularization for free

θ* = argmax_θ log p(data|θ) + log p(θ)
When you have a prior belief about what the settings should be (not too extreme, in a certain range, favoring simplicity), you can fold that belief directly into the search for the best settings. The equation says: pick settings θ-star that maximize two things at once—the log of how expected the data is under those settings, plus the log of how much you believed in those settings a priori. Adding these two logs together is the same as multiplying their un-logged odds, so settings that are both natural for the data and aligned with your prior belief get rewarded. Settings that fit the data perfectly but violate your prior get punished.

This is regularization born for free. You never have to add a separate penalty term or invent a punishment weight; the prior belief is already in the equation. A weak prior (almost uniform over all settings) lets the data dominate. A strong prior (sharp belief around a narrow range) acts like a strong brake on the settings, keeping them simple. It is the same machine—you just turned one dial to change how much the prior whispers into the answer.

The approaches, as variations on the math

Different models and data will turn different dials: a Bernoulli dial for coin flips, a Gaussian dial for measurements around a center, a categorical dial for picking one of many classes. A prior-strength dial changes how much you trust your prior belief versus the raw data. A number of data points dial shifts the balance—more data silences the prior, fewer data lets prior beliefs win. The thread never changes: find settings where the sum of log-scores (or equivalently, minimize average surprise) and trust that you are carving down the gap to the true odds.

Every approach, and the lever it pulls

One notch deeper: each of the theme's 4 approach-families and the specific mathematical mechanism that makes its papers work — the exact knob turned on the core relations above. Under each, the specific mathematical note for every paper in that family.

1Empirical vs. True Distribution
KL(p_data || p_model) = minimize sum over x of p_data(x) * log(p_data(x)/p_model(x))
Your model assigns probabilities p_model to outcomes. The data came from the true spread p_data. The KL term measures how far your model's spread is from what actually happened. Making this small means your model learns the real pattern, not just fitting random noise. This is the mathematical reason that minimizing cross-entropy shrinks the gap to the true spread.
2Class Imbalance & Weighted Loss
Loss = sum of w_c * (-y_c * log(p_c)) per class c, where w_c is the weight for class c
When rare events matter more than common ones (like disease detection), you multiply the cross-entropy penalty by higher weights for the rare classes. The weight w_c for each class c determines how much that class contributes to the total loss. This forces the model to get the scarce cases right instead of just predicting the common case all the time.
3Confidence Calibration (Label Smoothing)
y_smooth = (1 - alpha) * y_hard + alpha * (1/K), where alpha ∈ [0,1] and K is the number of classes
Hard targets (exactly 0 or 1) push the model to be overconfident. Label smoothing y_smooth blends the true label y_hard with a uniform spread across all K classes, controlled by smoothing strength alpha. This prevents the model from learning extreme certainty and makes it more honest about what it doesn't know. The result is better performance on new data and more realistic confidence scores.
4Regularization Through Priors (Weight Decay)
Loss_total = Loss_data + lambda * sum(w^2), where lambda is the prior strength
The four core ideas mention that adding a prior belief gives regularization. Weight decay does this by penalizing large weights with strength lambda, expressing a prior belief that weights should stay small. This acts like saying 'I believe the true pattern is simple' before looking at the data. Without it, the model can memorize noise; with it, you get a smoother, simpler solution that works better on held-out examples.

The through-line. The core problem is finding tunable settings that are most faithful to your observations—settings where the data would have happened naturally, without a shocked face from the model. Checking all settings is impossible; you need a clean target to aim for. Two plain ideas carry it: the log of the expectedness score is additive and stable on computers, so maximizing it sidesteps numerical chaos and unifies all data points into one sum, and that same sum is exactly measuring the shrinking gap between your model and the true underlying odds—so you are always chasing reality.

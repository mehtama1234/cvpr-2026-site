The problem — from first principles

In pathology, radiology, microscopy, and Earth observation, images are measurements, not pictures. A CT scan is a 3D grid of tissue density. A microscope slide is millions of cell boundaries and stains. Satellite imagery is spectral signatures layered in space and time. Unlike photos where pixels represent reflected light, here pixels encode medical or scientific data—and that data is expensive, fragile, and high-dimensional. The core challenge: how do you train a computer to reason over such images when annotations are sparse and costly, when modalities shift across scanners and labs, and when a single pixel error can propagate into a diagnostic mistake?

Computer vision's original goal was to turn pixels into semantic meaning. In medical and scientific imaging, that inversion becomes equally critical: you must turn meaning—knowledge about anatomy, disease, physics—into pixels, or pixels into quantitative predictions (gene expression, lesion location, vessel connectivity). The field is learning that generic vision models, trained on natural photos, fail spectacularly on tissue and instrument data. Medical images have different distributions, different failure modes, and higher stakes for mistakes.

This cluster attacks that core problem from eight directions: building modality-aware encoders that understand that MRI != CT != ultrasound; learning from weak supervision because pixel-level annotations don't exist; grounding predictions in anatomical geometry so hallucinations fail the geometry check; reasoning over sequences and hierarchies (scan→region→cell) instead of flat images; and scaling to whole slides, volumetric datasets, and real-time inference on gigapixel images. Each direction offers a different lever on the same fundamental tension: rich, high-dimensional data with sparse human guidance, where errors have real consequences.

The families — how the field tackles it

Modality-aware encoders: learning what's invariant across scanners

Medical imaging systems vary wildly: a Philips MRI scanner produces different signal distributions than a Siemens, even on the same patient. Mammography vendors embed energy metadata that affects the image. Ultrasound has different physics than radiography. This family treats modality shift not as a nuisance to ignore but as a signal to exploit. LUMINA introduces explicit energy metadata for mammography; RNED encodes medical numbers (lab values, vital signs) using specialized rotary representations. OmniFM treats federated medical data as inherently heterogeneous—learning priors that stay invariant across sites without centralizing patient data. Tell2Adapt and CoFiDA-M distill concept-guided reasoning into lightweight students that work across anatomy and modality. TANGO learns distribution-wise consistency from vision foundation models to make segmentation robust to domain shift. Keep It Frozen enables a single frozen transformer to adapt per-sample to both medical and natural images via domain-routed residual modulation. These papers recognize that forcing all modalities into one feature space is naive; instead, they learn what's preserved across shifts and what's specific to each instrument.

Weak supervision at scale: learning from imperfect labels

Annotating a whole-slide image (a 100,000 × 100,000 pixel pathology slide) at pixel level is impossible. Neither is labeling every frame in a surgical video. This family makes models work with bounding boxes, scribbles, image-level labels, or synthetic annotations. Rethinking Box Supervision inverts the supervision pipeline—converting sparse boxes into segmentation masks without bias. BackSplit shows that structured background supervision (not just "background" but "adipose vs. stroma vs. artifact") reduces false positives through information-theoretic gains. SD-FSMIS shifts from task-specific architectures to leveraging diffusion model priors for few-shot segmentation. FBTA enables end-to-end training on gigapixel slides on a single GPU via feature bridging and multi-view bags. MUSE combines sample-wise adaptation with retrieval-augmented generation to handle few-shot whole-slide classification. Focus on Background uses SAM's background prompting for medical images. AG-VAS enables large multimodal models to do pixel-level anomaly segmentation with learnable semantic anchors. These methods share a philosophy: human annotation is precious, so extract every signal from partial labels and use generative or foundation models as cheap priors.

Vision-language grounding: binding predictions to anatomy

A segmentation model might correctly identify a lesion in pixels but hallucinate its location when asked "where is it?" A report generator might describe findings that don't appear in the image. This family anchors multimodal predictions—text, segmentation, location—to geometric ground truth. EMAD grounds Alzheimer's disease diagnosis to 3D brain anatomy, localizing claims to actual structures. PETAR uses mask-aware focal prompting to localize PET lesions at 3D voxel level. BiOTPrompt explicitly detects emerging and disappearing abnormalities through optimal transport, enabling fine-grained temporal reasoning. CG-Reasoner grounds textual reasoning in spatial geometry via centroid prediction, with a novel consistency metric. Phrase-grounded APO uses phrase-level spatial localization to align chest X-ray reports. MR-RAG injects multimodal relevance signals at both retrieval and generation stages. CURE adapts curriculum learning to dynamically adjust sampling by anatomical category, preventing common anatomy from overwhelming rare findings. MEDIC-AD adds anomaly-aware and difference tokens to VLMs for clinical lesion sensitivity. These papers treat anatomy not as a downstream evaluation but as an integrated constraint: if your model says "mass in upper lobe," it should point to the upper lobe.

3D and volumetric reasoning: slices to structure

A CT scan is a 3D volume of 512 slices, each 512×512. A pathology WSI is 2D but effectively 3D if you include magnification levels. Neuron reconstruction requires 3D topology. This family learns to reason across slices and scales instead of treating each plane independently. NeuroSeg Meets DINOv3 inflates 2D self-supervised priors to 3D with topology-aware skeleton loss for morphological fidelity. VesMamba uses Mamba to capture long-range topological dependencies in vessel segmentation. Volumetric Functional Maps extends functional maps (used for surface correspondence) to volumetric data via Laplacian eigenbases. EchoPOSE infers 6D pose of 2D ultrasound slices from image content alone, enabling accurate 3D left-ventricular reconstruction. GaussianPile models finite focal-zone PSFs for slice-based volumetric imaging via Gaussian splatting. Prospective Dynamic 3D MRI leverages nonlinear manifold-based deformation with geometry-aware tri-plane mapping for ultra-sparse MRI. Spatial-SAM uses 3D SDF-based memory for spatially consistent electron microscopy segmentation. Virtual Full-stack Scanning reformulates any-to-any MRI modality imputation into region-level code prediction. These methods treat 3D structure as a hard constraint, not an afterthought: connectivity, topology, and geometry inform the entire learning process.

Generative priors and synthesis: learning the manifold

Instead of training discriminative models on scarce annotations, this family learns what the natural image manifold looks like—then uses that learned structure to segment, register, reconstruct, or generate. From Panel to Pixel automatically extracts hierarchical supervision from scientific literature's pedagogical structure, building VLM priors without manual annotation. MicroFM applies physics-guided flow matching for microscopy super-resolution. PnP-CM reinterprets consistency models as proximal operators within plug-and-play ADMM solvers. cryoSENSE combines sparse handcrafted priors with learned generative diffusion priors for high-throughput cryo-EM. Anatomica enables localized geometric and topological control over anatomical diffusion models via differentiable parsing. Sketch2CT uses two-stage latent diffusion with text-sketch fusion for 3D medical volume generation. Diffusion with a Linguistic Compass synthesizes clinically plausible future sMRI via LLM guidance. SemiGDA addresses overfitting under limited annotations through a generative paradigm with dual-distribution alignment. GeneVAR reformulates gene-to-WSI synthesis as coarse-to-fine generation with causal mean flow. These papers flip the script: instead of asking "how do I label every pixel?", they ask "what's the generative process?" and use that to constrain or bootstrap discriminative tasks.

Multi-task and multi-modal learning: reasoning across domains

Medical decisions aren't made from one image. A pathologist sees H&E stain, IHC stains, genomics, and clinical history. A radiologist integrates current and prior imaging. A surgical video contains frames, tool trajectories, and anatomical structures. This family learns to fuse heterogeneous signals and reason jointly. MedGRPO introduces MedVidBench (the first large-scale medical video instruction dataset) with an RL framework using cross-dataset reward normalization. SurgCoT applies chain-of-thought reasoning to surgical videos for step-by-step procedural understanding. OralGPT-Omni builds a dental MLLM with five-step TRACE-CoT reasoning and four-stage training from domain injection through GRPO. MLLM-HWSI enables the first MLLM that jointly reasons across four WSI scales (cell/patch/region/slide). Gastric-X captures the full clinical workflow—imaging, labs, narrative reports—in a multimodal benchmark. H2-Surv applies hyperbolic geometry to capture both intra-modality hierarchies and cross-modal relationships in survival prediction. MDCS-MoAME uses multi-directional composite scanning and dynamic expert selection for modeling complex inter-modal correlations. Uni-Encoder Meets Multi-Encoders decouples representation learning from segmentation for brain tumors with missing modalities, using masked self-supervision for cross-modal complementarity. These methods treat multi-modality as the rule, not an edge case: they learn to exploit complementarity, handle missing data gracefully, and fuse at the right semantic level.

Continual and adaptive learning: handling distribution shifts

Medical datasets are never static. New scanners arrive. Hospital protocols change. Datasets expand. This family learns to adapt online without catastrophic forgetting and without retraining from scratch. DK-DDIL is the first rehearsal-free dynamic domain-incremental learning framework for medical imaging, jointly handling evolving label spaces and heterogeneous domain shifts. Beyond the Static-World addresses both spatial modality conflict and temporal catastrophic forgetting in lifelong medical image restoration. SPEGC uses differentiable graph clustering with decoupled semantic prompts for continual test-time adaptation to sequential domain shifts. Forging a Dynamic Memory integrates retrieval-augmented generation into medical continual learning with dynamic reference dataset construction. Sparse Task Vector Mixup enables efficient cross-cancer knowledge transfer via sparse task vector aggregation. Personalized Longitudinal Medical Report Generation treats temporal evolution as a first-class component with demographic-driven personalization. InvCoSS enables continual self-supervised learning without data replay, preserving privacy. Unlocking Positive Transfer in Incrementally Learning Surgical Instruments systematically enables forward and backward knowledge transfer in class-incremental segmentation. These papers recognize that medical AI systems live in the real world, where distribution shift is constant; they build systems that improve over time.

Uncertainty and robustness: knowing when to trust predictions

A diagnosis is useless if you don't know how confident it should be. Robustness means a model works reliably even when data comes from a different lab, staining protocol, or disease prevalence. This family quantifies uncertainty and builds systems that degrade gracefully. Delving Aleatoric Uncertainty quantifies data uncertainty using feature vector singular value energy from foundation models. LATA uses deterministic, label-free transductive refinement with graph Laplacian regularization to preserve split conformal guarantees. Hyperbolic Relational Prompts addresses intersectional bias in medical VLMs through hyperbolic geometry and relational graphs. FairLLaVA minimizes demographic mutual information through alternating DAC-based variational minimization. TopoCL integrates persistent homology-based topological features into contrastive self-supervised learning. Spectral Conformal Risk Control combines spectral risk measures with Bayesian-quadrature conformal prediction for distribution-free tail-risk control. MedCLIPSeg uses probabilistic cross-modal attention in CLIP with variational Key-Value formulation. The Invisible Gorilla Effect in Out-of-distribution Detection reveals that artifact-ROI color similarity determines OOD performance, a critical finding for deployment. HierUQ integrates hierarchical uncertainty quantification with adaptive granularity reconciliation. These methods treat uncertainty not as a post-hoc metric but as a design principle: build models that quantify what they don't know.

Remote sensing and microscopy: scaling vision to the macro and micro

Remote sensing and microscopy share a problem: massive spatial extent or density at high resolution. A satellite image might be 10,000 × 10,000 pixels across multiple spectral bands. An electron microscopy volume is terabytes of slices. This family tackles scale-aware processing, modality fusion, and domain generalization. NeighborMAE models spatial dependencies between neighboring Earth Observation images in masked autoencoders. F2Net uses frequency-aware decomposition for ultra-high resolution remote sensing segmentation. UniGeoSeg combines referring, interactive, and reasoning segmentation in a million-scale instruction-driven dataset. ReAttnCLIP decomposes CLIP's attention for remote sensing segmentation without training. CF-IPT uses cross-modal fusion within prompt learning for multi-source remote sensing. RECS4R uses language-conditioned contours as a unified geometric intermediate for grounding and segmentation. SkySense-VITA enables universal in-context segmentation across multi-modal remote sensing with cross-modal and cross-geography generalization. Statistical Characteristic-Guided Denoising adaptively guides denoising for rapid high-resolution TEM imaging. VEMamba applies axial-lateral cross-SSM scanning tailored to volumetric EM anisotropy. These papers solve a shared constraint: global context is needed, but you can't fit the entire image in memory. They learn hierarchical, adaptive, or iterative approaches that respect that physical reality.

Where it stands

The field has cracked several core problems. Foundation models—DINOv2, SAM, CLIP—transfer surprisingly well to medical images when properly adapted, reducing reliance on massive labeled datasets. Vision-language models can now ground predictions in anatomy and reason about sequences (before/after, past/present). Weak supervision techniques make it viable to scale annotations: bounding boxes, scribbles, and image-level labels produce competitive segmentation. Generative models (diffusion, flow matching) can synthesize realistic training data and learn manifold structure, reducing overfitting on scarce annotations.

What remains hard: deployment in real hospitals, where scanners change, disease prevalence shifts, and integration with existing workflows matters as much as accuracy. Modality gaps persist—optical SAR ship tracking, multi-vendor mammography, and cross-stain pathology still pose challenges. Scaling to true gigapixel or volumetric reasoning remains memory-intensive and slow. Most critically, the field is still learning how to encode domain knowledge (physics, anatomy, causality, clinical reasoning) into models without hand-engineering feature engineering. Recent work on causality, symbolic grounding, and LLM-guided refinement suggests that next generation will integrate deep learning with principled reasoning—not as parallel systems but as unified architectures where geometry and causality constrain learned features from the start.

Weak supervision at scale: learning from imperfect labels

The everyday problem: Dense medical labels are expensive because experts must mark lesions, organs, or tissue regions carefully. In practice, the available supervision may be a box, point, report, weak tag, or only a few examples, and those hints do not directly specify the true boundary.

The hidden mathematical idea: The mathematical idea is constraint completion. Use weak labels as partial constraints, then infer the missing mask, lesion extent, or slide-level evidence by combining shape, background, semantics, and consistency across examples.

Why it matters: This matters because medical AI cannot wait for perfect labels at scale. Weak supervision is useful only if the model understands what the label leaves unspecified instead of treating a cheap hint as full truth.

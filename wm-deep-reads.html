1 · Driving world models: predicting the road ahead

A driving world model learns to simulate what comes next on the road: given the current view and your action (steer left, accelerate), the model predicts the future scene. The core promise is escape: instead of rolling out plans in the real world and crashing, you roll them out inside the learned model first, debug your driving strategy in imagination, and act only when confident. But imagination compounds errors—each small mistake in frame N feeds into frame N+1, and by second three you may be hallucinating entirely. These four papers show different ways to ground predictions in geometry (3D Gaussians, point clouds, occupancy grids) to fight drift and keep the dream from drifting too far from reality.
CVPR'26GaussianDWM: 3D Gaussian Driving World Model for Unified Scene Understanding and Multi-Modal Generation
Problem
Driving systems need both foresight (what will happen) and reasoning (why it will happen). But most world models learn only features—low-level patterns—and cannot explain or reason about the scene geometry, objects, or language-based queries about the road ahead.
Approach
Embed language directly into 3D Gaussian primitives so that each geometric blob carries semantic meaning. When you ask about the scene, use language to pick which Gaussians matter most, then generate the future image, depth map, and text description together by conditioning on both the language question and the image so far.
Why it matters
By tying language to geometry explicitly, the model learns a simulator that understands, not just paints. You can ask it why the car ahead is braking, and it can explain by reasoning over spatial structure—moving toward a learned simulator that acts like a reasoning engine, not a pixel factory.

CVPR'26MAD: Motion Appearance Decoupling for Efficient Driving World Models
Problem
Driving world models must track two kinds of change: objects moving (a car turning left) and surfaces changing appearance (shadows passing, lighting shifting). Bundling them together wastes computation and confuses the model about what is real motion versus what is just the world looking different.
Approach
Split the prediction task: one branch learns how things move (occupancy evolution, trajectories), another learns how they look (color, texture). Predict motion separately from appearance, then combine them to generate the future frame. This separation lets each branch focus and share computation efficiently.
Why it matters
A learned simulator that knows the difference between where things are going and how they look is more efficient and more truthful—it separates the physics from the rendering. Real-time autonomous driving lives on tight latency budgets, and this factorization makes the imagination fast enough to matter.

CVPR'26GEM: Generating LiDAR World Model via Deformable Mamba
Problem
LiDAR gives you a point cloud—raw 3D hits that are disordered and noisy. Unlike images, there is no natural grid to predict; unlike occupancy grids, you lose distance detail. It is hard to tell which points belong to moving cars and which to static road structure, so the model cannot separate dynamic futures from the fixed map.
Approach
Tokenize the point cloud using a custom architecture inspired by Mamba (a fast scanning model), then automatically separate dynamic points (cars, pedestrians) from static background. Roll forward token-by-token with adaptive gating so the model attends selectively to what matters for the next step. Support controllable generation so you can say what if that car turned and the model obeys your counterfactual.
Why it matters
A LiDAR world model that understands disentanglement lets you dream in 3D point space with true what-if reasoning. You can ask the simulator to rewind and replay the scene under different actions—a learned engine that separates your agency (the control signal) from the world's response (what physically follows).

CVPR'26GenieDrive: Towards Physics-Aware Driving World Model with 4D Occupancy Guided Video Generation
Problem
Video diffusion models generate future frames by learning pixels directly: given an action, paint the next image. But they are black boxes that ignore physics, so they generate cars moving through each other, or favor unrealistic straight motion because most driving is straight. Without explicit geometry, the dream drifts.
Approach
Split the task into two stages: first, a lightweight physics module predicts the 4D occupancy grid (where things are in space over time) using a tri-plane VAE and a control-aware transformer; then, a video model renders that occupancy into viewable frames and refines multi-view consistency using specialized attention. The occupancy acts as a physics guardrail so the rendering stays truthful.
Why it matters
By predicting occupied space before rendering pixels, you build a simulator that reasons about physics explicitly and separately from appearance. The occupancy grid is the learned world model; the video is just how you visualize it. This separation keeps the dream geometrically grounded—no ghost cars, no impossible physics.

2 · Video as a controllable simulator: steering a generative model with physics and goals

World models learn to simulate environments by predicting future observations from current ones and actions. This theme explores four paths to turn video generation into a steerable simulator: pose-aligned memory for 3D consistency under aggressive camera motion, coupling physics engines in-the-loop to enforce conservation laws, conditioning on force fields to enable goal-directed reasoning, and using latent world models as reward signals to guide inference-time physics alignment. Each method attacks the central tension: diffusion models excel at visual realism but break physics; simulators enforce physics but lack diversity. Together they show that a generative video model is a learned simulator—the work lies in aligning it with the physical world.

3 · Taming the drift: keeping long imagined rollouts on track

World models aspire to simulate environments forward—predicting what comes next given a state and action. But they face an insidious trap: the longer you imagine ahead by feeding predictions back as inputs, error compounds and the imagined trajectory wanders into hallucination. This drift is the central failure mode of world modeling, where small prediction mistakes accumulate into complete loss of realism. The papers in this collection tackle drift through four distinct lenses: reversed-trajectory self-correction, conservation-law grounding via Hamiltonian mechanics, disentangled latent motion chains, and dual semantic-geometric world models for structured 3D understanding. Each represents a different answer to the same hard question: how do you keep imagination honest over many steps?
CVPR'26BAgger: Backwards Aggregation for Mitigating Drift in Autoregressive Video Diffusion Models
Problem
Autoregressive video diffusion models suffer from exposure bias: as generated frames accumulate errors and become context for future predictions, quality degrades and the video drifts further from reality. The compounding error makes long-horizon generation unreliable for simulation tasks.
Approach
BAgger leverages a key insight: time-reversed videos remain realistic when prompts describe reversed motion. The model generates its own reversed rollouts, then uses these as corrective training signals to fine-tune on self-generated trajectories via standard diffusion objectives—no special losses or teacher models needed.
Why it matters
By learning from reversed simulations, the model encounters its own error modes but from the perspective of moving backward, breaking the exposure bias feedback loop. This grounds imagination in plausible dynamics rather than letting error compound monotonically forward.

CVPR'26DreamSAC: Learning Hamiltonian World Models via Symmetry Exploration
Problem
Learned world models interpolate well on familiar dynamics but fail when extrapolating to novel physical properties (e.g., unseen friction or gravity). They learn statistical correlations rather than the conservation laws and physical invariances that govern real systems.
Approach
DreamSAC uses Hamiltonian mechanics—where conservation laws (energy, momentum) are built into the representation—as the backbone for the world model. Symmetry-aware curiosity exploration probes for these invariants during data collection, and contrastive learning trains the model to recognize invariant physical state from pixels.
Why it matters
By anchoring imagination in conserved quantities rather than learned surface patterns, the model extrapolates correctly to novel parameters. Drift is tamed not by correction but by grounding in the physics itself—if a Hamiltonian holds, error cannot compound in the wrong direction.

4 · Thinking small: compact and tokenized latent world models

Planning in a simulated environment is far cheaper than trial-and-error in the real world. But simulating pixel-level video is prohibitively slow. This collection charts a new frontier: learning to compress observations into tiny codes—as few as 8 tokens—that retain just enough information for planning and control to work. Four papers show concrete paths: extreme discrete tokenization for real-time robot control, chained video generation and planning in shared latent space for autonomous driving, policy learning via world model distillation, and hierarchical navigation through unified vision-language world models. The common thread: a learned simulator you can run efficiently inside your head.
CVPR'26Planning in 8 Tokens: A Compact Discrete Tokenizer for Latent World Model
Problem
World models remain computationally prohibitive for real-time control because conventional tokenizers encode each observation into hundreds of tokens, making planning slow and resource-intensive. State-of-the-art methods require up to 3 minutes of computation per episode.
Approach
CompACT compresses each observation into as few as 8 tokens using a frozen vision encoder foundation with cross-attention resampling that preserves only planning-critical semantics. A generative decoder synthesizes perceptual details from the compact code without requiring photorealistic reconstruction.
Why it matters
On navigation tasks, 8-token planning achieves comparable accuracy to methods using 784 tokens while delivering 40x speedup. The extreme compression strategy proves that planning-relevant semantics can fit in vastly smaller codes than visual fidelity demands.

5 · Dream to act: world models as a robot's imagination for policy learning

A robot learns to simulate its world: given the current state and a planned action, predict what happens next. This internal model becomes imagination—a safe, fast sandbox where the robot can rehearse behaviors millions of times without touching the real world. World models unlock robot learning at scale: train on recorded data, then dream futures to refine policies, adapt to new situations, or generalize across tasks and embodiments. The core insight across this work is that prediction and control can be separated: let the world model absorb vast amounts of off-policy experience to build rich environment understanding, then train the control policy on top of that learned representation. On a real robot, dreaming beats learning by trial-and-error alone.
ROBOTICS'25LUMOS: Language-Conditioned Imitation Learning with World Models

LUMOS trains the robot inside a learned internal model of the world rather than in the real environment, so it can practice many more situations safely. It uses language and image goals to label past experience in useful new ways (hindsight relabeling) and rewards the robot for exploring unfamiliar internal states.
Why it matters
Practicing inside the world model generates experience far beyond the original recordings, closing the gap between what the robot has seen and what it will face in reality. Dreaming in imagination lets the robot safely explore and learn from failure without costly real-world mistakes.

6 · Imagine to plan: rolling out futures to choose actions and catch surprises

A world model learns to predict what comes next by simulating an environment—it sees what happens when a robot acts and builds a compact representation of cause and effect. That prediction engine becomes a planner's sandbox: roll out many possible futures, pick the action that leads to the best outcome, and even detect when reality deviates from the model's prediction (a sign of danger or novelty). These four papers show the pattern: world models unify planning, multi-task learning, and safety—anomaly detection, diffusion policies, language-guided adaptation, and curiosity-driven exploration all flow from imagining what should happen and checking when it doesn't.
ROBOTICS'25Imagine-2-Drive: Leveraging High-Fidelity World Models via Multi-Modal Diffusion Policies
Problem
Self-driving models that predict the future one step at a time accumulate prediction errors, and they struggle with driving scenarios that have many equally valid options. When each step compounds mistakes from the previous one, the agent's trajectory diverges from reality.
Approach
Build a high-fidelity world model (DiffDreamer) that generates entire future video sequences at once instead of step-by-step, then train a diffusion policy inside the model to sample diverse, valid driving behaviors. The model predicts all future observations jointly; the policy learns to condition its action sampling on the imagined rollout.
Why it matters
Planning over joint futures sidesteps error accumulation; sampling from many valid futures lets the policy explore which actions are safest and most effective, improving success on real courses by 15–20%. The imagined trajectories become the training ground for robust action selection.

When the robot discovers a novel situation, a symbolic planner crystallizes the discovery into a new action rule and generates a reward signal that trains a low-level policy to execute it reliably.

ROBOTICS'25World Models for Anomaly Detection during Model-Based Reinforcement Learning Inference
Problem
A robot trained in simulation may encounter situations during real deployment that it has never seen before, leading to unsafe or nonsensical behavior with no runtime detection. Out-of-distribution inputs are silent failures.
Approach
Reuse the world model learned during training as a continuous safety monitor: predict what the robot should observe and compare the prediction to what actually happens. When prediction error grows large, it signals that the situation is unfamiliar and a safety stop is triggered.
Why it matters
The world model was built from the same training data as the controller, so its predictions are accurate in familiar regimes and break down in novel ones. Prediction error becomes a reliable anomaly signal: if the world model is wrong, the action policy may be too, making it a grounded way to catch surprises and protect the robot.

ROBOTICS'25LIMT: Language-Informed Multi-Task Visual World Models
Problem
Learning multiple manipulation tasks from camera images is data-hungry because each task is learned mostly in isolation, with little knowledge transfer between related tasks. Repetition dominates; patterns go unshared.
Approach
Build a world model that predicts future observations and next states for multi-task learning. Embed language task descriptions into the model so it can identify and reuse shared patterns across tasks—skills, object interactions, and state transitions that generalize.
Why it matters
Language descriptions encode what tasks have in common (grasp an object, place it at a location, rotate it). The world model imagines futures conditioned on these descriptions and discovers which patterns transfer, so knowledge from one task accelerates learning in another without starting from scratch.

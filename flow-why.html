Optical flow — finding the same point in the next frame

Almost everything that involves time in vision — tracking, video, a robot knowing how it moved, stitching a scene into 3D — rests on one humble operation: take a point in this frame and find where it went in the next. It sounds trivial and it is quietly deep, because a single fact limits what is even possible: you can only find a match where the picture has real 2D structure. This page is the plain story, built on a real run where you can watch that limit appear and measure it.

1 · The problem — where did this pixel go?

Two consecutive frames of a video are almost the same picture, shifted a little by motion. Optical flow is the dense answer to one question asked at every pixel: which way, and how far, did the little patch of world under this pixel move? Get it right and you can follow objects, infer a camera's motion, warp one frame into the next, and lift a moving scene into 3D. The way you answer it is matching: for a small window around a pixel, search the next frame for the spot that looks most like it.

The problem, stated once: for every pixel in one frame, find the same physical point in the next — by matching local appearance — knowing that some places in an image simply do not contain enough structure to be matched.

2 · The catch — you can only match 2D structure

Matching works by finding the shift that best lines up a little window. That only has a unique answer if the window contains variation in two directions. Three cases:

what the window containscan you find the match?
A corner (two edges crossing, a blob, a texture)Yes — it looks different in every direction, so exactly one shift lines it up.
A straight edge (one direction of change)Only halfway — you see motion across the edge, but sliding along it looks identical. This is the aperture problem.
A flat region (no change)No — every shift looks the same; there is nothing to match.

So dense flow is fundamentally underdetermined: reliable only at corners, ambiguous on edges, impossible on blank walls. Every optical-flow method is, at heart, a strategy for filling in the motion of the ambiguous places by borrowing from the confident ones (smoothness, context, learned priors). The run makes the limit concrete.

The pattern is the whole story. At corners — where two edges cross, a real 2D landmark — the match is crisp (0.43 px error). Along a plain edge it is far worse (2.19 px), and on flat texture-less regions it is hopeless (3.49 px) — there is simply nothing to lock onto.

The aperture problem, measured

On an edge, you can only see part of the motion. Look at a straight edge through a small window and slide it: you can tell how far it moved across itself, but not how far it slid along itself — the view is identical either way. We measured exactly this on the edge pixels: the error across the edge is only 0.16 px, but the error along the edge is 0.80 px — the along-edge motion is essentially unrecoverable from local information alone. That gap is the aperture problem.

4 · Why it matters — correspondence became the scaffolding

For years optical flow was a benchmark to win. In 2026 its center of gravity moved: dense correspondence is now the free supervision and control signal under a huge amount of other work — reconstructing dynamic 3D and 4D scenes from ordinary video, animating 3D models, editing and generating video, and driving SLAM. Because you always have consecutive frames, flow is supervision you get for nothing — and following a single point across a whole clip (point tracking) extends it from neighbours to the long range.

5 · The surprise — the match becomes the robot's move

In vision, flow is a motion field you estimate and score against ground truth. In a robot, the very same match is never shown to anyone — it is converted into an action. Track the features and the way they slide reveals how the robot itself moved (visual odometry). Track a point on an object and the gap between where it is and where you want it steers the gripper (visual servoing). Subtract the robot's own motion and what is left is the 3D velocity of the things that might hit it.

Same match, a physical consequence. Vision scores flow by endpoint error on a leaderboard; a robot is judged by whether it went where it meant and did not hit anything — computed onboard, at control rate, on a power budget. A wrong match is not a worse number, it is a wrong move. The in-the-wild page lays out both sides.
